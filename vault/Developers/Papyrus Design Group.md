# Papyrus Design Group

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Papyrus Design Group was an American developer of racing simulations, active from 1987 to 2004. David Kaemmer and Omar Khudari founded it, and it was based in Watertown, Massachusetts. It is best known for its NASCAR and IndyCar racing series and for Grand Prix Legends.[^ref-5][^ref-11] Giant Bomb describes it as "a developer which played a key role in laying the foundations for the racing simulation genre."[^ref-8]

Papyrus made its name before Sierra. Indianapolis 500: The Simulation (1989) was published by Electronic Arts, and IndyCar Racing (1993) and NASCAR Racing (1994) were released through Virgin and Papyrus itself. Sources differ on which publisher handled which title.[^ref-25][^ref-10][^ref-24][^ref-27] Sierra On-Line acquired the studio in late 1995.[^ref-5][^ref-23] Vivendi Universal Games shut Papyrus down in May 2004.[^ref-11][^ref-10]

## History

### Founding and Early Years

Kaemmer and Khudari started the company in a Cambridge kitchen in 1987 with a "war chest" of $2000.[^ref-4] Kaemmer grew up in Indiana and graduated from Oberlin College in 1985 with a degree in mathematics.[^ref-21] He and a friend had flown to California to pitch Electronic Arts a flight simulator. Instead, EA asked whether they could make a game simulating the Indianapolis 500.[^ref-21] When Indianapolis 500: The Simulation appeared in 1989, Motor Sport writes, it "was the first legitimate racing sim to reach the market, and it sparked a revolution."[^ref-21]

IndyCar Racing (1993) was an officially licensed product, published by Virgin rather than EA, according to GameSpot.[^ref-10] MobyGames is more specific: Papyrus Publishing published it in the US, with Virgin distributing, while Virgin Interactive published it in Europe.[^ref-29] Wikipedia's NASCAR Racing article says the 1994 game was published by Virgin for MS-DOS, while its series article lists Papyrus as publisher.[^ref-24][^ref-27] GameSpot says it sold "in excess of 1,000,000 units".[^ref-10] In a 1995 interview, president Omar Khudari told Mac Games that Papyrus had about 80 employees and that its sales the previous year were about $6 million.[^ref-4]

### Sierra Era

Sierra On-Line acquired Papyrus in late 1995. *Next Generation*'s February 1996 issue reported that Sierra had bought "Papyrus Design Group Inc., developers of NASCAR Racing and Indy Car Racing, and subsequently all the assets of flight sim developer Sub Logic."[^ref-5][^ref-23] Khudari left Papyrus soon after the acquisition.[^ref-5] Wikipedia lists IndyCar Racing II (1995) as published by Papyrus, with a later Sierra re-release.[^ref-28] From NASCAR Racing 2 (1996) on, Sierra published the NASCAR series.[^ref-27] IndyCar Racing II sold slowly while the real-life league split, so Sierra had little interest in returning to open-wheel racing. IndyCar Racing II was reissued as CART Racing after the CART/IRL split.[^ref-10] In the fall of 1998 Papyrus released Grand Prix Legends through Sierra Sports.[^ref-10] More NASCAR titles followed, including NASCAR Racing 3, NASCAR Legends and NASCAR Craftsman Truck Series Racing (all 1999).[^ref-5][^ref-27]

### Later Years and Closure

Co-founder David Kaemmer left Papyrus in late 2002, just before the release of NASCAR Racing 2003 Season.[^ref-5] Electronic Arts had taken the exclusive NASCAR license for all platforms.[^ref-11] At the end of the first quarter of 2004, NR2003 was pulled from shelves when its license expired, and the Papyrus website was shut down on April 5, 2004.[^ref-5] In May 2004 Vivendi Universal Games closed the company.[^ref-11][^ref-10] On May 28, 2004, Vivendi and Papyrus sold the copyrights to *NASCAR Racing 2003 Season* to FIRST.net, LLC, which went on to develop iRacing using its engine.[^ref-5]

## Notable Games

### Indianapolis 500: The Simulation (1989)

Papyrus developed the game for Electronic Arts, with David Kaemmer as designer.[^ref-25] Motor Sport notes: "The hardware limitations of the day – typically a mere 512K of RAM – translated into primitive vehicle dynamics, with the cars moving unrealistically along x-y axes." Even so, players could change car set-ups, and it had a car-damage model.[^ref-21]

### NASCAR Racing (1994)

GameSpot says NASCAR Racing "simply blew away the competition", selling more than a million units and showing that ultra-realistic racing was not a niche market.[^ref-10] Virgin published the MS-DOS version, and Sierra On-Line later published the Mac and PlayStation versions.[^ref-24]

### Grand Prix Legends (1998)

Grand Prix Legends recreated the 3-litre Formula 1 cars of 1967. Motor Sport calls it "the most ambitious racing sim ever released" at the time.[^ref-21] Sierra Sports published it in the fall of 1998.[^ref-10]

### NASCAR Racing 2003 Season (2003)

The final game in Papyrus's NASCAR series came out in February 2003.[^ref-10] iRacing says that Grand Prix Legends and NR2003 "were considered monumental achievements in sim racing when they were released and continue to have a following even to this day."[^ref-26]

## Key People

David Kaemmer was Papyrus's co-founder and technical director, and a 1985 Oberlin College mathematics graduate.[^ref-21][^ref-22] After leaving Papyrus in late 2002, he began work on iRacing.com, founded in 2004, where he is CEO.[^ref-5][^ref-26]

Omar Khudari co-founded the company with Kaemmer. He was its president in 1995 and is listed by Wikipedia as CEO.[^ref-4][^ref-5] He left Papyrus soon after Sierra's late-1995 acquisition.[^ref-5]

## Legacy

GameSpot's history of the studio said Papyrus's games were so realistic "that professional drivers often use their games to train for real life races."[^ref-10] iRacing says it "can trace its roots back to the 1980’s when Dave Kaemmer co-founded Papyrus Design Group", and its engine came from NASCAR Racing 2003 Season.[^ref-26][^ref-5]
