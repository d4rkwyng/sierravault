# Papyrus Design Group

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

Papyrus Design Group was an American developer best known for racing simulations, active from 1987 to 2004. Its early work also included contract and porting jobs, such as J.R.R. Tolkien's Riders of Rohan, Nomad and the Windows version of Road Rash.[^ref-34][^ref-35][^ref-39] David Kaemmer and Omar Khudari founded it, and it was based in Watertown, Massachusetts. It is best known for its [[NASCAR Racing Series|NASCAR]] and IndyCar racing series and for Grand Prix Legends.[^ref-5][^ref-11] Giant Bomb describes it as "a developer which played a key role in laying the foundations for the racing simulation genre."[^ref-8]

Papyrus made its name before Sierra. Indianapolis 500: The Simulation (1989) was published by [[Electronic Arts]], and IndyCar Racing (1993) and NASCAR Racing (1994) were released through Virgin and Papyrus itself. Sources differ on which publisher handled which title.[^ref-25][^ref-10][^ref-24][^ref-27] Sierra On-Line acquired the studio in late 1995; Sierra's annual report dates the merger to November 30, 1995.[^ref-5][^ref-23][^ref-30] Vivendi Universal Games shut Papyrus down in May 2004.[^ref-11][^ref-10]

## History

### Founding and Early Years

[[Dave Kaemmer|Kaemmer]] and Khudari started the company in a Cambridge kitchen in 1987 with a "war chest" of $2000.[^ref-4] GameSpot's history says Papyrus "operated primarily as a contract programming services company" at first, although Kaemmer's stated goal was to build "the first truly authentic PC racing simulation".[^ref-43] MobyGames credits the studio on J.R.R. Tolkien's Riders of Rohan (1991, developed by Papyrus, with additional design by Beam Software) and Nomad (1993, developed with Intense! Interactive), both for DOS.[^ref-34][^ref-35] In September 1995, Inside Mac Games described Papyrus as "technically based in Somerville, MA"; MobyGames gives a November 1995 address in Watertown.[^ref-4][^ref-11] Kaemmer grew up in Indiana and graduated from Oberlin College in 1985 with a degree in mathematics.[^ref-21] He and a friend had flown to California to pitch Electronic Arts a flight simulator. Instead, EA asked whether they could make a game simulating the Indianapolis 500.[^ref-21] When Indianapolis 500: The Simulation appeared in 1989, Motor Sport writes, it "was the first legitimate racing sim to reach the market, and it sparked a revolution."[^ref-21]

IndyCar Racing (1993) was an officially licensed product, published by [[Virgin Games|Virgin]] rather than EA, according to GameSpot.[^ref-10] MobyGames is more specific: Papyrus Publishing published it in the US, with Virgin distributing, while Virgin Interactive published it in Europe.[^ref-29] Wikipedia's NASCAR Racing article says the 1994 game was published by Virgin for MS-DOS, while its series article lists Papyrus as publisher.[^ref-24][^ref-27] GameSpot says it sold "in excess of 1,000,000 units".[^ref-10] Introducing a 1995 interview with president Omar Khudari, Inside Mac Games reported that Papyrus had about 80 employees and sales the previous year of about $6 million.[^ref-4]

### Sierra Era

Sierra On-Line acquired Papyrus in late 1995. Sierra's 10-K for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1996 states: "On November 30, 1995 the Company merged with Papyrus, developers of NASCAR Racing and Indy Car Racing, in exchange for 1,169,404 shares of Sierra's common stock."[^ref-30] *Next Generation*'s February 1996 issue reported that Sierra had bought "Papyrus Design Group Inc., developers of NASCAR Racing and Indy Car Racing, and subsequently all the assets of flight sim developer Sub Logic."[^ref-5][^ref-23] Wikipedia says Khudari left Papyrus soon after the acquisition; I found no period source for the departure.[^ref-5] Sources differ on who published IndyCar Racing II: Wikipedia lists Papyrus as publisher (1996), with a later Sierra re-release, while MobyGames gives Sierra On-Line as the 1996 DOS publisher.[^ref-28][^ref-32] From NASCAR Racing 2 (1996) on, Sierra published the NASCAR series.[^ref-27] GameSpot wrote in 2004 that NASCAR Racing 2 remained "the second highest-selling Papyrus game of all time at 800,000 units worldwide."[^ref-31] Dates for IndyCar Racing II also differ: MobyGames gives 1996 for DOS, Windows and Mac, while GameSpot's history says it "arrived one year later in 1995".[^ref-32][^ref-31] IndyCar Racing II sold slowly while the real-life league split, so Sierra had little interest in returning to open-wheel racing. IndyCar Racing II was reissued as CART Racing after the CART/IRL split.[^ref-10] In the fall of 1998 Papyrus released Grand Prix Legends through Sierra Sports.[^ref-10] More NASCAR titles followed, including NASCAR Racing 3, NASCAR Legends and NASCAR Craftsman Truck Series Racing (all 1999).[^ref-5][^ref-27] MobyGames also credits Papyrus as developer of NASCAR Racing: 1999 Edition (Sierra On-Line, 1999) and of the 1996 PlayStation version of NASCAR Racing, and as producer of SODA Off-Road Racing (Sierra On-Line, 1997), which MobyGames credits to Software Allies as developer.[^ref-36][^ref-37][^ref-38]

### Later Years and Closure

According to Wikipedia, co-founder David Kaemmer left Papyrus in late 2002, just before the release of NASCAR Racing 2003 Season; I found no period source for the date, and iRacing's own history page says "Papyrus shut its doors in 2002".[^ref-5][^ref-26] Electronic Arts had taken the exclusive NASCAR license for all platforms.[^ref-11] Wikipedia, with no period source found, says NR2003 was pulled from shelves at the end of the first quarter of 2004 when its license expired, and that the Papyrus website was shut down on April 5, 2004.[^ref-5] In May 2004 Vivendi Universal Games closed the company.[^ref-11][^ref-10] On June 21, 2004, Vivendi announced a North American staff reduction of 350 positions that named "Papyrus & Impressions".[^ref-41] Wikipedia says that on May 28, 2004, Vivendi and Papyrus sold the copyrights to *NASCAR Racing 2003 Season* to FIRST.net, LLC, which went on to develop iRacing using its engine.[^ref-5] MobyGames says Kaemmer bought back the source code and assets in July for FIRST, LLC, which he had founded a month earlier.[^ref-11]

## Notable Games

### Indianapolis 500: The Simulation (1989)

Papyrus developed the game for Electronic Arts, with David Kaemmer as designer.[^ref-25] Motor Sport notes: "The hardware limitations of the day – typically a mere 512K of RAM – translated into primitive vehicle dynamics, with the cars moving unrealistically along x-y axes." Even so, players could change car set-ups, and it had a car-damage model.[^ref-21]

### NASCAR Racing (1994)

GameSpot says NASCAR Racing "simply blew away the competition", selling more than a million units and showing that ultra-realistic racing was not a niche market.[^ref-10] Virgin published the MS-DOS version, and Sierra On-Line later published the Mac and PlayStation versions.[^ref-24]

### IndyCar Racing (1993)

IndyCar Racing was an officially licensed open-wheel game, published by Virgin rather than EA, according to GameSpot, and it was the studio's first game after a four-year gap (MobyGames).[^ref-10][^ref-11]

### Grand Prix Legends (1998)

Grand Prix Legends recreated the 3-litre Formula 1 cars of 1967. Motor Sport calls it "the most ambitious racing sim ever released" at the time.[^ref-21] Sierra Sports published it in the fall of 1998.[^ref-10]

### NASCAR Racing 2003 Season (2003)

The final game in Papyrus's NASCAR series came out in February 2003.[^ref-10] iRacing says that Grand Prix Legends and NR2003 "were considered monumental achievements in sim racing when they were released and continue to have a following even to this day."[^ref-26]

## Key People

David Kaemmer was Papyrus's co-founder and technical director, and a 1985 Oberlin College mathematics graduate.[^ref-21][^ref-22] After leaving Papyrus in late 2002, he began work on iRacing.com, where he is CEO. iRacing's own pages differ on its founding year: its Papyrus page says 2004, while iRacing Studios says Kaemmer and John Henry founded it in 2003.[^ref-5][^ref-26][^ref-33]

Omar Khudari co-founded the company with Kaemmer. He was its president in 1995 and is listed by Wikipedia as CEO.[^ref-4][^ref-5] Wikipedia says he left Papyrus soon after Sierra's late-1995 acquisition.[^ref-5] In November 2004 Game Developer described Cecropia Inc., which opened an animation studio in Orlando, as "formed by Papyrus Studios co-founder Omar Khudari".[^ref-42]

## Legacy

GameSpot's history of the studio said Papyrus's games were so realistic "that professional drivers often use their games to train for real life races."[^ref-10] iRacing says it "can trace its roots back to the 1980’s when Dave Kaemmer co-founded Papyrus Design Group", and its engine came from NASCAR Racing 2003 Season.[^ref-26][^ref-5]
