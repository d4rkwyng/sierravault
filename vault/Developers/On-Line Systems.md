# On-Line Systems

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

On-Line Systems was the company Ken and Roberta Williams founded in 1979 in Simi Valley, California, and the direct predecessor of [[Sierra On-Line]].[^ref-1][^ref-30] Ken had originally registered the name for a planned FORTRAN compiler for the Apple II; the company began trading in earnest in May 1980, when it started selling Roberta's *Mystery House*.[^ref-25][^ref-11] *Mystery House* is credited as the first graphical adventure game, and the company went on to pioneer the graphic adventure genre.[^ref-1][^ref-20]

[[Ken Williams]] was then a programmer and consultant who worked on IBM mainframes, while [[Roberta Williams]] had become an avid player of text adventures.[^ref-24] Dissatisfied with the text-only format, she saw that the Apple II's graphics could enhance the adventure game, and with Ken's programming help she designed *Mystery House*.[^ref-1] The success of *Mystery House* and *The Wizard and the Princess* let the couple move to the Sierra Nevada foothills and expand rapidly.[^ref-27] In 1982 the company was renamed Sierra On-Line.[^ref-1][^ref-30]

## History

### Founding and Early Years

In 1979 Ken had a remote terminal at home for one of his contract jobs, and the mainframe it connected to carried a copy of *Adventure* (Colossal Cave Adventure). Roberta played it obsessively and went on to the Scott Adams adventures once Ken brought an Apple II home.[^ref-25][^ref-1] Other accounts describe Ken finding the game while logged onto a remote mainframe during a tax-software programming session.[^ref-3][^ref-4] Inspired by Agatha Christie's *And Then There Were None* and the board game *Clue*, Roberta sketched out a murder-mystery adventure. Ken proposed using the Apple II's hi-res mode to show pictures alongside the text.[^ref-25][^ref-20]

*Mystery House* was designed, written and illustrated by Roberta and programmed by Ken. It was finished on May 5, 1980.[^ref-20] Roberta traced her drawings on a VersaWriter tablet, and Ken wrote routines that stored each scene as drawing instructions so that the pictures would fit on a floppy disk.[^ref-20][^ref-11] The couple turned down a 25 percent royalty offer from the publisher Programma International and sold the game themselves. They shipped it in Ziploc bags alongside a friend's game, *Skeet Shoot*.[^ref-11] Priced at US$24.95, *Mystery House* sold more than 10,000 copies through mail order, and about 80,000 including its 1982 SierraVenture re-release.[^ref-20] At the time the Williamses were living in Simi Valley, and they demonstrated the game to local computer stores themselves.[^ref-11]

The second Hi-Res Adventure, *The Wizard and the Princess*, followed in September 1980 at $32.95. For it, Ken built a color drawing system and a reusable adventure engine that kept game data separate from the interpreter.[^ref-26] It introduced color graphics, which the Apple II could only display in a handful of colors, using dithering to suggest more. It sold more than 60,000 copies.[^ref-21][^ref-14]

On the strength of those sales the family moved to Coarsegold, near Yosemite National Park, and declared their mountain cabin the company's "headquarters." In December 1980 they leased their first office, a small room above a print shop in nearby Oakhurst.[^ref-27][^ref-13] Ken's brother John Williams became the first official employee.[^ref-27] *Mission: Asteroid*, an easier "Hi-Res Adventure #0," reached the market at the turn of the year.[^ref-27]

### Expansion (1981–1982)

Ken went looking for outside programmers. He advertised royalties of 16 percent of list price and the promise that a programmer could "do nothing but wait for royalty checks." He also recruited at trade shows, including 19-year-old John Harris, whose Atari 8-bit *Jawbreaker* became the company's first big hit on a platform other than the Apple II.[^ref-27] The catalog grew to include arcade-style games such as *Threshold*, *Sabotage*, *Gobbler* and *Pegasus II*, sports games such as *Hi-Res Football* and *Hi-Res Soccer*, and Chuck Benton's adult text adventure *Softporn Adventure*.[^ref-9][^ref-31] The company also briefly ran a software distribution business, which Ken sold to former colleague Robert Leff, who built it into the distributor SoftSel.[^ref-11]

### Rename to Sierra On-Line

On-Line Systems was renamed Sierra On-Line in 1982 and moved to Oakhurst, taking its new name from the nearby Sierra Nevada mountains.[^ref-1][^ref-30] Sources differ on the date: some company histories put the rename in 1980, and HistoryLink dates it to 1983, when the company moved into its own building.[^ref-3][^ref-13] Under the Sierra name the company launched *King's Quest* in 1984, built on the new Adventure Game Interpreter. *Space Quest* followed in 1986, and later *Leisure Suit Larry*, a modern version of *Softporn Adventure*.[^ref-1]

## Notable Games

### Hi-Res Adventure 1: Mystery House (1980)

*Mystery House* was the company's first game and is credited as the first graphical adventure. Its monochrome line drawings show each location above the text.[^ref-20][^ref-1] Players explore an abandoned Victorian mansion in search of hidden jewels while the other occupants are murdered one by one.[^ref-20] *Computer Gaming World* ranked it fourth on its 1996 list of the most innovative computer games.[^ref-20]

### Hi-Res Adventure 2: The Wizard and the Princess (1980)

The player is a wanderer in the land of Serenia who sets out to rescue King George's daughter, Princess Priscilla, from the evil wizard Harlin.[^ref-21] The game improved on *Mystery House* by adding color graphics, and it sold more than 60,000 copies.[^ref-21][^ref-14] Filfre's Jimmy Maher calls its graphics "easily the most impressive graphical display that had yet graced an Apple."[^ref-26] It was ported to the IBM PC in 1982 as *Adventure in Serenia*, Sierra's first PC game, and it is regarded as a precursor to *King's Quest*.[^ref-21]

### Hi-Res Adventure 5: Time Zone (1982)

*Time Zone* was Roberta's most ambitious design. It is a treasure hunt across every continent in several historical eras, ending on an alien planet in the far future. Her design grew to twelve disk sides, at a time when even epics like *Ultima* and *Wizardry* were only just spilling onto a second side.[^ref-28] Ken assembled a team of about ten people for the project, a "Time Zone task force." Artist Terry Pierce drew some 1,400 pictures, and [[Jeff Stephenson]], formerly of VisiCalc developer Software Arts, was brought in as co-head to get the project back on track.[^ref-28] The game shipped on six double-sided disks in March 1982, with more than 1,300 illustrated locations.[^ref-22] At US$99 it sold poorly, and On-Line Systems shelved a planned Atari port.[^ref-22][^ref-29]

### Threshold (1981)

*Threshold* is a fixed shooter written by [[Warren Schwader]] and Ken Williams for the Apple II, based on Sega's arcade game *Astro Blaster*. Ken wrote the Apple II animation routines during two weeks of the two-month project.[^ref-23] On-Line Systems sold about 25,000 copies. Ports followed for the Atari 8-bit, VIC-20, Commodore 64, ColecoVision and Thomson computers, and Tigervision published an Atari 2600 adaptation.[^ref-23]

## Key People

- **[[Ken Williams]]**: co-founder and the company's business head. He trained at Control Data Institute and spent the 1970s as a contract programmer and consultant.[^ref-25] He programmed the early Hi-Res Adventures and wrote the drawing tools and adventure engine they were built with.[^ref-20][^ref-26][^ref-28]
- **[[Roberta Williams]]**: co-founder, and designer and writer of *Mystery House*, *The Wizard and the Princess* and *Time Zone*.[^ref-20][^ref-21][^ref-22] HistoryLink notes that her graphic adventures "made her one of the first recognized female computer-game designers in the world."[^ref-13]
- **John Williams**: Ken's younger brother. He sold *Mystery House* to computer stores around the country and was the company's first official employee.[^ref-11][^ref-27]
- **[[Warren Schwader]]**: co-author of *Threshold*.[^ref-23]
- **John Harris**: author of the Atari version of *Jawbreaker*.[^ref-27]
- **Bob Davis**: designer of *Ulysses and the Golden Fleece* (Hi-Res Adventure #4) and initial manager of the *Time Zone* project.[^ref-28]
- **[[Jeff Stephenson]]**: joined to co-lead *Time Zone* and later designed the AGI and SCI engines behind Sierra's adventure games.[^ref-28][^ref-29]
- **[[Chuck Benton]]**: author of *Softporn Adventure*, which Ken published knowing it would court controversy.[^ref-31]

## Legacy

On-Line Systems' first game opened the graphic adventure genre. *Mystery House* is credited as the first graphical adventure game, and GamePro named it the 51st most important game of all time in 2007.[^ref-20] *Time Zone* is cited as an early example of the modern development model, in which programmers, artists and designers work as separate members of a larger team.[^ref-22] It was also where Jeff Stephenson started, and he went on to become the technical architect of Sierra's later adventure engines.[^ref-29] *The Wizard and the Princess* is regarded as a prelude to the *King's Quest* series.[^ref-21]

The company itself continued as Sierra On-Line, later Sierra Entertainment, whose brand is now owned by Microsoft.[^ref-1] The Strong National Museum of Play holds a Ken and Roberta Williams Sierra On-Line collection of corporate records dating from 1979.[^ref-30]
