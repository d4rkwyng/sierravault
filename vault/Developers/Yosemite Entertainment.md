# Yosemite Entertainment

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Yosemite Entertainment was the name used from 1998 for Sierra On-Line's original Oakhurst, California, location; a May 27, 1998 press release announced the name for what it called a nearly 20-year-old design facility.[^ref-1][^ref-28] In Sierra's 1998 reorganization it housed Sierra FX, the division for adventure games and online multiplayer games, which was based at Sierra's old headquarters in Oakhurst and publicly referred to as Yosemite Entertainment.[^ref-1] MobyGames credits it with two released games, Police Quest: SWAT 2 and Quest for Glory V: Dragon Fire (both 1998); Dragon Fire was its last.[^ref-26][^ref-5]

The studio was closed on February 22, 1999, the day that became known as "Chainsaw Monday."[^ref-1][^ref-19] Of its three major projects, the *Babylon 5* space simulator and the Middle-earth MMORPG were moved to Bellevue, Washington, and, in Jimmy Maher's account, cancelled about six months later; a Navy SEAL shooter was cancelled outright.[^ref-19] Sources from 2000 describe the Middle-earth project differently, as a later restaffing followed by a reported December 2000 cancellation (see [[CXL - Middle-earth Online|Middle-earth Online]]).[^ref-33][^ref-34] IGN reported at the time that Sierra was folding Yosemite into its Bellevue headquarters and that its Middle Earth Online, Navy Seals and Babylon 5 projects were "being folded into the Sierra FX brand name."[^ref-27]

## History

### Formation and Sierra's Reorganization

Sierra moved its headquarters from Oakhurst to the Seattle area in 1993; [[Ken Williams]] cited the difficulty of convincing senior executives to move to rural Oakhurst.[^ref-24] The Oakhurst location was later renamed Yosemite Entertainment and continued under that name until early 1999.[^ref-1] The studio's own press release, dated May 27, 1998, announced the name, with General Manager Craig Alexander saying it "distinguishes us from the rest of the company," and presented the studio's titles as released under the new Sierra FX brand of Sierra's parent, Cendant Software.[^ref-28] After CUC International's acquisition of Sierra in 1996 and a restructuring in 1998, Sierra was divided into five sub-brands: Sierra Attractions, Sierra Home, Sierra Sports, Sierra Studios, and Sierra FX.[^ref-1] Craig Alexander was the studio's manager.[^ref-19][^ref-4][^ref-28]

Former Sierra designer [[Josh Mandel]], in a eulogy quoted by The Digital Antiquarian, wrote that in Oakhurst "the artists and programmers who helped to create those glories were, in fair measure, still living and working there."[^ref-19]

### Projects and Development

Police Quest: SWAT 2 was "the first game to ship under the new Sierra FX brand," developed by "Yosemite Entertainment game design studio in Oakhurst, California"; its gold master left the Oakhurst studio on July 4, 1998, for US release on July 11.[^ref-25]

Yosemite Entertainment's most significant completed project was Quest for Glory V: Dragon Fire, released on December 8, 1998 (some databases list November 30, 1998).[^ref-5][^ref-31] Lori Ann Cole designed it; Wikipedia lists Eric Lengyel and Larry Scott as programmers and says Corey Cole was "preoccupied with programming on the game."[^ref-5] Wikipedia notes that it was the first game in the series to use polygonal 3D for its characters, and says the game "would not have been produced if not for fans' enthusiasm and their pleas to Sierra On-Line."[^ref-5]

The Realm Online, which Sierra had released in December 1996, was also run from Oakhurst: after the closure, a few support personnel for The Realm were the only people left in the building, and its servers stayed there.[^ref-1][^ref-19] The studio's March 2000 Realm fact sheet listed "Version 3.0 Available Now," a price of US $49.95 per year, and room for up to 3,000 players at once on its single server.[^ref-30] The game outlived the studio: its own site was still taking subscriptions in 2026, copyrighted to Digital Alchemy Studios.[^ref-32]

### Cancelled Projects

As of February 1999 Yosemite had three major projects in development, none of them an adventure game: a *Babylon 5* space simulator, a Middle-earth MMORPG conceived as "a far more ambitious successor to The Realm," and an Unreal-engine shooter made in consultation with a former Navy SEAL commander.[^ref-19] The first two were to resume production in Bellevue and the shooter was cancelled outright. An expansion pack for Quest for Glory V was also cancelled.[^ref-19] The May 1998 press release had listed the same slate, giving the Middle-earth online game a beta "in mid-1999" and a "squad-based 3D tactical" Navy SEALs game, drawing on the work of author Richard Marcinko, as "slated for completion in May 2000."[^ref-28] Both relocated projects were then "cancelled just six months later in Bellevue, as part of another round of 'reorganizing.'"[^ref-19]

Babylon 5: Into the Fire was a space combat simulator based on the television series. According to Sierra, it was to feature more than 1,000 different ships and 200 unique locations.[^ref-14] In October 1999 lead programmer Dan Foy described the game to Wired as 65 percent complete.[^ref-20]

Middle-earth Online was an early attempt to bring J.R.R. Tolkien's world to the MMORPG genre. Its team's best-remembered ideas included permanently killing characters in certain situations and keeping Elves few.[^ref-22] The Sierra project did not become The Lord of the Rings Online: Vivendi announced a separate agreement with Turbine in 2003 to produce a Middle-earth Online, and Engadget describes the license as passing to Turbine, "which took the IP and did its own thing."[^ref-15][^ref-22]

### Closure

On February 22, 1999, staff were called to a 10:00 all-hands meeting in the building's common room and told that the Oakhurst office was closing. Management read out a list of the third of the Oakhurst employees who would be offered relocation to Bellevue, and the rest were given until noon to clear their desks.[^ref-19] Sources give different headcounts. Josh Mandel's eulogy, quoted by The Digital Antiquarian, says the shutdown put "most of its 125-plus employees out of work."[^ref-19] Williams's open letter to the Yosemite staff, quoted by GameSpot, called it "a sad ending to Sierra's 20-year operating history in Oakhurst, which at one time represented over 550 Oakhurst-based employees," a historical peak rather than the 1999 total.[^ref-29] Even Alexander had been given only a few minutes' warning.[^ref-19] The name "Chainsaw Monday" was coined by Space Quest designer Scott Murphy.[^ref-19] The closure came three months after Cendant, which then owned Sierra, agreed to sell its software studios to Havas, a subsidiary of Vivendi.[^ref-4][^ref-19] Wikipedia puts the total at about 250 people across Sierra, not Yosemite alone, and says the entire Oakhurst studio was shut down except for the warehouse and distribution department.[^ref-1]

Two well-known designers were not among the Oakhurst layoffs that day. [[Scott Murphy]] said: "I didn't actually lose my job on Chainsaw Monday. I was dumped a month and a half previous to that."[^ref-23] [[Al Lowe]] was by then working in Bellevue; Mandel's eulogy lists him among the "Oakhurst refugees" still making games there.[^ref-19]

About six months after Chainsaw Monday, Codemasters bought Sierra's old Oakhurst facility, along with The Realm, its servers and the rights to the cancelled Navy SEAL game. Most of the people who had worked on the SEAL game had already left town, and "Codemasters had trouble attracting more to such a rural location"; the game was cancelled again within about six months.[^ref-19] Codemasters' own announcement, dated September 17, 1999, had said the Oakhurst studio would be managed by Craig Alexander and staffed by "a significant number of the past employees of the former Yosemite Entertainment."[^ref-26] Wikipedia adds that Codemasters obtained the rights to keep using the Yosemite Entertainment name for the studio.[^ref-1]

## Key People

[[Lori Ann Cole]] and [[Corey Cole]], creators of the Quest for Glory series, led Dragon Fire.[^ref-5] [[Christy Marx]] was involved with Sierra's Babylon 5 game, according to series creator J. Michael Straczynski.[^ref-18] Daniel James was the writer on Middle-earth Online.[^ref-21] Craig Alexander managed the studio.[^ref-19]

## Legacy

The Digital Antiquarian sums up what followed the closure: "The emerging new business model for Sierra was that of a publisher and distributor of games only, not an active developer of them."[^ref-19]

Chance Thomas's Quest for Glory V soundtrack was released alongside a demo before the game's launch; according to Wikipedia, that product sold 50,000 copies and made $500,000.[^ref-5]
