# Bright Star Technology

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Bright Star Technology, Inc. was an educational software company based in Bellevue, Washington.[^ref-21][^ref-26][^ref-36] It was founded during the early 1980s by Elon Gasper and Nedra Goedert, and its best-known products included the HyperAnimation technology, Alphabet Blocks and the Talking Tutors series.[^ref-1][^ref-12] Sierra On-Line acquired the company in July 1992 and ran it as a wholly owned subsidiary.[^ref-12][^ref-21][^ref-29][^ref-30]

## History

### Founding and Early Years

Wikipedia dates the founding only to "the early 1980s" and gives no year; the Corum Group's description uses the same words as Wikipedia's lead, so it is not an independent second source.[^ref-1][^ref-12] The Washington Post reported in December 1993 that "ten years ago" Gasper had been teaching computer science at the California Institute of Technology while trying to help his daughter learn to read; a chart of the lip-synchronisation positions used in Disney animation gave him the idea for Bright Star's animated tutors.[^ref-26][^ref-1] Ken Williams, writing in Sierra's InterAction magazine in Summer 1993, gave a different description of Gasper, calling him "a genius ex-college professor specializing in linguistics", and wrote that Bright Star "had spent nearly a decade wrestling with lip synching" before Sierra turned to it in 1992.[^ref-35]

Williams called Bright Star a "small Seattle-based educational software company", while the same issue's news section reported that Bright Star "will maintain its present offices in Bellevue, WA, near the new Sierra corporate headquarters".[^ref-35] The New York Times (October 1993) likewise placed it in Bellevue, Washington.[^ref-36]

Gasper's US Patent 4,884,972, "Speech synchronized animation", was filed on November 26, 1986 and granted on December 5, 1989; USPTO records show Gasper and Goedert assigning it to Bright Star Technology, Inc. of Washington, effective November 22, 1988.[^ref-27] Encyclopedia.com's company history describes Bright Star as "an educational software firm founded by programmer Elon Gasper".[^ref-15]

### Sierra Era

Sierra's 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 1995 states: "On July 6, 1992, the Company acquired Bright Star, a developer and publisher of educational computer software, for an initial purchase price of $1,000,000 plus transaction costs of $87,000."[^ref-33] The same note says Bright Star's common and preferred shareholders as of July 2, 1992, "certain key executives and a financial advisor" obtained the right to receive "a maximum of up to $2,500,000 in cash or a maximum of up to 400,000 shares of the Company's common stock over a five-year period based on the attainment of certain revenue goals"; $307,000 was earned in fiscal 1994 and nothing in fiscal 1993 or 1995.[^ref-33] Jimmy Maher of The Digital Antiquarian gives the price as $1 million, which matches the initial payment.[^ref-25][^ref-12][^ref-29] He writes that Bright Star brought technology for syncing recorded voices to the mouths of on-screen characters, and that King's Quest VI was the first Sierra game to use it.[^ref-25] Computer Gaming World (September 1993) described Leisure Suit Larry 6 as using a "talker technology" from "Bright Star (Sierra's wholly-owned subsidiary)".[^ref-37] According to Encyclopedia.com, the deal came "just as the educational software market was becoming the fastest-growing segment of the software industry"; Bright Star gained improved distribution and marketing, Sierra built on its HyperAnimation, Talking Tiles and Alphabet Blocks products, and Bright Star hired 19 new employees in the first year, "quadrupling the work force".[^ref-15] After the acquisition Gasper became Sierra's VP of Educational Games.[^ref-22] Popular Science (May 1993) described Bright Star's Alphabet Blocks as a DOS and Macintosh program that teaches letters and sounds through a talking tutor, using "Hyperanimation" to give the tutor humanlike facial movements while it speaks.[^ref-31]

When Sierra moved its corporate headquarters to Bellevue in 1993, Maher describes Bellevue as "close to Microsoft, Nintendo of America, and of course Sierra's own new subsidiary of Bright Star".[^ref-25] Sierra's InterAction magazine reported in Holiday 1993 that management was "temporarily taking refuge in the offices of Bright Star" while a new headquarters was built, and the same issue describes Bright Star Technology, Inc. as "a wholly owned subsidiary of Sierra On-Line, Inc."[^ref-30] The Washington Post's December 1993 survey of children's software placed Bright Star in Bellevue, named its "Dream Team" line, and reviewed Kid's Typing.[^ref-26]

Alphabet Blocks was first released for the Macintosh on October 16, 1992, and Early Math followed in 1993.[^ref-23][^ref-24] Bright Star's other titles include Kid's Typing, Spelling Jungle, Spelling Blizzard and The Lost Mind of Dr. Brain.[^ref-1] Wikipedia also lists two products made with Golden Books Family Entertainment and two Berlitz language titles (see Games).[^ref-1] MobyGames credits Bright Star on seven games from 1992 to 1994, including Math and Spelling with Monker (1994), which has no vault page.[^ref-29] A February 1994 ST Report review says [[1992 - A.J.'s World of Discovery|A.J.'s World of Discovery]] "was developed by Sierra's subsidiaries Coktel Vision and Bright Star Technology", though the vault's game page lists Coktel Vision as the sole developer.[^ref-32][^ref-1] Sierra's InterAction (Summer 1993) also called it "a Bright Star product", with A.J.'s World of Math and A.J.'s World of Language as add-on lessons.[^ref-35]

### Later Years and Closure

USPTO assignment records show Bright Star's patents passing to Sierra On-Line, and record a merger of Bright Star Technology, Inc. into Sierra, both effective October 29, 1999 (reel/frame 010461/0895 and 015541/0374).[^ref-27][^ref-28] This dates the end of the legal entity, not necessarily the end of its operations; no source found says when Bright Star stopped working as a separate studio.

## Notable Games

### Alphabet Blocks (1992)

Alphabet Blocks was first released for the Macintosh on October 16, 1992, and for 16-bit Windows on May 11, 1993.[^ref-23] Popular Science (May 1993) described it as a DOS and Macintosh program teaching letters and sounds through a talking tutor animated with "Hyperanimation".[^ref-31]

### Kid's Typing (1993)

The Washington Post's December 1993 survey of children's software reviewed Kid's Typing.[^ref-26] MobyGames credits Bright Star on it (1993, Windows 3.x and Macintosh).[^ref-29]

### Spelling Jungle (1993) and Spelling Blizzard (1994)

MobyGames credits Bright Star on both: Spelling Jungle (1993) and Spelling Blizzard (1994).[^ref-29] Wikipedia notes they were also sold as Basic Spelling and Advanced Spelling respectively; it also gives Spelling Jungle the alternate title Yobi's Basic Spelling Tricks and the code name Spelling Demons.[^ref-1]

### The Lost Mind of Dr. Brain (1995)

Wikipedia lists The Lost Mind of Dr. Brain among Bright Star's games.[^ref-1] MobyGames' Bright Star credits (1992 to 1994) do not include it.[^ref-29]

## Key People

- **Elon Gasper**: co-founder; inventor on Bright Star's speech-synchronised animation patents; afterwards Sierra's VP of Educational Games.[^ref-1][^ref-22][^ref-27][^ref-28]
- **Nedra Goedert**: co-founder.[^ref-1][^ref-27]

## Legacy

Maher writes that King's Quest VI "became the first Sierra game to make use of" Bright Star's technology for syncing recorded voices to onscreen characters, and that "it was followed by many others".[^ref-25] He also writes that Bright Star "would deliver a steady stream of slick, educator-approved learning software over the years to come", while the acquisition "did lead to the sad demise of Sierra's in-house 'Discovery Series' of educational products".[^ref-25] Bright Star's speech-synchronised animation patents passed to Sierra On-Line in 1999.[^ref-27][^ref-28]
