# Krondor Series

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Krondor series is a three-game RPG line published by Sierra On-Line between 1993 and 1998. Two of its games are set in Midkemia, the world of **Raymond E. Feist**'s *Riftwar Cycle* novels,[^ref-1][^ref-8][^ref-17] and the third, *[[1997 - Betrayal in Antara|Betrayal in Antara]]*, is an unlicensed spin-off set in a world of Sierra's own.[^ref-6] MobyGames catalogs the series in its "Krondor series" group, which it describes as role-playing games based on Feist's Riftwar novels.[^ref-16] The founding entry, *[[1993 - Betrayal at Krondor|Betrayal at Krondor]]* (1993), won *Computer Gaming World*'s Role-Playing Game of the Year award, and it has appeared on all-time lists from *Computer Gaming World*, *PC Gamer US* and IGN.[^ref-4][^ref-18]

Each game came from a different developer: [[Dynamix]] in Eugene, Oregon made *Betrayal at Krondor*;[^ref-1][^ref-15] Sierra On-Line developed *Betrayal in Antara* in-house;[^ref-6][^ref-15] and *[[1998 - Return to Krondor|Return to Krondor]]* was made by 7th Level and the Cincinnati studio PyroTechnix.[^ref-8] *Betrayal at Krondor* set the template: first-person exploration, with a switch to a third-person, turn-based grid for combat.[^ref-1]

## Betrayal at Krondor (1993)

The founding entry. Dynamix released it on June 22, 1993, with [[John Cutter]] as director and Cutter and [[Neal Hallford]] as designers.[^ref-1] Feist had editorial final say on the game, but in his afterword to the novelization he states that the plot, text and new characters were created solely by Hallford and Cutter.[^ref-1] Feist later novelized the game as *Krondor: The Betrayal* (1998).[^ref-3]

**Design innovations:**
- **Chapter-based narrative** in nine chapters, with a party of two or three characters at any time. Chapter 1 follows Locklear, Owyn and Gorath; James later joins Owyn and Gorath; James, Locklear and the magician Patrus take chapters 5 and 7; and Pug travels with Owyn and Gorath in chapters 8 and 9.[^ref-1]
- **First-person exploration** of the overworld, dungeons and caves, switching to a third-person view for **turn-based combat on a grid**.[^ref-1]
- **Moredhel wordlock chests**: combination locks with letters on each dial, opened by solving the riddle written on them. The riddles are written in Moredhel, which Gorath can read and Patrus can read with the Union spell, but the chests can also be opened by brute force.[^ref-1]

**Reception:** Won *Computer Gaming World*'s Role-Playing Game of the Year award in June 1994, and *Computer Games Strategy Plus* named it 1993's best role-playing game.[^ref-4] GOG.com re-released it in 2010, and it is still sold there.[^ref-1][^ref-5]

## Betrayal in Antara (1997)

A spin-off rather than a direct sequel, set in a different world (Ramar), with a new character (Aren Cordelaine).[^ref-6] Sierra On-Line developed and published it, and it shipped on June 30, 1997. Peter Sarrett designed it, Susan Frischer and Peter Sarrett wrote it, and Steven Lee Miles produced it.[^ref-6]

**Design notes:**
- **Familiar interface**: it copied some of the interface elements and gameplay approaches of *Betrayal at Krondor*.[^ref-15] GameSpot noted it was "one of the first RPGs to utilize high-resolution SVGA graphics".[^ref-7]
- **New world**: Sierra had lost the rights to make another game based on Feist's *Riftwar Cycle*, so it created the world of Ramar.[^ref-6]
- **Protagonists**: the story follows William Escobar, Aren Cordelaine and Kaelyn.[^ref-6]

**Reception:** Mixed. GameSpot gave it 6.6/10[^ref-7] and *Next Generation* rated it three stars out of five.[^ref-6] Between January and July 1998 it sold 52,309 copies in the United States.[^ref-6] It is sold on Steam alongside *Betrayal at Krondor* in the "Betrayal Collection".[^ref-19]

## Return to Krondor (1998)

The series finale, and the officially licensed sequel to *Betrayal at Krondor*.[^ref-8] 7th Level bought the license, developed the game basics, produced the backgrounds and added the character voices. It then brought in Cincinnati-based PyroTechnix for the more technical development work. Sierra bought PyroTechnix on December 8, 1997, and PyroTechnix finished the game in 1998.[^ref-8] Andy Ashcraft and Jeryd Pojawa were the designers, and Neal Hallford is among the credited writers.[^ref-8]

**Design notes:**
- **Pre-rendered backgrounds** with changing camera angles for exploration, and **3D character models**,[^ref-20] running on the True3D engine.[^ref-8]
- **Turn-based combat**, with the characters moved manually over the battlefield.[^ref-20]
- **Recorded character voices**, added by 7th Level.[^ref-8]
- **Alchemy and lockpicking**: players brew custom potions and disarm locks in a timed mouse-click minigame.[^ref-8]
- **Party**: James, the player character, with Jazhara, William, Kendaric and Solon.[^ref-8]

**Reception:** Above-average reviews overall. *Computer Games Strategy Plus* called it "a stunning return to form for the Krondor series" with "some of the juiciest turn-based battles ever to grace a role-playing game", while *Computer Gaming World*'s Petra Schlunk found it "a lightweight RPG" that failed to match *Betrayal at Krondor*. CNET Gamecenter reported in December 1998 that it was "selling well".[^ref-9] Available on GOG.com.[^ref-10]

## Rights History

The license changed hands several times. Floppy-disk sales of *Betrayal at Krondor* were slow, so Sierra sold the *Riftwar* rights back to Feist. The game became a hit once it was re-released on CD-ROM.[^ref-1] In 1994 Dynamix cancelled a planned sequel, *Thief of Dreams*.[^ref-8] Without the license, Sierra made *Betrayal in Antara* in its own world.[^ref-6] 7th Level then licensed the rights for *Return to Krondor*. When 7th Level ran into financial difficulties, Sierra bought back the rights along with PyroTechnix's development contract.[^ref-8][^ref-15] *Return to Krondor* was the last game in the series. Feist kept writing Midkemia novels until 2013, ending with *Magician's End*.[^ref-15]

## Critical Legacy

*Betrayal at Krondor* (1993) is the most-cited entry in the series and the one that defines its reputation:

- ***Computer Gaming World*** ranked it #43 on its list of the 150 best games of all time (November 1996) and added it to its Hall of Fame in 2001.[^ref-4]
- ***PC Gamer US*** named it the 31st best computer game ever in 1994, included it in its 1997 "Best 50 Games of All-Time" list, and ranked it 44th in 1998.[^ref-4]
- **IGN** placed it at #92 on its Top 100 RPGs of All Time.[^ref-18]
