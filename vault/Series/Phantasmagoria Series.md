# Phantasmagoria Series

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Phantasmagoria series is Sierra's two-game foray into mature-rated full-motion-video horror — [[1995 - Phantasmagoria|Phantasmagoria]] (1995) designed by [[Roberta Williams]] and [[1996 - Phantasmagoria - A Puzzle of Flesh|Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh]] (1996) designed by [[Lorelei Shannon]]. The two titles share branding and atmosphere but tell unrelated stories — Sierra initially intended the Phantasmagoria title to be a horror anthology, with each installment telling a different story in a different style.[^ref-1][^ref-2]

The series is significant for three reasons: it represents [[Roberta Williams]]'s pivot away from family-fairy-tale adventure into horror aimed at adults; it produced Sierra's best-selling game to that point (the original *Phantasmagoria* sold 300,000 units in its first weekend and more than one million copies in all, and the Digital Antiquarian calls it "the best-selling game ever released by an independent Sierra"); and it tested the commercial limits of mature adventure-game content. The first game carried an ESRB "M" rating, was refused classification in Australia, was declined by CompUSA before release, and was condemned by religious organizations and politicians.[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-11]

## Phantasmagoria (1995)

[[Roberta Williams]]'s passion project — and a deliberate departure from the King's Quest design idiom that had defined her Sierra career. Development took more than two years (Sierra Chest says nearly three) and filming four months; more than 200 people were involved, and the game shipped on **7 CD-ROMs**, "a large number for a computer game" at the time. Its twenty-five actors were filmed entirely in front of a blue screen and composited with more than 1,000 computer-generated backgrounds.[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-9]

The story follows Adrienne Delaney, a novelist who moves with her husband Don into a remote New England mansion once owned by Zoltan "Carno" Carnovasch, a 19th-century magician who practiced black magic and summoned a demon that drove him to murder his wives. Adrienne unknowingly releases the demon from a secret chapel hidden behind a bricked-off fireplace, and the plot escalates through possession and murder.[^ref-1][^ref-5]

**Technical achievements:**
- **Sierra's first computer game targeted to an adult audience** — rated "M" (17+) by the ESRB, with a password-protected filter to tone down the graphic content.[^ref-1]
- **Live-action production at scale** — the first game made in a new $1.5 million filming studio Sierra built in Oakhurst, California; the budget grew from $800,000 to $4.5 million.[^ref-1][^ref-4]
- **Original score** by Mark Seibert and Jay Usher, whose opening theme features a neo-Gregorian chant performed in studio by a 135-voice choir.[^ref-1][^ref-6]
- **7 CD-ROM distribution** — far more than the four or five discs originally expected.[^ref-1]

**Reception:** Reviews were mixed, praising the graphics and suspenseful tone while criticizing the slow pacing and easy puzzles. The game drew controversy: CompUSA and other retailers declined to carry it, religious organizations and politicians condemned it, it was refused classification in Australia, and it received mature ratings in Germany and the United Kingdom.[^ref-7]

## Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh (1996)

The 1996 sequel was written and designed by [[Lorelei Shannon]], who had previously been a writer on Roberta Williams's *King's Quest VII* and *The Dagger of Amon Ra*; Williams was not involved. The story is unrelated to the original — Shannon moved the series into a modern, urban setting with a psychological-horror approach and science-fiction undertones. The protagonist is Curtis Craig, a 26-year-old employee of a pharmaceutical company who suffers disturbing hallucinations at his office; these turn out to be the work of the "real" Curtis, trapped and mutated in Dimension X, acting through a creature called the Hecatomb.[^ref-2][^ref-8][^ref-21]

Unlike the blue-screen original, *A Puzzle of Flesh* was filmed on set and on location around Seattle from February to September 1996, with only its final Alien World scenes shot on bluescreen; it was developed on a $4.5 million budget and released on five CD-ROMs. Its sexual content includes a coworker, Therese, introducing Curtis to the local BDSM club scene. To avoid the ESRB's "Adults Only" rating the game went to the rival RSAC instead. It was banned in Singapore and heavily censored in Australia, and Sam's and Wal-Mart sold only a PG-13 version. It was not a critical or commercial success, and a planned *Phantasmagoria 3*, for which Sierra had asked Roberta Williams to return, never materialized.[^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-10][^ref-20]

**Reception:** Mixed-to-negative. Adventure Classic Gaming scored it 2 of 5 (gameplay 1 of 5), crediting Shannon for attempting to treat its sexual and psychological subject matter in a mature fashion while warning it was suitable only for a limited adult audience.[^ref-10]

## Series Design Identity

What unifies the two titles despite their unrelated stories:

1. **Live-action FMV protagonists** — the first game composited blue-screen actors over rendered backgrounds; the sequel moved to real sets and locations.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-9]
2. **Adult-content positioning** — Sierra stressed the first game was intended for adults and submitted it for an ESRB "M" rating; the sequel was rated by the RSAC.[^ref-1][^ref-3]
3. **Cinematic-thriller storytelling** with horror set-pieces, body horror, and adult themes.[^ref-1][^ref-2]
4. **Multi-disc chapter structure** — the original's seven chapters were spread across seven CDs, with chapters written onto several discs to reduce mid-chapter swapping; the sequel shipped on five CDs.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-9]
5. **Different composers** — Mark Seibert and Jay Usher scored the original; Gary Spinrad is credited with the sequel's music.[^ref-6][^ref-19]

## Legacy

*Phantasmagoria* was Sierra's first game to pass one million copies sold, and the Digital Antiquarian describes it as "the last such sparkling success among its peculiar species of game".[^ref-1][^ref-3] The sequel's commercial disappointment, together with the decline of point-and-click adventures and interactive movies, ended plans for a third installment.[^ref-2] IEEE Spectrum's profile of Roberta Williams names *Phantasmagoria* alongside *King's Quest* and *Mystery House* as her signature games, and notes that it caused controversy because it was aimed at a more mature audience.[^ref-11]

*Phantasmagoria* has been sold digitally on GOG.com since February 2010; both games are currently sold there as separate products.[^ref-1][^ref-12][^ref-18]
