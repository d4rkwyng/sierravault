# Hi-Res Adventures Series

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 16, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Hi-Res Adventures are the graphic adventure games that On-Line Systems (later [[Sierra On-Line]]) released before *King's Quest*: seven numbered games, #0 to #6, from 1980 to 1983.[^ref-1] They began [[Roberta Williams|Roberta Williams's]] design career and built Ken and Roberta Williams's company. This page also covers On-Line's other Hi-Res-branded titles of the period, from 1980 to 1984. Smithsonian calls Mystery House (1980) "the first home computer game ever to include graphics as well as text".[^ref-2]

The games were written for the Apple II's high-resolution graphics mode. Mystery House was written "in 100% assembly language for speed and efficiency, during an era when even most commercial software was still unashamedly coded in BASIC".[^ref-3] Like Sierra's later AGI games, the Hi-Res Adventures drew their pictures as vector graphics rather than bitmaps.[^ref-4] The numbered adventures ran on ADL, Ken Williams's adventure interpreter.[^ref-12][^ref-11]

## Mystery House (1980) — the founding entry

[[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 1 - Mystery House|Mystery House]] is the most historically significant entry in the series. Roberta Williams had played *Adventure* on a remote terminal Ken had at the house for one of his jobs.[^ref-5] The couple then implemented Mystery House in a single month. With 100 blank disks, Ziploc bags for packaging and a couple of magazine advertisements, On-Line Systems "was born in earnest in May of 1980".[^ref-3] The [[Ken Williams]] and [[Roberta Williams]] pages date the founding of On-Line Systems to 1979; Maher's May 1980 date refers to the launch of the business with its first products.

**Key features:**
- **Graphics with text**: line-art pictures of each location accompanied the text parser.[^ref-2]
- **Mystery genre**: the story was inspired by Agatha Christie's *And Then There Were None*; the player meets seven people in an abandoned Victorian house.[^ref-2]
- **Sales**: accounts differ. Jimmy Maher gives 10,000 copies.[^ref-7] The often-quoted figures of about 15,000 copies and $167,000 in revenue come from Wikipedia's Sierra article, whose cited sources do not contain them, so they are unconfirmed (see [[Roberta Williams]]).

## The Wizard and the Princess (1980)

Roberta Williams's second adventure was the company's first major commercial hit. Set in the fairy-tale kingdom of Serenia, it was "the first adventure game with color graphics".[^ref-26] Softalk's sales chart for September 1980 showed it second among Apple II software, behind only VisiCalc. It eventually sold over 60,000 copies against Mystery House's 10,000, and by the end of 1980 Ken and Roberta had rented their first office space near their new home in Coarsegold.[^ref-7][^ref-13]

The IBM PC booter release of 1982 carried the title [[1982 - Adventure in Serenia|Adventure in Serenia]].[^ref-8]

## Time Zone (1982) — the magnum opus

[[1982 - Hi-Res Adventure 5 - Time Zone|Time Zone]] (1982) was Roberta Williams's most ambitious Hi-Res Adventure. It shipped on six double-sided floppy disks, and Softalk counted thirty-nine scenarios spanning "fighting dinosaurs in 400 million B.C. to battling on an alien planet in 4082 A.D."[^ref-9][^ref-24] *Computer Gaming World* called it "easily the largest game program commercially available".[^ref-23]

Jimmy Maher writes that Time Zone "met a fairly cool reception" and that its sales were "very disappointing", and that planned Atari 400/800 ports were dropped.[^ref-10] Its commercial record is disputed, however; the game page sets out the contemporary Softalk chart placings.

## The Dark Crystal (1983) and Gelfling Adventure (1984)

[[1983 - Hi-Res Adventure 6 - The Dark Crystal|The Dark Crystal]] (1983), the final numbered Hi-Res Adventure, was based on Jim Henson's 1982 film and was the first Hi-Res Adventure released under the SierraVenture label. An alternative version for younger players, [[1984 - Gelfling Adventure|Gelfling Adventure]], followed in 1984.[^ref-11] Gelfling Adventure was programmed by [[Al Lowe]], with text and educational concepts by Michael and Rae Lynn MacChesney.[^ref-21]

After *The Dark Crystal*, Sierra's next adventure engine was [[Adventure Game Interpreter|AGI]], which launched with [[1984 - King's Quest - Quest for the Crown|King's Quest]] in 1984.[^ref-1][^ref-4]

## Technical features

- **Vector-drawn graphics**: each picture was stored as drawing commands rather than a bitmap, as in Sierra's later AGI games.[^ref-4]
- **Shared engine**: the numbered adventures ran on Ken Williams's ADL interpreter.[^ref-12][^ref-11]
- **Text parser interface**: players typed short commands. In most games the player saw only a still picture of each location, but The Dark Crystal showed the Gelfling Jen on screen.
- **Platforms**: the series appeared on the Apple II, Atari 8-bit, Commodore 64, PC booter and DOS, and the Japanese FM-7, PC-88 and PC-98.[^ref-12]

## Critical reception and legacy

Softalk's sales chart for September 1980 already showed The Wizard and the Princess as the second-best-selling piece of Apple II software, behind only VisiCalc.[^ref-13] In its November 1996 fifteenth-anniversary issue, *Computer Gaming World* ranked Mystery House fourth among the most innovative games of all time: "Roberta's first game was the first adventure game to use text and graphics together on the screen".[^ref-25]

The series's lasting legacy:

1. **Graphic adventures**: Mystery House is credited as the first home computer game with graphics as well as text,[^ref-2] and The Wizard and the Princess showed the genre could sell.[^ref-7]
2. **Founding Sierra**: the games launched On-Line Systems, which became Sierra On-Line.[^ref-3][^ref-7]
3. **Roberta Williams's career**: Mystery House was her first game, followed by The Wizard and the Princess, Time Zone and The Dark Crystal.[^ref-26]
4. **Numbered series**: the games were numbered #0 to #6.[^ref-1]

Ken and Roberta Williams jointly received the inaugural Industry Icon Award at The Game Awards 2014.[^ref-15] Mystery House is not in the World Video Game Hall of Fame; their later *King's Quest* was inducted in 2020.[^ref-16]
