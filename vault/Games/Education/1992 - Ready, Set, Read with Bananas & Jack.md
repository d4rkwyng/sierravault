# Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

**Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack** (also rebranded as **Beginning Reading**) is an educational software title developed by [[Bright Star Technology]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] as part of the Sierra Discovery Series in 1993 for Windows 3.1 and the Macintosh[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-24][^ref-28]. The program was designed to help young children develop fundamental reading skills through interactive mini-games featuring two animated coaches: Bananas, a chimpanzee (MobyGames calls him a monkey), and Jack, a jack-in-the-box[^ref-1][^ref-28]. Bright Star Technology, founded by Elon Gasper and Nedra Goedert in the early 1980s and described as "a key player in multimedia technology," was acquired by Sierra in July 1992 and became the cornerstone of Sierra's educational games department[^ref-8][^ref-9][^ref-11][^ref-14].

The game was released in 1993. MobyGames dates the Windows 16-bit release to 1993 and the Macintosh version 1.0 to May 19, 1993[^ref-1][^ref-24]; GameFAQs, the GOG Dreamlist and the Sierra Wiki also give 1993[^ref-4][^ref-6][^ref-26], and *The New York Times* reviewed it as a new sequel in October 1993[^ref-28]. The Sierra Chest disagrees, giving a first release date of 1992-01-01[^ref-2]. That date conflicts with the period sources: Sierra's own magazine, *InterAction* (Summer 1993), presents the game as new, with a feature article and a "New!" order-form listing at $42.45[^ref-32], and Bright Star's trademark application for the name "Ready, Set, Read with Bananas and Jack" shows a filing date of 3-15-1993 in the USPTO *Official Gazette* (as read from the scan's OCR text)[^ref-35].

As a sequel to *Alphabet Blocks*, *Ready, Set, Read* targeted slightly older children. *Alphabet Blocks* concentrated on the sounds and names of letters; this title moved on to recognizing simple words and two-letter sounds, building words, rhyming, alphabetizing and reading stories, for ages 4 to 7[^ref-1][^ref-28]. Sierra had added Bright Star in 1992 "just as the educational software market was becoming the fastest-growing segment of the software industry"[^ref-11].

The title was part of the broader "Talking Tutor" series, which included other educational titles such as *Spelling Jungle*, *Spelling Blizzard*, *Kid's Typing*, and *Early Math*[^ref-3][^ref-14]. In *Ready, Set, Read*, wrong answers bring no penalty: the coach narrows the choices after a while, and incorrect responses are kept so the same question can be asked again later[^ref-1][^ref-28].

## Story Summary

*Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack* does not feature a traditional narrative structure, as it is educational software rather than a story-driven adventure game[^ref-1]. Instead, the program creates an interactive playhouse environment where children can explore and learn at their own pace.

The setting consists of a playhouse with six doors, each leading to a different playroom containing distinct educational activities[^ref-1]. The child is accompanied throughout their learning journey by one of two talking coaches: Bananas, a chimpanzee, or Jack, "who lives in a box"[^ref-1][^ref-28]. These characters serve as guides, providing instructions, encouragement, and assistance as the child works through various reading exercises.

The educational content is framed as a series of fun activities rather than formal lessons, with the animated characters creating a welcoming and non-threatening atmosphere for young learners[^ref-1]. The coaches celebrate correct answers with verbal encouragement and animated surprises, making the learning experience feel more like play than schoolwork.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

*Ready, Set, Read* employs a first-person perspective with a point-and-select interface designed for young children[^ref-1]. Players navigate through the playhouse environment by clicking on doors to enter different activity rooms. Within each mini-game, interactions involve clicking or drag-and-drop mechanics to select answers and complete tasks[^ref-1].

The coach characters give spoken instructions, and the player answers by selecting words with the mouse[^ref-1][^ref-28]. MobyGames classifies the pacing as turn-based[^ref-1].

### Structure and Progression

The program is organized around a central playhouse hub with six distinct playrooms, each focusing on different reading skills[^ref-1]:

- **Alphabet Order:** Activities focused on alphabetizing words and understanding letter sequence[^ref-1]
- **Build Words:** Exercises in combining word parts to construct new words[^ref-1]
- **Rhymes:** Games teaching rhyming patterns and word families[^ref-1]
- **Sight Words:** Recognition practice for common sight words[^ref-1]
- **Story Reading:** Simple stories for beginning readers to practice reading comprehension[^ref-1]
- **Two-Letter Sounds:** Phonics exercises focusing on two-letter sound combinations[^ref-1]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Each mini-game presents the player with questions from their chosen coach character[^ref-1]. The child responds by clicking or dragging the correct answer from available options. The program employs a supportive learning approach with no negative reinforcement for incorrect responses[^ref-1].

Instead of penalties for wrong answers, the coach helps the player find the correct answer by gradually reducing the number of available choices after a certain period of time[^ref-1]. Correct answers are rewarded with smiles, verbal encouragement, and animated surprises when the player succeeds on the first attempt[^ref-1]. When all correct responses have been given, the mini-game concludes[^ref-1]. *InterAction* writes that in each playroom "the game is completed when your child has eight correct answers," tracked by a scorekeeping device at the top of the screen such as "eight yellow bananas peeling one by one or eight jack-in-the-box toys popping open"[^ref-32]. *Computer Gaming World* noted that each exercise is timed, that each correct answer earns praise and a banana or jack-in-the-box from the classroom frieze, and that eight right answers earn "a brass band fanfare, a fireworks display, and an invitation to play again"[^ref-31]. MobyGames' screenshot captions show some of these surprises: a running man after building the word "wall" or finding a sight word, a sun after making a rhyme, a fairy for the "wall"/"ball" rhyme, and a brass orchestra congratulating the player on winning a mini-game[^ref-23].

Parents had the ability to customize the game's environment to best suit the needs of their child[^ref-1]. *InterAction* adds that parents can customize the program to prevent children from exiting to an area of the computer's hard disk, and can turn off the music, though not the voices[^ref-32].

*InterAction* names the rooms as the Storybook Room (Jack, story reading), the Word Tiles Room (Bananas, rhyming), the ABC room (Jack, alphabetizing), the Wooden Blocks Room (Bananas, two-letter sounds) and the Stacking Blocks Room (Jack, sight words)[^ref-32].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

L. R. Shannon reviewed the game for *The New York Times* on October 5, 1993, as the sequel to Bright Star's "successful *Alphabet Blocks*". He found the screens "colorful and well drawn" and said the sounds from Bananas and Jack "are startlingly real if the equipment is good enough", with lip-sync technology that "makes the characters look as if they are actually saying the words"[^ref-28]. In his trial rhyming lesson, a wrong guess drew the word spoken back to him, "letting me know I was wrong without actually saying so", while the right answer brought "a cheerful 'That's right.'"[^ref-28]. *Computer Gaming World* reviewed it in its December 1993 issue (the review is unsigned) and concluded: "It is, after all, the program's faultless realism in speech that sets Ready, Set, Read apart from other early reading programs"[^ref-31]. *InterAction*, Sierra's own magazine, described it as a return of "the talking toys from *Alphabet Blocks*" teaching early reading skills for ages 4 to 7[^ref-32]. *MacUser*'s December 1994 children's-software guide listed *Beginning Reading* (Sierra On-Line, ages 3-7, $49.95) in its reading section, calling Bananas and Jack "wonderfully realistic talking toys" that "effectively balance fun and reading-skills instruction", and noting that "children are able to choose their own difficulty level"[^ref-33]. The same issue's "Bottom Line" shopping list named *Beginning Reading* among its top five programs for preschoolers (four years old and under)[^ref-33]. In *Childhood Education* (Summer 1994), Marsha A. Gladhart reviewed it alongside *Word Connection*; the journal's abstract says the program "uses animation and sound to teach early reading skills" and is available for Windows or Macintosh[^ref-38]. Sierra's Spring 1994 *InterAction* lists "National Parenting Center, 1993 Seal of Approval" for *Beginning Reading* in its awards box, which is Sierra's own claim[^ref-37].

*The Computer Museum Guide to the Best Software for Kids* (1995) reviewed the program under its *Beginning Reading* title on p. 90. The page prints one star on the guide's 1-to-4-star scale, marks it "Best for ages 5-7" (one star each for Learning, Looks and Longevity in its "Just the Facts" box), and says: "overall, Beginning Reading is not particularly exciting or innovative." Its bottom line reads: "Used sparingly, this program is helpful for reinforcing the word skills your children are learning in school."[^ref-30] MobyGames records this as its one scored critic rating (25%), plus an unscored *Computer Gaming World* entry[^ref-29].

### Modern Assessment

Modern preservation and retro gaming communities have shown continued interest in Sierra's educational catalog, with users requesting digital re-releases of titles like *Ready, Set, Read* on platforms such as GOG.com[^ref-6]. GameFAQs lists a single user rating of "Unplayable"[^ref-4].

## Development

### Origins

*Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack* was developed by Bright Star Technology of Bellevue, Washington[^ref-28], a company that specialized in educational software[^ref-1][^ref-2]. The title was conceived as a sequel to *Alphabet Blocks*, designed to provide the next step in reading education for children who had mastered basic letter recognition[^ref-1].

By September 1993 *Computer Gaming World* described Bright Star as "Sierra's wholly-owned subsidiary"[^ref-34]. Sierra On-Line published the title as part of its Sierra Discovery Series[^ref-2][^ref-3]. According to the Software Publishers of America, as cited by FundingUniverse, annual home educational software sales rose from $146 million to $243 million in 1993, and Ken Williams stated that Sierra's goal was to become the leader in educational software[^ref-11].

Ken Williams gave a first-hand account of the merger in a column in Sierra's *InterAction* (Summer 1993). He wrote that Sierra's CD-ROM adventure games had struggled to synchronize animated characters' lips with speech, and that "in 1992 I solved the problem by the only other means left to me. A small Seattle-based educational software company named Bright Star had spent nearly a decade wrestling with lip synching." He described its "chief techie, Elon Gasper" as "building an early-reading product called Alphabet Blocks. It featured a talking monkey, Bananas, and a jack-in-the-box named Jack," and added: "It took me about five seconds to decide to merge our companies."[^ref-32] (Williams calls Bright Star Seattle-based; *The New York Times* places it in Bellevue, Washington[^ref-28].)

### Production

Bananas and Jack return from Bright Star's earlier *Alphabet Blocks*: *The New York Times* called this title a sequel to it, and *InterAction* wrote that "Bananas and Jack are back" as animated talking coaches for six reading lessons[^ref-28][^ref-32]. The coaches' role is that of virtual tutors[^ref-1]. Sierra's own magazine describes how the program adapts: if a child waits too long before choosing, "the six choices are reduced to three"[^ref-32]. The design emphasized positive reinforcement: there is no negative reinforcement for wrong answers, and missed questions come back later[^ref-1][^ref-28].

MobyGames counts 10 credited people (7 professional roles, 3 thanks); the game is dedicated to "Cathy, Vincent, Kory"[^ref-22]. People credited on the game were also credited on Bright Star's *Kid's Typing*, *Spelling Blizzard* and *Spelling Jungle*[^ref-22].

### Technical Achievements

The talking coaches used lip-sync technology so that Bananas and Jack appear to say the words coming from the speakers; *The New York Times* called their voices "startlingly real if the equipment is good enough"[^ref-28]. The animated characters provide spoken instructions and feedback throughout[^ref-1].

### Technical Specifications

**Both versions:**[^ref-1][^ref-28]
- **Interface:** Point-and-select with drag-and-drop
- **Perspective:** First-person, fixed/flip-screen
- **Audio:** Spoken character dialogue with lip-synced animation
- **Requirements (IBM PC compatible):** 386 at 16 MHz or better, 4 MB RAM, 10 MB hard disk space, 256-color VGA, DOS 3.1 or higher, Windows 3.1 or higher, Windows-compatible sound card
- **Requirements (Macintosh):** color Mac, System 6.0.7 or later, 4 MB RAM, 10 MB hard disk space
- **Price:** $49.95 suggested retail (also $49.95 in *MacUser*'s 1994 guide[^ref-33]; $42.45 on Sierra's 1993 order form[^ref-32]; "about $35" in the 1995 *Computer Museum Guide*[^ref-30])
- **Editions:** *The Computer Museum Guide* lists "Macintosh CD-ROM or floppy; Windows CD-ROM or floppy"[^ref-30]. Eli's Software Encyclopedia records a retail box with 1.44 MB 3.5" disks, copyright 1993, for ages 4 to 7, with Windows 3.1, a 386, 4 MB RAM and 256-color SVGA listed as requirements[^ref-39].
- **Disk space (*InterAction*):** Macintosh 9.5 MB free (14 MB to install); MS-DOS/Windows 9.5 MB free (11 MB to install)[^ref-32]

The demo version allowed access to all puzzle rooms but limited the number of puzzles that could be solved[^ref-2]. Running the demo and full version requires Windows 3.x, which can be emulated through DOSBox[^ref-2][^ref-3].

### Technical Issues

As 16-bit Windows software, *Ready, Set, Read* does not run natively on modern 64-bit Windows operating systems[^ref-7]. Users seeking to play the software must use Windows 3.x emulation through DOSBox or similar virtualization solutions[^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-7].

The Sierra Help Pages note that many of Sierra's demos from this era are "digital antiques" that require special configuration to run on modern systems[^ref-7].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sierra's *InterAction* order forms carried the title from Summer 1993, as *Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack* at $42.45, through Spring 1994, where it appears as *Beginning Reading* at $39.95[^ref-32][^ref-36][^ref-37].

### Collections

The game was part of three overlapping product lines[^ref-2][^ref-3]:
- **Sierra Discovery Series** – Sierra's umbrella brand for educational titles
- **Bananas & Jack series** – Character-based series featuring the talking coaches
- **Talking Tutor series** – Educational software line with voiced instruction

A demo of the game was included on Sierra's Sneak Peeks 2 PC CD-ROM alongside demos for other Talking Tutor titles including *Alphabet Blocks*, *Spelling Jungle*, *Spelling Blizzard*, *Kid's Typing*, and *Early Math*[^ref-3].

### Fan Projects

Modern retro gaming enthusiasts have expressed interest in seeing Sierra's educational catalog preserved and made available through digital distribution platforms. A wishlist entry on GOG.com requests the addition of *Ready, Set, Read with Bananas & Jack* to their catalog[^ref-6].

### Related Publications

- **Interactive Demo:** An interactive demo version was released that allowed access to all puzzle rooms with limited puzzle completion[^ref-2]
- **Talking Tutor Demos Compilation:** Demo included on Sierra's Sneak Peeks 2 CD-ROM (26.6 MB)[^ref-3]

### Critical Perspective

While Sierra is primarily remembered for adventure series like *King's Quest* and *Space Quest*, it also published educational software such as this title through its Discovery Series[^ref-2][^ref-13]. *The New York Times* framed the game within the challenge facing all such software: being "sufficiently game-like to appeal to children and sufficiently school-like to appeal to parents"[^ref-28].

The game's reliance on Windows 3.x makes it hard to run today, as 16-bit software does not run natively on modern 64-bit Windows[^ref-7].
