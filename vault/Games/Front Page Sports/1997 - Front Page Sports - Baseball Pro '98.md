# Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 is a baseball sports simulation video game developed by [[Dynamix]][^ref-3] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] for Microsoft Windows in 1997[^ref-1]. The game was the third and last edition of the Front Page Sports Baseball series, following Front Page Sports: Baseball '94 and the 1996 Season edition[^ref-2][^ref-9]. Sierra's official description pitched it as "a true-to-life, physics-based baseball game that uses the laws of nature, not probability"[^ref-3].

Contemporary reviewers found its depth overwhelming. GameSpot's Stephen Poole wrote that "if you can think it up, there's a 99 percent chance that there's a way to do it"[^ref-14]. PC Gamer noted that, unlike EA's Triple Play, the series aimed for "a more mature, contemplative, 'expert' audience by offering many gameplay features and reams of in-depth statistics"[^ref-5].

The game was licensed by the Major League Baseball Players Association, with players from the 1996 and 1997 MLBPA rosters[^ref-3]. Its feature list promised all 28 major league ballparks in 3D and player statistics in over 2,000 categories[^ref-7]. Randy Johnson appeared on the cover, as he had on the '96 edition[^ref-2].

## Story Summary

As a sports simulation game, Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 does not feature a traditional narrative storyline. Instead, players can play exhibition games, a full season including the playoffs, or carry on into later seasons with the career play feature[^ref-7].

The Career Mode is the closest thing to a long-form narrative, letting players manage athletes "from their rookie season all the way to the Hall of Fame"[^ref-3]. Sierra's feature list described players who can be "drafted, trained and age gracefully into the Hall of Fame"[^ref-7], and a computer GM can be told to handle anything from free agent signings and draft picks to in-game calls[^ref-14].

A season begins in spring training, where the player sets the roster through exhibition games before moving on to the regular season[^ref-16]. Over time, the player's leagues develop histories and diverge from their real-life 1997 counterparts[^ref-16]. Poole found "an inordinate number of injuries" in simulated play[^ref-14]. Interleague play support was new in the '98 edition[^ref-10].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 supports keyboard and mouse, with joystick and gamepad options[^ref-3][^ref-16]. GameCenter found that "access to features is better" than in the '96 version[^ref-13], but Computer Games Strategy Plus called the new interface "barely an improvement"[^ref-10]. PC Gamer complained of "complex, unattractive, and unhelpful menu screens"[^ref-5].

Sierra's CAMS (Camera Angle Management System) sets up viewing perspectives once the ball has been hit. Poole found it powerful but said it was "difficult to find a setting that works well all the time"[^ref-14]. Play cuts from a close-up batter screen to a full stadium view when the ball is in play[^ref-16]. In the large demo, all player animations are rendered at 16 angles for smoother movement[^ref-12], and the game lists motion-capture animation among its features[^ref-16].

### Structure and Progression

The game offers several modes of play[^ref-7]:

- **Arcade Mode:** Quick games; Sierra's blurb promised a "bigger, better Arcade mode"
- **Batting Practice:** Match up any batter against any pitcher from the 1997 MLBPA rosters
- **Exhibition:** Single games between any two teams
- **Season:** A full season's schedule including the playoffs
- **Career Play:** Multi-season play with drafting, training and aging players
- **Remote League:** Internet league play using the Remote Manager utility[^ref-12]

Wikipedia's article on the series describes each team as holding 50 players: 25 on the active roster, 15 in AAA and 10 in the low minors[^ref-9].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Rather than traditional puzzles, the game challenges players with strategic decisions based on realistic baseball mechanics. The physics engine produces results based on weather, altitude and ball spin, and '98 added altitude data based on each city's location[^ref-7].

A new pitching and hitting model was built around actual MLBPA player performance, based on stats through the end of the 1996 season[^ref-13]. An editable configuration file (PB.INI) lets experienced players change dozens of gameplay factors, including computer manager behaviour and ten physics factors[^ref-14]. Difficulty levels in arcade mode are adjustable[^ref-13].

Team management covers trades, drafts, lineup construction and in-game tactical decisions[^ref-14][^ref-16], with player statistics tracked in over 2,000 categories[^ref-7].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

GameSpot's Stephen Poole awarded the game 8 out of 10. He wrote that "the game looks and feels a lot like real baseball – the animations are dead-on without looking canned and the ball physics seem to be about right"[^ref-14]. He also noted that "if it's on-field action that gets you pumped, you'll probably be a little disappointed with BB Pro '98 – but if you're looking to take on the role of general manager and guide your favorite team to the World Series, there's no better game in town"[^ref-14].

PC Gamer gave a more critical assessment with a 60% score. Reviewer Gary Whitta acknowledged that "Sierra's commitment to excellence and authenticity in this area is admirable" but lamented that "it's just a shame that it has to come at the expense of good, old-fashioned fun"[^ref-5]. The review's bottom line stated the game "achieves what it sets out to do, but baseball has never looked less inviting"[^ref-5].

GameCenter's Lance Elko gave the game 8 out of 10, noting that "arcade play is passable but not the game's prime mode"[^ref-13].

Steve Bauman of Computer Games Strategy Plus gave a mixed but largely positive verdict[^ref-1]: "While it's true this is really the game Baseball Pro 96 should have been, any hardcore baseball grognard can't deny this is still the game they'd design"[^ref-10]. He added that "with more features than any game on the market, it's as close to baseball perfection as anything out there, but remains enough of a mess to make it unlikely to convert its past critics"[^ref-10].

Computer Gaming World's Robert Coffey gave the game 2.5 out of 5 in a review titled "Minor Leaguer" (issue #159, October 1997), and Game Informer's Erik Reppen gave it 7.25 out of 10 (issue #52, August 1997)[^ref-1]. Wikipedia's review table also lists Computer Games Strategy Plus at 4/5 and AllGame at 4.5/5[^ref-1].

### Modern Assessment

The game keeps a following among baseball simulation fans. On GOG's Dreamlist, one user calls it "one of the greatest baseball simulation games ever," and another recalls that "the results in the simulated games were always believeable [sic]"[^ref-8]. GameFAQs users rate it as "Great" based on 9 ratings, with a reported length of 47 hours[^ref-15].

AllGame reviewer Brad Cook called it "a baseball lover's dream come true." He advised that "if you just want a pure arcade game, you'd be better off passing this one by and going for one like Electronic Arts' Triple Play"[^ref-16].

## Development

### Origins

Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 grew out of Dynamix's line of sports simulations, which began with Front Page Sports Football in 1992, Dynamix's first sports simulation game[^ref-30]. The baseball series launched in 1994, the first baseball game developed under Dynamix's "Front Page Sports" brand name[^ref-2].

Sierra announced the '98 edition as including "many user-requested updates and features"[^ref-23]. Computer Games Strategy Plus wrote that Sierra "concentrated on making the game actually work as advertised," after the bugs of the 1996 version, "and offered little in the way of new features"[^ref-10].

### Production

Glen Wolfram served as Director and Lead Programmer, with Doug Johnson as Lead Designer[^ref-3]. MobyGames credits 114 people: 94 developers and 20 people in the thanks[^ref-3].

The physics engine simulates ball behavior under varying conditions, including altitude, weather and spin[^ref-7]. Motion capture was used for the player animations[^ref-7][^ref-16].

MobyGames credits the music to Charles Barth, with Tim Pierce, Mike Miller, Billy Savage and Barth as musicians. It credits sound effects to "LOUDMOUTH, Ken Rogers" and sound programming to James Poelke[^ref-29].

### Technical Achievements

The game's physics engine set it apart from games driven by predetermined statistics. Each play's outcome was calculated from physics, including weather, altitude and ball spin[^ref-7]. A retail product description invited players to "hit one over the Green Monster in Boston or bounce one into the ivy along the outfield walls at Wrigley Field"[^ref-27].

The CAMS camera system offered multiple viewing perspectives, and a VCR feature allowed play review from any angle in the ballpark[^ref-7]. The feature list promised all 28 major league ballparks rendered in 3D[^ref-7]. GameSpot, however, noted that the Braves' Turner Field was missing[^ref-14].

The Remote Manager utility automatically sends and receives league data files over the Internet for league commissioners[^ref-12]. Poole noted that the lack of modem support meant head-to-head play between league members was only possible at the same keyboard[^ref-14]. The game also added support for interleague play[^ref-10].

GameRankings' archived page lists a patch entry from GameSpot dated September 21, 1997[^ref-21]. A playable demo was released in 1997[^ref-23][^ref-20].

The game was released in the United States, United Kingdom and Germany[^ref-17]. CUC Software International distributed it in the UK and Germany[^ref-31], and alternate titles include "DSF Baseball Pro '98" (Germany) and "Sierra Sports: Baseball Pro '98" (UK)[^ref-3].

### Technical Issues

PC Gamer found the action "numbingly slow even on a fast Pentium"[^ref-5] and called the menus "unattractive, and unhelpful"[^ref-5].

The 1996 edition had suffered from significant bugs, which the '98 release was designed to address[^ref-10]. Bauman still found "some quirks and an occasional crash bug, but nothing near the problems its previous incarnation experienced"[^ref-10].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- Randy Johnson appeared on the covers of both the '96 and '98 editions[^ref-2]
- The game was priced as an update ($29.95) rather than a full-price standalone, with Sierra offering a $10 rebate for owners of the 1996 version[^ref-23][^ref-13]
- Wikipedia's series article notes that team colors can be adjusted but there are no logos on player uniforms[^ref-9]

## Voice Cast

MobyGames credits Bill Barrett as voice talent[^ref-29]. GameSpot described the play-by-play as "minimalist in the extreme," limited to an announcer calling strikes, outs and fouls[^ref-14].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for the game have been documented. CNET Download records more than 43,500 downloads of the large demo[^ref-28]. The game was rated E for Everyone by the ESRB[^ref-24].

Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 was the last edition of the Front Page Sports Baseball series[^ref-9]. The related Football Pro line continued until a 1999 version that was recalled[^ref-30].

### Collections

No compilation releases of the game are documented in the sources consulted.

### Fan Projects

The series remains popular as a simulation engine for online leagues[^ref-9]. Shortly after release, web sites were already "flooded with alternate configurations" of the PB.INI file[^ref-10].

### Related Publications

- **Online Manual:** Michael Stearns and Doug Johnson[^ref-29]
- **Printed Manual:** Michael Stearns and Doug Johnson[^ref-29]

### Critical Perspective

Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 shows the split in 1990s sports gaming between simulation depth and arcade accessibility. PC Gamer contrasted EA's Triple Play, which "goes for the throat with audio visual sizzle, slick presentation, and arcade action," with this game's "expert" audience[^ref-5]. Reviewers broadly agreed that its strength was management rather than on-field play[^ref-13][^ref-14][^ref-16].
