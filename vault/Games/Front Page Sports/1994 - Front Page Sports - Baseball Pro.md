# Front Page Sports: Baseball '94

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

Front Page Sports: Baseball '94 is a baseball simulation released in 1994 for DOS, developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line[^ref-19]. It was the first baseball game developed under Dynamix's "Front Page Sports" brand, and it delivers "an equally balanced team-management and statistically accurate experience" based on the 1994 Major League season[^ref-19][^ref-4]. It shipped on both floppy disk and CD-ROM[^ref-19][^ref-21].

The game followed Dynamix's Front Page Sports football games. Computer Gaming World's November 1994 review recalled that two years earlier Dynamix had "re-wrote the book on computer football" with Front Page Sports Football, and judged the baseball game: "Quite simply, Dynamix has done it again."[^ref-26] Sierra released it, CGW noted, "on the very day commissioner Bud Selig declared Major League baseball's season over"[^ref-26].

## Story Summary

As a sports simulation, Front Page Sports: Baseball '94 has no scripted story. The player manages a club through single games, full seasons or a career mode, in which players "age, improve, decline, retire"[^ref-19][^ref-4]. Computer Gaming World described career leagues as the feature that "really sets it apart in this crowded market": managers could build "a last place team into a championship dynasty over several seasons", and no other game then on the market offered this[^ref-26]. A season can end with the playoffs and "the ultimate goal, the World Series"[^ref-32].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Play is divided into two modes, action and manage-only, with three difficulty levels for each mode[^ref-26]. In Computer Game Review's description, pitching, batting, fielding and running can each be set to computer control or to Basic, Standard or Advanced. On Basic you pick only the pitch type; Standard adds location; Advanced adds speed[^ref-32]. The game supports keyboard, mouse, analog joystick and the "Batter Up" bat controller[^ref-21].

CGW praised the "gorgeous, high resolution pitcher-batter interface" and the rotoscoped animation, but noted an awkward control choice: the same button that throws the ball also makes fielders dive[^ref-26]. Old-games.com's later review found the drop-down menu interface hard to navigate, but said it allowed "a lot of options"[^ref-18].

The Camera Angle Management System (CAMS) lets players replay action from many perspectives. CGW's reviewer said the feature alone increased his playing time "by 50%"[^ref-26]. Old-games.com describes it as letting you "position the camera anywhere in the ballpark from any angle"[^ref-18].

### Structure and Progression

Leagues can be defined with 8 to 48 teams in up to three leagues with three divisions each, with the designated hitter rule set per league and short, half or full season lengths[^ref-18]. Next Generation noted that "Up to 48 teams in three different leagues can be created"[^ref-25]. The game ships with rosters for all 28 major league teams[^ref-26].

Each team carries up to 50 players: 25 on the major league roster, 15 at AAA or on the disabled list, and 10 in the low minors[^ref-31]. Every June an amateur draft is held, and players drafted out of high school or college start in the low minors[^ref-26]. Trades and free agency are also simulated. CGW called the computer general managers "heartless sharks"[^ref-26]. A separate program lets players modify existing players or create new ones[^ref-19][^ref-4].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Instead of deriving performance from prior-year statistics, the designers gave each player numerical ratings on a 1–100 scale. Ratings improve with spring training and deteriorate with age and injury[^ref-26]. Batters are rated for contact, power, speed, fielding at all nine positions, arm strength and pull and ground-ball tendencies. Pitchers are rated for holding runners, endurance, control, ground-ball/fly-ball tendency and seven different pitches[^ref-18]. The statistical data was supplied by STATS, Inc.[^ref-22][^ref-32]

Sierra's own claim, quoted by CGW, was that "Ball-flight is based on a real physics model." The reviewer saw pulled balls spin foul or skip down the line, unlike the "arrow-straight paths" of rival games[^ref-26]. Weather is modelled per stadium, and CGW gave the example of the wind at Candlestick turning homers into pop flies. Rain delays also occur[^ref-26]. Injuries range from everyday sprains and bruises to, in one of CGW's games, a broken skull that cost a player six months; disabled-list stints last at least 15 days[^ref-26]. Next Generation summed it up: "Injuries, recovery time, weather conditions, and both amateur and free-agent draft options create an amazingly realistic approach to the management of baseball"[^ref-25].

The game includes twelve ballparks: The Ballpark at Arlington, the Astrodome, Atlanta–Fulton County Stadium, Camden Yards, Candlestick Park, Comiskey Park, Dodger Stadium, Fenway Park, Jacobs Field, Royals Stadium, Wrigley Field and Yankee Stadium[^ref-18][^ref-26].

| Next Generation (Apr 1995) | 4/5 stars | Issue 4[^ref-9][^ref-25] |

MobyGames recorded a critic average of 78% from eight ratings as of August 2025[^ref-19]. George Jones wrote in CGW that the game "will be the baseball sim once a few glitches are ironed out", adding: "I'm a die-hard Tony LaRussa II fan, but I'll be switching to this game."[^ref-26] The review's cons noted that a full season took 36 hours on a Pentium, and that there were "some bugs to fix"[^ref-26]. He also flagged fielder AI that skips easy force-outs, too many hit batsmen, and sound-card detection problems[^ref-26]. PC Gamer wrote that "The career play, physics-based action, and unmatched graphics and sound set a new standard for baseball simulations," but that "Weak stats and a few annoying bugs" kept it from an Editor's Choice Award[^ref-24]. Next Generation called it "the best diamond sim"[^ref-25].

Not every critic was won over. A May 1995 CGW roundup of baseball games called the game "statistically suspect": player ratings barely changed from year to year, and the on-field fielding display was too basic. It also complained that the game "takes days to simulate a full season"[^ref-30].

### Awards

- Power Play: Best Sports Game of 1994 (issue 02/1995)[^ref-23]
- Computer Game Review: runner-up (second place) for Sports Game of the Year, behind NASCAR Racing (February 1995). The magazine wrote that it "went as far with stats as it could"[^ref-33]
- Computer Gaming World readers' "Playing Lately?" poll: No. 7 in the results for the November 1994 issue (published January 1995)[^ref-27]

### Modern Assessment

Old-games.com calls it "one of the very best baseball simulations ever made" for the armchair manager. It also says the game "never achieved the same level of accolades and commercial success as its football counterpart", suggesting its graphics, interface and action mode as reasons[^ref-18]. MyAbandonware's users rate it 4.56/5 from 9 votes[^ref-34]. MobyGames' four player ratings average 2.8 out of 5[^ref-19].

## Development

### Origins

According to CGW, plans for a Front Page Sports baseball game were disclosed soon after the release of Front Page Sports Football[^ref-26]. MobyGames credits Patrick Cook, the game's executive producer, with the original concept[^ref-20]. Computer Game Review described the game as carrying over "the same attention to detail" as its football predecessor[^ref-32].

### Production

MobyGames lists 63 people in the credits (48 developers, 15 thanks)[^ref-19]. Key credits[^ref-20]:

| Role | Credit |
|------|--------|
| Lead Designer | L. Allen McPheeters |
| Additional Design | Scott Youngblood, Doug Johnson |
| Original Concept / Executive Producer | Patrick Cook |
| Lead Simulation Programmer | David McClurg |
| Lead Shell Programmer | Glen Wolfram |
| Art Director | Ian Gilliland |
| Audio Director | Jan Paul Moorhead |
| Music & Additional Sound Effects | Timothy Steven Clarke |
| Sound Effects | Christopher Stevens |
| Manual Writer | Randy Chase |

Bill James is among those listed under special thanks[^ref-20]. The sound engine was by Human Machine Interfaces (HMI), and STATS, Inc. contributed to the release[^ref-19][^ref-22]. At launch CGW listed the price as $54.95, while Computer Game Review gave a retail price of $59.95[^ref-26][^ref-32].

### Version History

| Version | Date | Notes |
|---------|------|-------|
| 1.0 | 1994 | DOS release, floppy and CD-ROM; US and UK[^ref-22] |
| 1.01 | 12/2/94 (listed in CGW, February 1995) | "fixes many reported problems, including crash bugs and General Manager AI"[^ref-28] |
| 1.02 | 2/3/95 (listed in CGW, April 1995) | Also fixes "roster handling bugs"[^ref-29] |

An official add-on, *Front Page Sports: Baseball '94 – Legends & Stadiums*, added rosters and stats for 48 of the greatest teams in baseball history and 16 more big-league ballparks[^ref-35]. CGW's May 1995 roundup described it as "much delayed" and still on its way[^ref-30].

## Legacy

CGW reported in May 1995 that a *Front Page Sports: Baseball '95* had been in the works "but has now been put on hold due to the baseball strike"[^ref-30]. The next edition was *[[1996 - Front Page Sports - Baseball Pro '96|Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '96 Season]]*, released on 30 June 1996. *[[1997 - Front Page Sports - Baseball Pro '98|Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98]]* followed on 31 May 1997[^ref-9].
