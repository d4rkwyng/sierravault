# More After Dark

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

More After Dark, released in 1991, was an expansion package for Berkeley Systems' After Dark screensaver software—"the software that kicked off the screensaver craze of the 1990s."[^ref-1] The add-on contained 26 new modules,[^ref-2] one of which was Lunatic Fringe, a playable top-down space shooter.[^ref-5] The Macintosh Repository's description calls that module "absolutely brilliant... a fully playable game similar to Maelstrom but somewhat even cooler."[^ref-3]

The package required an existing installation of After Dark 2.0 or another After Dark product to function, and would refuse to install if the base software could not be found.[^ref-2] After the original, Berkeley Systems released further editions, including themed ones built around licensed properties such as Star Trek, The Simpsons, Looney Tunes, Marvel, and Disney characters.[^ref-4] The After Dark system also allowed for the development and use of third-party modules, "many hundreds of which were created at the height of its popularity."[^ref-4]

According to MobyGames, the inclusion of Lunatic Fringe made More After Dark Berkeley Systems' first foray into games. The package billed the module as "the exciting new space game from the creator of Solarian II."[^ref-5]

More After Dark was published by Berkeley Systems, not Sierra.[^ref-5] In April 1997, CUC International, which had already acquired Sierra On-Line the year before, acquired Berkeley Systems.[^ref-20]

## Story Summary

More After Dark's gaming content centers on Lunatic Fringe, a simple top-down space shooter with a sci-fi/futuristic setting.[^ref-5] The player flies a ship through 360° space, encountering planets, asteroids and enemy ships, and starts from a home base that must be revisited to refuel and repair.[^ref-5] David Tointon's 1992 review summed up the objective plainly: "the aim is to kill everything in sight."[^ref-3]

Across the wider After Dark line, modules sometimes referenced each other: flying toasters from the famous module might appear in the Fish screensaver, while the cat from Boris could show up in the Bad Dog screensaver.[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Once the Lunatic Fringe module is activated, the player controls the ship with the keyboard, flying it freely in 360° space.[^ref-5] A mini-map shows when objects are approaching.[^ref-5] According to the Macintosh Repository, the module offers a bigger playing area than Maelstrom and scrolls rather than wrapping at the screen edges.[^ref-3]

Modules were chosen and configured from the After Dark control panel. Tointon described clicking a module's name and then either pressing the demo button, moving the pointer into the "sleep now" corner, or simply waiting for the screen to fall asleep on its own; almost every module had customisable settings.[^ref-3]

### Structure and Progression

The game begins at the player's home base, to which the ship must return from time to time to refuel and/or repair.[^ref-5] As the player explores, planets, asteroids and enemy ships come into view.[^ref-5]

Tointon's review of After Dark and More After Dark together warned: "If you intend to do any work whatsoever, DO NOT buy After Dark. It is more addictive than all the Mac games put together."[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Rather than traditional puzzles, Lunatic Fringe is a straightforward shooter.[^ref-5] Its most unusual mechanic is component damage: different parts of the ship can be damaged to varying degrees, and once a part passes a damage threshold it misbehaves in its own way, such as a disabled blaster or misbehaving thrusters.[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

David Tointon reviewed the two packages together as "After Dark and More After Dark" in *Apple 2000* (February 1992, p. 55).[^ref-3] He called Lunatic Fringe "a fairly decent game by the author of Solarian II," judging it "a lot better than some of the games out commercially on the Mac, but I still think it could be better."[^ref-3] He also praised the visual quality of the modules generally: "I wish that Mac games had as good graphics as the Fish! module."[^ref-3] His verdict on the whole package was "buy it. You'll love it."[^ref-3]

### Modern Assessment

Interest in Lunatic Fringe outlasted the original software: Low End Mac's 2007 After Dark history pointed readers "who loved Lunatic Fringe, which was a part of More After Dark" to a Sealie Software project for running it under the Classic environment of Mac OS X. That project still needed the original Lunatic Fringe module.[^ref-21] The Macintosh Repository's description still rates the module "absolutely brilliant."[^ref-3]

Berkeley Systems' founders, Joan Blades and Wes Boyd, later created MoveOn.org.[^ref-8]

## Development

### Origins

After Dark itself began as a collaboration between Jack Eastman and Patrick Beard at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory. In Beard's account, Eastman "wrote the basic engine, and I came up with the plugin model," before Beard took a job at Berkeley Systems.[^ref-10] "We never imagined it would define a new category for entertainment software," Beard later reflected.[^ref-10]

More After Dark was an add-on for After Dark 2.0.[^ref-5] Its Lunatic Fringe module came from the author of Solarian II.[^ref-5][^ref-3] No source consulted names the individual developers of More After Dark's modules.

### Production

More After Dark was built as an add-on package for the After Dark 2.0 base software and contained 26 new modules.[^ref-2] The underlying After Dark framework was written in assembly language and C.[^ref-6] On the Macintosh, the More After Dark installer decompressed and installed the new modules and also updated After Dark for System 7 compatibility.[^ref-3]

Speaking about the After Dark team in general, Beard said: "I think you go through the rest of your working life looking for that kind of experience. It doesn't come along every day."[^ref-10] He left Berkeley Systems in 1992 to attend graduate school.[^ref-10]

**Development Credits:**
- Individual credits for More After Dark are not documented in available sources; MobyGames lists Berkeley Systems as developer and publisher only[^ref-5]
- **Lunatic Fringe:** credited as "from the creator of Solarian II"[^ref-5]

### Technical Achievements

The After Dark system was notable for its modular architecture, which allowed third-party developers to create compatible screensaver modules; many hundreds were created at the height of the software's popularity.[^ref-4]

Beard named the modules as After Dark's defining feature: "Clearly the modules made it stand out. Our competition, Pyro, hastily brought out their own modules after we did, because we quickly eclipsed it." He also credited its design: "I also think there was an elegance to the product, a simplicity that was consistent with the philosophy of the Macintosh itself."[^ref-10]

### Technical Specifications

**Macintosh Version:**
- **Media:** 1 disk (3.5" Floppy Disk)[^ref-5]
- **Requirements:** After Dark 2.0[^ref-5]
- **File Size:** 600 KB[^ref-7]
- **Perspective:** Top-down[^ref-5]
- **Later edition:** The Macintosh Repository's entry for More After Dark is dated 1996. It lists the package as "26 AD modules for After Dark 3.0 or 4.0" with a 68K + PPC (FAT) architecture, so these specs describe that later edition, not the 1991 release.[^ref-3]

**Windows 3.x Version:**
- **File Size:** 1 MB[^ref-7]
- **Requirements:** Windows 3.0/3.1[^ref-2]
- **Prerequisite:** Existing After Dark 2.0 installation[^ref-2]

### Cut Content

Despite the volume numbering suggesting planned sequels, no "Volume 2" of More After Dark was ever released.[^ref-5] Later editions of After Dark added more games as screensaver modules instead.[^ref-5] Berkeley Systems continued the main After Dark line through version 4.0 in 1996.[^ref-6]

### Technical Issues

The Windows 3.x version required proper installation of the base After Dark software, with the installer checking for its presence before proceeding.[^ref-2]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The After Dark series was known for intertextual references between modules:
- Flying toasters from the famous module would occasionally appear in the Fish screensaver[^ref-9]
- The cat from the Boris screensaver appeared in the Bad Dog screensaver[^ref-9]
- The Bad Dog module was later developed into an animated TV series[^ref-6]

The Flying Toasters became After Dark's best-known image. In October 1993, U.S. District Judge Eugene F. Lynch ruled that Berkeley Systems had a valid copyright on the "Flying Toaster" symbol and granted a preliminary injunction against Delrina's Opus 'n Bill Screen Saver.[^ref-22] Discussing the MultiModule meta-module, Beard recalled: "The first time I saw Boris running on top of a field of flowers, I realized I'd come up with something very special."[^ref-10]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

The Internet Archive describes After Dark, the base product More After Dark extended, as "the software that kicked off the screensaver craze of the 1990s."[^ref-1] MobyGames notes that the After Dark games proved popular enough to warrant a release of their own, After Dark Games.[^ref-5] In April 1997, CUC International, which already owned Sierra On-Line, acquired Berkeley Systems.[^ref-20] Sierra On-Line then published After Dark Games in 1998.[^ref-11]

### Collections

- **After Dark Deluxe:** The Macintosh Repository notes that "some of the modules also appear in After Dark Deluxe."[^ref-3]
- **After Dark Series** (2003): a Sierra Entertainment compilation containing After Dark Games and an After Dark screensaver collection. MobyGames does not list which modules it includes, so it is unconfirmed whether Lunatic Fringe is among them.[^ref-13]

### Fan Projects

The After Dark community created hundreds of third-party modules during the software's peak popularity.[^ref-4] The Internet Archive's emulated After Dark 2.0 item has More After Dark and Art of Darkness added to its install.[^ref-1] The Macintosh Repository and the Internet Archive's Windows package also preserve copies.[^ref-2][^ref-3]

### Related Publications

- **Manuals:** After Dark and More After Dark each came with a small accompanying manual[^ref-3]
- **Books:** Two books about After Dark, *Cool Mac After Dark* and *Art of Darkness*, were both released in 1992[^ref-21]

### Critical Perspective

More After Dark marks the point where Berkeley Systems' screensaver line first included a game.[^ref-5] Tointon's 1992 review captured that blurred line, warning that "After Dark is so much fun that I keep forgetting that it's a utility, not a game."[^ref-3] Beard attributed the product's appeal to "an elegance to the product, a simplicity that was consistent with the philosophy of the Macintosh itself."[^ref-10]
