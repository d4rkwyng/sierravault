# Indianapolis 500: The Simulation

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

Indianapolis 500: The Simulation is a racing simulation of the Indianapolis 500. It was developed by [[Papyrus Design Group]] and published by [[Electronic Arts]] for MS-DOS in December 1989, and an Amiga version followed in 1990.[^ref-1][^ref-3] It was the first game from Papyrus, the Massachusetts studio that David Kaemmer and Omar Khudari founded in 1987. Sierra On-Line bought Papyrus in late 1995, which is why the studio's whole catalogue is covered here.[^ref-4][^ref-5]

The game models a single track, the Indianapolis Motor Speedway, and the field of 33 cars from the 1989 race.[^ref-1][^ref-6] Kaemmer built it around a detailed physics model. In his words, "when you move the joystick, it does no more than change the steering angle of the front wheels", and every change in speed or direction is then worked out "using gobs of Newtonian mechanics".[^ref-7] Players can adjust wings, tyre pressures, wheel stagger, suspension, gearing and turbo boost, and test each change in practice.[^ref-1][^ref-6]

Reviewers in 1990 rated it highly. *Computer Gaming World* called it "a hyper-realistic simulation" and "a valuable educational tool for any aspiring race car driver". Several European magazines scored it above 90%.[^ref-6][^ref-13] In 1996 *Computer Gaming World* placed it 122nd on its list of the best games of all time, saying that "the nascent Papyrus redefined the racing sim on the PC and Amiga".[^ref-8] Papyrus followed it with *[[1993 - IndyCar Racing|IndyCar Racing]]* in 1993.[^ref-10][^ref-14]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The race is seen from the cockpit, in first person.[^ref-1][^ref-7] The game supports keyboard and joystick control on PC, and the Amiga version adds mouse steering.[^ref-14][^ref-4] *Computer Gaming World* found the keyboard workable "once one gets used to it". With a joystick, the review said, "control is natural and transparent", and steering felt "highly responsive, a pleasant surprise after so many recent sluggish driving games".[^ref-6]

Players can call up an instant replay at any time. It has six camera angles: driver's view, behind the car, track-mounted, grandstand, helicopter, and a leader/crash camera that shows events the driver could not have seen.[^ref-6] Up to three car set-ups and one replay can be saved to disk, but a race in progress cannot be saved.[^ref-14]

### Structure and Progression

The game follows a real race weekend: practice, a four-lap qualifying run, and the race. The qualifying position comes from the average of the four laps.[^ref-1][^ref-14] Players can race 10, 30, 60 or the full 200 laps. The shorter options turn off car damage and the full-course yellow flag. The 10-lap race has neither, the 30-lap race adds yellow flags, and the 60- and 200-lap races add damage as well.[^ref-14][^ref-1]

The 32 computer-driven cars follow the starting grid of the real 1989 Indianapolis 500. The player's car, number 17, takes the place of Rich Vogler, who qualified last.[^ref-4][^ref-14][^ref-16] There are three chassis and engine combinations. DOS Days describes the March/Cosworth as the slowest and easiest to handle, the Lola/Buick as faster but harder, and the Penske/Chevrolet as the fastest and most difficult.[^ref-14][^ref-16]

### Car Setup and Simulation

Setting up the car is the heart of the game. *Computer Gaming World* listed the adjustable parts: shock absorbers, wheel stagger, camber, anti-roll bars, tyre pressure and rubber compound, plus the wings and the gearset. It noted that these settings "interact so thoroughly that one has to remain constantly aware of their potential for cancelling effects on one another".[^ref-6] In practice any setting can be changed out on the track. During qualifying and the race, only a few changes are possible, and only in the pits.[^ref-6] MobyGames notes that turbo boost "provides boost, but stresses the engine and uses more fuel".[^ref-1]

Kaemmer explained the approach in 1993: "I spent a great deal of time researching race car dynamics, and the model includes hundreds of variables that influence the car's handling, just as in real life."[^ref-7] The detailed manual covered the history of the race as well as car setup. *Computer Gaming World* noted that its gallery of photographs of past Indy winners "doubles as a clever anti-piracy device".[^ref-6] At start-up the game asks a question about a historical driver or car that the player answers from the manual.[^ref-14]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Autocross champion and racing steward Barry Werger reviewed the game for *Computer Gaming World* in May 1990 under the headline "A Driver's View Of Electronic Arts' Indianapolis 500". He called it "not only a hyper-realistic simulation but a valuable educational tool for any aspiring race car driver". He described the VGA graphics as "astounding", but said every sound from the PC's internal speaker was "annoying, at best, and unbearable at worst". An editor's note added that Ad Lib and Roland owners would not have that problem.[^ref-6] Werger warned that "this is a game for those who take auto racing seriously. Gamers looking for immediate gratification should stick to Out Run."[^ref-6]

European magazines gave it high marks. The Amiga Magazine Rack's review index lists these scores:[^ref-13]

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames lists a critic average of 84% from 22 reviews and a Moby Score of 7.6.[^ref-1] *Computer Gaming World* ranked the game 122nd in its November 1996 "150 Best (and 50 Worst) Games of All Time".[^ref-8] GameSpot's 2004 history of Papyrus, by Gord Goble, said it is "generally and justifiably regarded as the PC's first true auto racing simulation". Goble also noted that Geoff Crammond's *Revs* (1986) had already offered a semi-realistic driving model on the Commodore 64.[^ref-9]

Later retrospectives agree. Top Gear's Mike Channell wrote in 2021 that the game "could make a reasonable claim to being the first proper PC sim", though he compared its crowds to "the world's most painful magic eye puzzle".[^ref-15] The Highway Forever blog wrote in 2016 that "like all the best simulators, Indianapolis 500 balances accessibility and realism very well". The same post called the DOS engine sound "a swarm of angry, metallic hornets" and found the computer drivers "a bit clumsy".[^ref-16]

## Development

### Origins

According to Kaemmer, he and Omar Khudari founded Papyrus in August 1987, and he started work on the game in January 1988.[^ref-7] The pair pitched several ideas to Electronic Arts in San Francisco in 1987 and mentioned a possible Indy 500 game. "Unbeknownst to us, Rich Hilleman at EA was simultaneously looking for someone to do an Indy 500 game, and so the ball started rolling."[^ref-7] The *Boston Globe* gave a slightly different version in 1994: EA rejected a first concept and then signed Papyrus to make an Indianapolis 500 game for $70,000.[^ref-10]

Kaemmer told GameSpot that *Flight Simulator* was his model: "Just as Flight Simulator let you climb into a Cessna and see what it was like to be a private pilot, I wanted to make a program that would show someone, as much as possible, what it was like to drive a racecar."[^ref-9]

### Production

The sources give different development times. The *Boston Globe* reported 18 months for the PC and Amiga versions.[^ref-10] Kaemmer told *Computer Gaming World* in 1993 that it took "about two years for the Indy PC version plus another eight or nine months for the Amiga version".[^ref-7]

For the track, Kaemmer said, "The only research I did at the 'Brickyard' was visiting the museum and riding around the track in a bus." He also worked from "a lot of books, maps, and video footage". The game shows the speedway as it was in 1988, before the 1989 resurfacing and the VIP boxes added in 1990.[^ref-7]

MobyGames credits Kaemmer with lead design and programming, Richard Garcia with programming, Wilfredo J. Aguilar with graphics, Nancy L. Fong as art director, Rob Hubbard with music and sound programming, Richard Hilleman as producer, and Scott Cronce as technical director.[^ref-2] Kaemmer singled out Hubbard's Ad Lib and Roland drivers. He said Indy "was one of the first games, if not the first, that used those boards for sound effects". That is his own claim, and no other source here confirms it.[^ref-7]

### Technical Specifications

**MS-DOS Version:**[^ref-14]
- **CPU:** Intel 8088/8086 or better (DOS Days recommends a 386DX-25 for full detail)
- **Memory:** 384 KB (512 KB recommended)
- **Graphics:** Tandy/PCjr, CGA, composite CGA, EGA, VGA/MCGA (320×200, 256 colours)
- **Sound:** PC speaker, Tandy, Ad Lib, Roland MT-32
- **Media:** Two 5.25" 360 KB disks; 685 KB installed
- **Copy protection:** Manual look-up

**Amiga Version:**[^ref-4]
- Single floppy disk with manual-based copy protection
- Mouse, joystick or keyboard control
- Instant replays need a 512 KB RAM expansion

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

The sources disagree on how well the game sold. In 1993 Kaemmer told *Computer Gaming World* that "Indy 500 never sold as well as we and EA thought it would", and that for Papyrus "financially for us it's been about a break-even". He added that the game had helped the studio's visibility.[^ref-7] A year later the *Boston Globe* reported that it "sold more than 200,000 units, noted Kaemmer", and GameSpot later gave the same figure.[^ref-10][^ref-9]

The game also helped pay for Papyrus's next project. The *Globe* reported that Steven Dalton, an Indianapolis financial adviser and "a fan of their first game", funded most of *IndyCar Racing*'s $550,000 development cost.[^ref-10] GameSpot wrote that Indy 500 "was to form the nucleus for every Papyrus game to follow".[^ref-9] Papyrus followed it with *IndyCar Racing* (1993), *NASCAR Racing* (1994) and *IndyCar Racing II* (1995).[^ref-14] Sierra On-Line acquired the studio in late 1995.[^ref-5] Papyrus's technology later passed to iRacing, and Top Gear calls Papyrus the studio "that eventually morphed into simracing behemoth iRacing".[^ref-5][^ref-15]

### Collections

MobyGames lists the game in several compilations: *Air●Land●Sea* (1992), *Sports Collection* (1992), *Sports Masters* (1992), *Power Game II* (1993), *Electronic Arts Top Ten Pak* (1994), *Formula 500: The Ultimate Racing Five Pack* (1995) and *Top Ten Pak II* (1995).[^ref-1]
