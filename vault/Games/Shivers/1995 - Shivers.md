# Shivers

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Shivers is a first-person horror adventure game released by Sierra On-Line on September 30, 1995 (some sources give November 1995)[^ref-6][^ref-28][^ref-1]. A Myst-style puzzle adventure, it was "a surprising change from the typical Sierra blueprint"[^ref-19]. Set within the mysterious Professor Windlenot's Museum of the Strange and Unusual, players must survive a night trapped inside while capturing ten malevolent spirits called Ixupi using special ceremonial pots and talismans[^ref-2][^ref-3]. The game was Sierra's second attempt at the horror genre following Phantasmagoria, which had "met with mixed feelings by game critics", though Shivers took a distinctly different approach with its puzzle-focused, nonlinear gameplay[^ref-3].

"The superb production value in Shivers is testimonial to the talent of the artists involved," noted Philip Jong of Adventure Classic Gaming, highlighting the game's exceptional visual design[^ref-3]. The development team created over 2,500 hand-painted background shots using watercolors and gouache, which were then scanned into the game[^ref-3]. Despite receiving mixed critical reception upon release, Shivers achieved cult status and became one of the sleeper hits of 1995, praised particularly for its atmospheric presentation and original logic puzzles[^ref-3].

What distinguished Shivers from contemporaries like Myst and The 7th Guest was its randomization system—item locations and some puzzle states change between games, so that "every game is played differently and there is no fixed path through it"[^ref-5]. According to MobyGames, designer Marcia Bales said this random-play factor was influenced by Sierra's earlier Mixed-Up Mother Goose Deluxe[^ref-13]; Adventure Classic Gaming notes the "random play" concept was introduced "to enhance the replay value of Shivers"[^ref-3].

## Story Summary

The narrative begins with a group of teenagers daring the protagonist to spend the night inside Professor Windlenot's abandoned Museum of the Strange and Unusual, located in Mt. Pleasant, Ohio[^ref-7]. The museum was constructed by the eccentric archaeologist Professor Hubert Windlenot, who filled it with strange artifacts and elaborate puzzles before mysteriously dying before its planned 1980 opening[^ref-8]. "Was this place a failed dream or was it the waking nightmare of an eccentric old man?" asks the game's promotional material[^ref-9].

Fifteen years prior to the game's events, two teenagers named Beth Ann Nelson and Merrick Campbell broke into the museum and accidentally released evil spirits called Ixupi from an exhibited set of ancient ceramic vessels[^ref-1]. These malevolent entities from South American legend—their culture based partially on Egyptian mythology—drain the life essence from humans, and they killed both teenagers and Professor Windlenot himself[^ref-3][^ref-1]. Thirteen Ixupi had originally been sealed in their own vessels[^ref-8]; three of the thirteen escaped into the world, leaving ten in the museum[^ref-1]. The ghosts of Beth and Merrick now wander the museum, warning visitors with desperate pleas: "Get out—get out—They are evil!"[^ref-2]

Once locked inside by their friends with the ominous warning "If you die in the museum tonight, I'll make sure she gives the eulogy at your funeral," the player discovers the horrifying truth about the museum's past[^ref-2]. The Ixupi roam freely throughout the various exhibits, each spirit tied to a specific element—ash, wood, metal, crystal, wax, tar, sand, cloth, water, and lightning[^ref-11]. Players must piece together the mystery by exploring the museum, reading Windlenot's journals, and understanding the ancient curse that binds these spirits to the mortal realm.

The only hope for survival lies in reassembling the ceremonial vessels and capturing each Ixupi before they drain the protagonist's life essence completely. A "Flashback" feature lets players review cutscenes and re-read documents found along the way, such as the victims' notebooks, which expand the backstory and hint at puzzle solutions[^ref-1]. Windlenot himself had a reputation for interest in fringe topics such as ancient astronauts, Hollow Earth and Atlantis[^ref-1].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Shivers employs a first-person slideshow-style presentation similar to Myst, where players navigate through pre-rendered still images by clicking on movement hotspots[^ref-2]. The interface uses node-based movement with forward, left, and right directional options controlled via mouse cursor positioning[^ref-5]. Unlike many contemporary Sierra adventures, "Shivers is a Myst-like game" that eschews the traditional parser or icon-based interfaces for a purely point-and-click exploration system[^ref-12].

A unique feature for its time was the inclusion of closed captions for all video sequences, a suggestion by Lead Game Developer Willie Eide who came from a deaf family: "Having deaf individuals or hard-of-hearing individuals play our game is very important to me. So we really tried to make sure that the hard-of-hearing person, although he will miss the cool stereo ambient sounds that we have in each of the rooms, will at least be able to play the game and all the puzzles"[^ref-7]. Captioned dialogue was unusual at the time; Wikipedia describes it as "somewhat of a deviation from other FMV-based games of the era"[^ref-1].

### Structure and Progression

The game divides into two main sections: first finding the hidden entrance to the museum, then exploring its vast interior[^ref-2]. Unlike linear adventure games, Shivers offers a nonlinear design where players can explore most areas in any order, backtracking freely as needed[^ref-3].

The museum's rooms are divided into sections, each with a completely different look and each based on a historical legend[^ref-3]. Key locations include:

- **Main Hall:** Central hub connecting to most museum wings
- **Library:** Contains research materials and clues
- **Theater:** Features projection room puzzles
- **Clock Tower:** Home to complex timing puzzles
- **Strange Beasts Room:** Exhibits of unusual creatures
- **Mysteries of the Deep:** Ocean-themed displays with the Poseidon statue
- **Subterranean World:** Underground cave exhibits
- **Tombs and Curses:** Egyptian-themed displays
- **Shaman's Room:** Native American artifacts
- **Planetarium:** Astronomical exhibition
- **Man's Inhumanity to Man:** Torture device displays
- **Professor Windlenot's Bedroom and Workshop:** Personal quarters[^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay revolves around a scavenger hunt mechanic where players must locate ten ceramic pots and their corresponding lids (talismans) scattered throughout the museum[^ref-11]. A critical limitation forces players to carry only one inventory item at a time, requiring careful note-taking of item locations[^ref-25]. "You need to keep track of where you find items in the game, so that you can go back and collect them when needed"[^ref-15].

The game contains 27 logic puzzles of varying types[^ref-3]. According to Balmoral Software's walkthrough, about half of the puzzles have fixed solutions while the rest have randomized beginning or ending states[^ref-5]. Puzzle varieties include sliding tile puzzles, music-based challenges, pattern recognition, and mechanical contraptions. As one walkthrough notes: "Shivers isn't another Doom where you can sit down and play it with no brains whatsoever. Playing this game fully takes a lot of preperation and proactive thinking"[^ref-8].

Unlike pure puzzle games, Shivers incorporates a life meter that depletes when Ixupi attack the player[^ref-2]. While death is possible, it's generally easy to avoid by learning Ixupi locations and approaching dangerous areas cautiously. The game includes a point system: solved puzzles earn 6,750 points, inventory items 2,500 points, reading plaques 250 points, and life essence loss deducts 1,500 points[^ref-16].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

PC Gamer awarded Shivers 84%, with reviewer Steve Poole noting that "comparisons between it and earlier titles like Myst and The 7th Guest are inevitable. But fortunately for Sierra—and for puzzle fans—most of those comparisons will be quite favorable"[^ref-17]. However, he criticized the horror elements as ineffective: "it's just not scary. Sure, the museum is full creepy critters and horrifying devices, but any atmosphere of terror they create vanishes when you face the cartoon-like Ixupi—they're closer to Casper than to the essence of evil"[^ref-17].

PC Games Magazine's Hermann Peterscheck similarly praised the atmosphere in a February 1996 review, calling Shivers a game that "will send chills down the spine of any mystery/adventure game lover" while featuring "some of the best sound effects ever in an adventure game"[^ref-18]. He compared the tone as "more Scooby-Doo than Psycho," noting it was less graphic than Sierra's Phantasmagoria[^ref-18].

GameSpot gave the game 6.6/10, with Ron Dulin describing it as "a surprising change from the typical Sierra blueprint" and "more like Myst or The 7th Guest than Mystery House or King's Quest VII"[^ref-19]. However, he concluded it was "a game that has a look and feel guaranteed to get your attention, but may lack the depth of play to keep it for the long run"[^ref-19].

Next Generation was more critical, awarding 3/5 stars and citing "the long load times, grating music, and overreaching puzzles keep it mediocre at best"[^ref-1]. MacUser's Roman Loyola gave a negative assessment: "With a name like Shivers, you'd think I'd get really scared while playing this game—or at least get really cold. Actually, I got really bored"[^ref-12].

Quandary's Rosemary Young enthusiastically wrote: "Fun. Fun. Fun. I'm waiting for the next in the series. Shivers was truly a very pleasant surprise"[^ref-21]. Coming Soon Magazine (issue 12) gave an 89% rating, praising the graphics as "truly exceptional with a plethora of details that make the rooms stunningly realistic"[^ref-22].

### Modern Assessment

Adventure Gamers awarded 3.5 out of 5 stars in a 2002 review by Tom Arbour, noting "If you've never played this horror-themed Myst clone, it's worth the trip to the bargain bin"[^ref-2]. In a 2009 retrospective, French site Jeuxvideo.com rated it 17/20, calling it "une incontestable réussite" (an undeniable success) and "sans doute l'un des meilleurs jeux de sa génération" (without doubt one of the best games of its generation)[^ref-20].

Just Adventure's Ray Ivey gave a mixed C grade, acknowledging "This game has a lot going for it. Nice atmosphere, smooth gameplay, and I experienced no technical glitches at all" but ultimately finding "By the time I finished Shivers, I was ready to let an evil spirit get me just to put me out of my misery"[^ref-23].

## Development

### Origins

Shivers was inspired by designer Marcia Bales' personal interest in archaeology[^ref-3]. Bales' initial concept involved travelling the world to collect the spirits; the idea of placing the game in a set location came from [[Roberta Williams]], who served as creative consultant[^ref-7][^ref-1].

The Ixupi mythology drew inspiration from multiple cultural sources. "The Ixupi culture is based on Egyptian legends and beliefs," according to Adventure Classic Gaming, though the spirits themselves are presented as South American in origin[^ref-3]. The museum setting was grounded in authenticity, with Bales incorporating actual footage from a trip to Belize (around 1994, per the user guide's making-of chapter as summarized by Wikipedia[^ref-1]), and claiming that "90% of the facts one finds in the museum are factual"[^ref-3].

### Production

Development of Shivers presented significant challenges for the Sierra team. According to Wikipedia's summary of the user guide's making-of chapter, the team faced steep learning curves because many members were inexperienced in 3D modeling or adventure game design, and it was the first time the SCI language had been used for a first-person adventure game[^ref-1]. Roberta Williams served as creative consultant while simultaneously working on Phantasmagoria[^ref-1].

The art production was remarkably labor-intensive. Each room was assigned to one of a team of ten artists, with the art director ensuring continuity and consistency[^ref-1]; Ron Spears is credited as art director[^ref-27]. "All artworks are original paintings except for a few wire frame models. Most are painted traditionally with watercolors or gouache"[^ref-3]. Over 2,500 individual background shots were created, scanned, and compiled using 3D Studio for room construction[^ref-3]. Blue screen film sequences were shot using Ultimatte technology for the live-action video elements[^ref-3].

Sound designer and composer Guy Whitmore handled multiple audio roles that would typically be divided among several specialists in film production: "If you look at movie credits, you'd see sound designers, a composer, sound editors, an orchestrator, a sound coordinator, and foley artists. So far in the computer industry, at least where the industry is right now, all those roles are often rolled into one person"[^ref-1]. The soundtrack incorporated various atmospheric elements, including "mumblings from a Hamlet performance" for the abandoned theatre area[^ref-1].

### Technical Achievements

Shivers was developed using SCI-32, Sierra's fully 32-bit operating system language developed in-house[^ref-3]. The engine, also known as SCI2.1, represented a significant technical advancement for Sierra's adventure game lineup[^ref-28]. The game ran at 640x480 SVGA resolution with 256 colors[^ref-3].

The randomization system changes item locations and some puzzle states between games[^ref-5]; if the player dies, all the pots and talismans are randomly redistributed and the puzzles reset themselves[^ref-3]. Balmoral Software notes that as a result there is no fixed path through the game, "as you might have in a walkthrough of a more typical adventure game"[^ref-5].

Significant unused content has been documented by researchers at The Cutting Room Floor[^ref-29]:

- **Multiple draft versions** of Beth and Merrick's farewell note
- **Inaccessible clocktower surveillance view**
- **Unused Stone Ixupi animation** featuring a noticeably different art style
- **Demo leftovers:** screens and narration from a demo, including the line "Available at your local retailer, November '95"—TCRF notes the game was actually released in September instead of November

Additionally, Just Adventure's review noted that "Several extremely interesting elements in the museum never get used, including intriguing doors in the basement with skeleton hands and skulls for doorknobs"[^ref-23].

### Technical Issues

The original retail version's 16-bit setup executable does not function on 64-bit Windows operating systems[^ref-28]. Many users reported compatibility problems: "The program is incompatible with current operating systems and is apparently unusable" noted one Amazon reviewer[^ref-9]. Another lamented that "one of their very best games, Shivers, is incompatible with 64-bit Windows"[^ref-31].

Modern compatibility is achieved through ScummVM emulation, which has supported Shivers since version 2.0.0[^ref-32]. "This seems to work perfectly fine under ScummVM now if anyone is struggling with compatibility in 2022/2023"[^ref-26]. The GOG.com release packages the game with ScummVM for modern system compatibility[^ref-25].

Jeuxvideo.com's review noted "quelques erreurs dans le pointage des directions" (some errors in direction pointing) and called some puzzles "horriblement difficiles à résoudre" (horribly difficult to solve)[^ref-20].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Shivers contains numerous hidden interactions and Easter eggs documented by the community[^ref-33]:

- **Lightbulb Easter Egg:** Click twice on the lightbulb after solving the Stonehenge puzzle
- **Poseidon Statue:** Click several times on the statue's head in the Mysteries of the Deep room
- **Stone Sliding:** Slide stones in the door puzzle without actually solving it
- **Subterranean World Statue:** Click 5-6 times on the statue's head after reading the plaque
- **Shaman Drum:** Pound the drums in a "Ram-Bo-Ram-Bo-Ram-Bo" pattern, then hit the statue with the mallet
- **Crate Easter Egg:** Open and close the crate with the ax in Incredible Inventions room
- **Caged Skeleton:** Click the pitcher, then the glass, then the skeleton's hand to hear "I feel your pain"[^ref-16]
- **Gallows:** Click the hanging man's nipple four times
- **Guillotine:** After chopping the head, click the executioner statue's head twice

Cross-Sierra references include elevator music that later appeared in Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh, and the jukebox containing songs from Mixed-Up Mother Goose deluxe version[^ref-34]. One user noted: "This game is so full of Easter Eggs, I was surprised not to find it here. The only way to find out about them is to either find them by accident, or ask someone at the Mystery Games forum at Sierra.com"[^ref-33].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Despite mixed critical reception, Shivers became what Adventure Classic Gaming called "one of the sleeper hits in 1995" and eventually attaining "cult status"[^ref-3]. The game carried an ESRB Teen rating[^ref-6].

GOG.com's blog retrospective noted: "The spooky first-person adventure game Shivers (1995) was one of the titles that showed that Sierra's creators had matured and were pursuing more edgy topics"[^ref-35]. User reviews demonstrate lasting affection: "The only 10 rating I have given in 20 years. Outstanding puzzles, ambiance and story"[^ref-36]. Another recalled: "It's soo scary that I didn't play it for 11 years. but finally I pluck the curace [sic]"[^ref-36].

### Collections

Shivers has been re-released digitally through GOG.com since June 21, 2018, priced at $5.99 with occasional discounts of 20-30%[^ref-30]. The digital release includes ScummVM compatibility for modern Windows systems, cloud saves, and single-player support[^ref-30].

German language patches have been created by fan communities for both Shivers and its sequel, restoring German text menus while keeping English voice acting (which was never localized)[^ref-37].

### Fan Projects

An active speedrunning community maintains records at Speedrun.com, with categories including "FIR NSS," "FIR SS," "First To Five," "Room Shuffle," "100%," and various randomizer modes[^ref-38]. The current world record is held by Jaffra at 24 minutes 26 seconds[^ref-38]. The community has 23 followers, 167 total runs, and 17 total players[^ref-38].

### Related Publications

- **User Manual:** Written by Vanessa Raymond, included with game[^ref-27]
- **USA Version Bonus:** The USA retail version includes a thicker book with a walkthrough chapter that the international version lacks[^ref-16]

### Critical Perspective

Shivers was a Myst-style first-person puzzle adventure from a company better known for its third-person adventures; GameSpot's Ron Dulin found it "more like Myst or The 7th Guest than Mystery House or King's Quest VII"[^ref-19]. Adventure Classic Gaming concluded: "While the unique nonlinear design of Shivers may not appeal to all adventure gamers, the authentic look of the game and original logic puzzles will win many fans over"[^ref-3].

The closed captioning stemmed from lead developer Willie Eide's own deaf family[^ref-7]. On the horror elements, PC Gamer's Steve Poole found the Ixupi "closer to Casper than to the essence of evil," and PC Games Magazine called the tone "more Scooby-Doo than Psycho"[^ref-17][^ref-18].

Players have remained fond of it—it holds an 8.4/10 user rating on IMDb and a 4.7/5 user rating on GOG.com[^ref-24][^ref-25]—though Just Adventure's Ray Ivey dismissed it as "a protracted scavenger hunt punctuated with irrelevant puzzles"[^ref-23].
