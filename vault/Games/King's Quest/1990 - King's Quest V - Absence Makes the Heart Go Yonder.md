# King's Quest V: Absence Makes the Heart Go Yonder!

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 16, 2026</small>

## Overview

King's Quest V: Absence Makes the Heart Go Yonder! marks a pivotal turning point in Sierra On-Line's gaming philosophy, representing one of the most significant technological leaps in adventure game history.[^ref-1] Released in November 1990, it was Sierra's first icon-based, point-and-click adventure game, abandoning the text parser that had defined the series since its inception.[^ref-1][^ref-17] It was also the first King's Quest with 256-color VGA graphics and the first adventure game on Sierra's SCI1 interpreter.[^ref-1][^ref-6] A fully voiced CD-ROM edition followed in August 1991, the first of Sierra's full-fledged adventure games to ship on CD;[^ref-15] Adventure Classic Gaming goes further and calls it the first adventure game released on CD-ROM in Multimedia PC format.[^ref-1]

The Digital Antiquarian calls it "the most dramatic single visual leap forward" in Sierra's history.[^ref-15] It was made on a budget of around one million dollars,[^ref-6] and Adventure Classic Gaming describes it as the first title to cost over $1 million to produce.[^ref-1] Its backgrounds were painted by hand on paper and then digitized,[^ref-15] and Computer Gaming World's Scorpia called the game "a graphic tour-de-force."[^ref-5] Computer Gaming World noted that "Roberta Williams horrified Sierra traditionalists by getting the parser out of the way of some of the most beautiful graphics ever."[^ref-6]

King's Quest V became a commercial phenomenon, certified Platinum (at least 250,000 audited units) by the Software Publishers Association by February 1993;[^ref-58] Sierra claimed it became "the fastest-selling single computer game in the history of the industry."[^ref-15] The game won the Software Publishers Association's Excellence in Software Award for Best Fantasy Role-Playing/Adventure Program in 1991,[^ref-57][^ref-6] along with Computer Gaming World's Adventure Game of the Year award.[^ref-6] Despite its commercial success and technical achievements, modern retrospectives have been more critical, with reviewers noting that while revolutionary for its era, certain design decisions have aged poorly.[^ref-8]

## Story Summary

King Graham is out walking in the woods near his home when the birds fall silent and a sudden storm rolls in. By the time he reaches the rise overlooking Daventry, the castle, with Queen Valanice, Prince Alexander and Princess Rosella inside, is simply gone.[^ref-1] A talking owl named Cedric saw it happen and names the culprit: the evil wizard Mordack.[^ref-1][^ref-6] Cedric takes Graham to the land of Serenia to meet his master, the good wizard Crispin, who gives Graham his old wand and the power to talk with animals, and sends Cedric along to help.[^ref-10][^ref-6]

Graham first works his way around Serenia's town, enchanted forest and desert, helping its inhabitants and gathering what he will need.[^ref-8] Once he leaves, the journey turns linear: over snowy mountains and across the water, past a yeti, harpies and a giant two-headed roc, to Mordack's island fortress.[^ref-6][^ref-8]

A fortune teller's vision explains the motive. Mordack is the brother of Manannan, the sorcerer Prince Alexander turned into a cat in King's Quest III. Only the caster can undo that spell, so Mordack has taken the royal family to force Alexander to reverse it, threatening to feed them to the transformed Manannan.[^ref-6] Inside the castle Graham is helped by Cassima, a captive princess from the Land of the Green Isles. He recharges Crispin's wand, defeats Mordack in a magical duel, and Crispin restores the family and sends everyone home; Alexander, smitten with Cassima, sets the stage for King's Quest VI.[^ref-6][^ref-26]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

King's Quest V introduced a completely new icon-driven interface that requires no typing on the keyboard, representing Sierra's first icon-based adventure game.[^ref-1] A toolbar at the top of the screen offers four actions (walk, look, action and talk) along with the inventory and game options.[^ref-1] This marked a dramatic departure from the text parser interface used in all previous King's Quest games and fundamentally changed how players experienced Sierra adventures.[^ref-1]

The interface was designed to be more accessible to a broader audience, fitting Sierra's marketing push to "bring the family" into the world of King's Quest.[^ref-15] Players interact with the environment by selecting appropriate icons and clicking on objects, characters, or locations within the scenes. An inventory holds the items Graham collects, which are central to solving the game's puzzles.[^ref-1]

### Structure and Progression

The game follows a semi-linear structure as Graham travels through different regions on his quest to reach Mordack's castle:

- **Serenia Forest and Town:** The starting area, with a town center and an enchanted forest whose inhabitants all need aid of some kind.[^ref-8]
- **The Dark Forest:** A dangerous woodland area featuring encounters with a witch, gnomes, and other fairy tale creatures.
- **The Desert:** A challenging desert map that received significant criticism for its difficulty in navigation.[^ref-17]
- **The Mountain Pass:** Snowy peaks featuring encounters with an Ice Queen, a Yeti, and a memorable sled ride sequence.[^ref-18]
- **The Sea and Harpie Island:** Graham's ocean voyage and encounters with mythological creatures.[^ref-18]
- **Mordack's Castle:** The final dungeon, including a maze, where Mordack can randomly appear and kill Graham, and where Graham must rescue his family and defeat the evil wizard.[^ref-8]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The game features inventory-based puzzles typical of Sierra adventures, requiring players to collect items and use them in appropriate situations.[^ref-1] A scoring system tracks player progress, with a maximum of 260 points available throughout the game.[^ref-19] However, the game has been criticized for containing numerous dead-ends—situations where players can progress past a point of no return without necessary items, making the game unwinnable without reloading an earlier save.[^ref-1]

Players must frequently perform good deeds that are rewarded later in the game, embodying the fairy tale morality that defined the King's Quest series.[^ref-8] Its dead ends are more relentless than in earlier Sierra games, so frequent saving is essential.[^ref-8] Some puzzle solutions have been criticized as illogical or overly obtuse, such as having only seconds to throw a boot at a cat to save a rat, an act that matters much later.[^ref-8]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

King's Quest V received widespread critical acclaim upon release. Scorpia of Computer Gaming World praised the game's "graphic tour-de-force" visuals, while the magazine's editors named it the 1991 Adventure Game of the Year.[^ref-5][^ref-6] Dragon magazine awarded the game 4 out of 5 stars in 1991.[^ref-6] It won the Software Publishers Association's 1991 Excellence in Software Award for Best Fantasy Role-Playing/Adventure Program, beating nominees including The Secret of Monkey Island and Ultima VI: The False Prophet.[^ref-57]

Critics praised the game's VGA graphics, audio and user interface.[^ref-17] However, the CD-ROM version's voice acting received more mixed reactions, with Charles Ardai of Computer Gaming World, reviewing the Space Quest IV CD-ROM in April 1993, recalling "the somewhat wooden line readings for which Sierra has become known as a result of the CD-ROM edition King's Quest V."[^ref-60] The desert navigation section was singled out for criticism by multiple reviewers.[^ref-17] MPC World readers voted it the Best Multimedia Fantasy/Adventure Game in 1992.[^ref-6]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been more critical of the game's design choices. Adventure Gamers rated the game 3 out of 5 stars,[^ref-22] with Emily Morganti's 2007 review calling it "a step forward in graphics and interface; a step backward in story and character development."[^ref-59] The review recommended that players who are not King's Quest fans "skip ahead to KQVI, which is so much better."[^ref-59]

Hardcore Gaming 101 noted that "despite its popularity at the time, [the game] has aged quite poorly."[^ref-8] GameCola's Nathaniel Hoover gave it a 5 (Average) rating, criticizing that "the gameplay ruins the game, but it's not even that the puzzles themselves are bad—it's the misleading or altogether absent communication about what is actually a puzzle."[^ref-23] The Adventurers' Guild blog rated the game 56/100 in their retrospective playthrough.[^ref-24]

## Development

### Origins

With King's Quest V, Sierra sought to push the boundaries of what was possible in adventure gaming. [[Roberta Williams]], who had created the original King's Quest in 1984, returned as designer and director.[^ref-6][^ref-10] The game marked a return to the land of Serenia, first seen in Sierra's earlier title Wizard and the Princess.[^ref-26]

Sierra's creative director Bill Davis introduced new storyboarding methodologies borrowed from the film industry to manage the ambitious project. "We are going to take some of the techniques that have been used in the film industry to manage gigantic feature projects and apply them here," Davis explained.[^ref-15]

### Production

The game represented Sierra's most ambitious production to date, with a budget of around one million dollars.[^ref-6] The art direction was revolutionary: background scenes were painted by hand on paper and then digitized, giving the game a distinctive painterly look.[^ref-15][^ref-6] Character animations employed rotoscoping techniques inspired by Disney animation from The Little Mermaid, using live actors as the framework for character sprites.[^ref-6]

The game was built on the new SCI1 interpreter.[^ref-1] [[Corey Cole]], credited under Development System, later explained that SCI in general "included a scripting language, graphic and sound features, the parser, ways to animate characters, and so on," and that his own earlier work converting SCI to the Atari ST became the core of the Amiga and Macintosh versions.[^ref-28]

### Technical Achievements

King's Quest V brought 256-color VGA graphics at 320x200 to the series via the new SCI1 interpreter.[^ref-1] The Digital Antiquarian calls it "the most dramatic single visual leap forward" Sierra had made.[^ref-15] The CD-ROM version shipped in August 1991 (some sources give 1992),[^ref-15][^ref-10] and added full voice acting;[^ref-15] Ken Williams coined the term "talkies" for such CD re-releases.[^ref-15]

The game's audio system supported multiple sound cards including Game Blaster, Ad Lib, Sound Blaster, and Roland MT-32.[^ref-10] The soundtrack was MIDI-based, composed by Mark Seibert and Ken Allen, and KHInsider's rip of the soundtrack runs to 42 tracks.[^ref-18] The huge increase in storage capacity offered by CD-ROM allowed for both digital sound effects and speech files.

### Cut Content

Several planned versions of the game were cancelled during development. A Sega CD port was announced but never released.[^ref-6] An Atari ST version was announced for Spring 1991 but was ultimately cancelled.[^ref-6] Some unused dialogue and background scenes exist within the game's data files, including hidden unused game over icons.[^ref-32]

### Technical Issues

Modern players using the Steam version may encounter save game issues, receiving the message "This directory/disk can hold no more saved games."[^ref-37] Solutions include installing community patches from The Sierra Help Pages.[^ref-37]

Common compatibility issues include:
- "Unable to initialize your audio hardware" error on modern systems[^ref-38]
- "Out of heap" errors requiring specific save game workarounds[^ref-38]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Game Title Pun:** The subtitle "Absence Makes the Heart Go Yonder" is a play on the proverb "Absence makes the heart grow fonder."[^ref-25]
- **Sled/Cape Easter Egg:** In the floppy disk version, after Graham crashes the sled in the snowy mountains, using the cape on the broken sled pile causes a sea creature to mysteriously appear—this was removed from the CD-ROM version.[^ref-39]
- **Contest Winners:** Sierra ran a contest where winners became characters in the game. Amanda Logue and Austin Hartman appear as in-game characters.[^ref-25]
- **Real-World Architecture:** The desert temple exterior is based on "Al Khazneh" in Petra, Jordan.[^ref-25]
- **Sierra Logo Mountain:** A mountain in the game is designed to resemble the Sierra On-Line logo.[^ref-25]
- **Cedric References:** The owl Cedric became so notorious that he appeared as the brunt of jokes in later Sierra games including Freddy Pharkas: Frontier Pharmacist, Quest for Glory: Shadows of Darkness, and Space Quest VI.[^ref-26]
- **Unused Dialogue:** Files contain one unused line where King Graham says "I'll take care of you, Cedric."[^ref-32]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

According to Jimmy Maher of The Digital Antiquarian, King's Quest V sold 160,000 copies in its first three months and more than 300,000 copies within fifteen months of release.[^ref-15] By February 1993 it had been certified Platinum by the Software Publishers Association, a designation requiring audited sales of at least 250,000 units.[^ref-58] By November 2000, US sales alone were estimated at 300,000-400,000 units.[^ref-6]

Sierra claimed it was the fastest-selling single computer game in the industry's history.[^ref-15]

### Collections

King's Quest V has been included in numerous compilation releases:
- **King's Quest Collection Series (1997):** Included with games I-VII[^ref-26]
- **King's Quest Collection Series (1999):** Re-released compilation[^ref-22]
- **King's Quest Collection (2006):** Modern Windows compatible release[^ref-22]
- **GOG.com King's Quest 4+5+6:** Digital distribution bundle[^ref-43]
- **Steam King's Quest Collection:** Digital distribution[^ref-37]

### Fan Projects

The game has inspired various fan efforts over the years. Fan community member MusicallyInspired created an AGI-style version of King's Quest V as an artistic project, though not as a full playable game.[^ref-45]

### Amiga Port & SCP Enhancement

King's Quest V's Amiga conversion differed from the DOS version mainly in its color palette.[^ref-8]

In January 2026, kikems (AmigaWave) and Darasco of the [[2025 - SCP Sierra Conversion Project|Sierra Conversion Project (SCP)]] group showed an Amiga OCS remaster that reconstructs the palette and graphics to bring them closer to the PC version.[^ref-54] The music is the original Amiga version's, with MIDI support via Roland MT-32 (demonstrated on an mt32-pi).[^ref-54]

### Related Publications

- **King's Quest V Manual:** Official game documentation written by Bridget McKenna, included with original release[^ref-48]
- **Sierra Hint Book:** Official hint guide available for players needing puzzle assistance
- **Nintendo Power Strategy Guide:** NES-specific walkthrough published in August 1993 issue[^ref-50]

### Critical Perspective

King's Quest V occupies a fascinating and somewhat contradictory place in adventure game history. On one hand, it represents a genuine technological milestone—the game's 256-color VGA graphics, point-and-click interface, and CD-ROM voice acting were genuinely revolutionary and helped define what adventure games could become.[^ref-1] Adventure Classic Gaming's Eivind Hagerup wrote that it "marks a pivotal turning point in Sierra On-Line's gaming philosophy which has forever changed the face of adventure game design."[^ref-1]

On the other hand, the game also exemplifies design philosophies that would eventually contribute to the adventure genre's decline. The numerous dead-ends, illogical puzzle solutions, and frequent deaths frustrated players; Hardcore Gaming 101 called it "filled to the brim with impossibly frustrating dead ends and illogical puzzles."[^ref-8] Adventure Classic Gaming notes that some gamers felt the icon interface cheapened the puzzles, though the interface went on to be used across Sierra's later series.[^ref-1] The game stands as both a monument to technological progress and a cautionary tale about game design, its legacy defined as much by what it achieved as by what its successors would improve upon.
