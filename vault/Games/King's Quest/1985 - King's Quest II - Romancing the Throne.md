# King's Quest II: Romancing the Throne

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

King's Quest II: Romancing the Throne is the second installment in the King's Quest series, released in 1985 for IBM PC and PCjr—in May according to Wikipedia, while MobyGames dates the PC booter release to June 1985[^ref-2][^ref-1]. Designed by [[Roberta Williams]], it uses the AGI (Adventure Game Interpreter) engine developed for the original King's Quest[^ref-1][^ref-3]. The subtitle parodies the 1984 Robert Zemeckis film Romancing the Stone[^ref-4][^ref-5]. Computer Gaming World's "Taking a Peek" column (June–July 1985) said that "the animated graphics are quite simply the best we have ever seen on a home computer"[^ref-41], while Sierra's packaging quoted Compute! magazine calling it "like playing an animated cartoon"[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

One year after recovering the three stolen treasures and becoming King of Daventry, Graham is urged by the vision of his predecessor, King Edward, to marry and provide an heir[^ref-2][^ref-40]. Looking into the magic mirror, he sees Valanice, a maiden from the kingdom of Kolyma, whom the jealous crone Hagatha has whisked away to an enchanted land and imprisoned in a quartz tower[^ref-40]. The mirror sends Graham to Kolyma, where he must find the three golden keys that unlock the three doors to the enchanted land where Valanice is held[^ref-22][^ref-40].

The land of Kolyma draws from folklore, mythology, and classic literature[^ref-4]. Graham encounters King Neptune in an underwater kingdom, Little Red Riding Hood and her ailing grandmother, and Count Dracula in a haunted castle[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-35][^ref-45]. A genie's lamp grants three predetermined wishes: a bridle, a sword, and a magic carpet[^ref-4][^ref-35]. After overcoming these challenges, Graham rescues Valanice and they marry[^ref-2].

## Gameplay

King's Quest II shares the same engine and visual style as its predecessor[^ref-4]. Like Daventry in the first game, Kolyma is a cyclical world where traveling north or south wraps around[^ref-1]. However, unlike King's Quest I where tasks could be completed in any order, this sequel requires finding each key in sequence, creating more linear progression[^ref-4][^ref-10].

### Interface and Controls

Players move Graham using keyboard arrow keys and perform actions by typing verb-noun commands such as "open door" or "take lamp"[^ref-1][^ref-10]. King's Quest I already offered Slow, Normal and Fast walking speeds; King's Quest II added a fourth, faster setting[^ref-36].

### Structure and Progression

The game features goal-sensitive events where the world changes as the story progresses[^ref-10]. The antique shop remains closed until a specific action is performed, and characters appear only when the story is ready[^ref-10]. Adventure Gamers saw these triggered events as showing "an evolution from KQI's open-ended treasure hunt to the more linear, story-driven model still widely used in adventure games today"[^ref-10].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

- Puzzles typically involve trading items or using them to bypass obstacles[^ref-1]
- Knowledge of fairy tales helps solve certain puzzles[^ref-1]
- Complex solutions earn more points than brute force approaches[^ref-1]
- **Maximum score: 185 points**[^ref-21]
- Some points are mutually exclusive—putting the bridle on the snake earns 5 points and talking to the freed horse 2 more (plus a sugar cube worth 1 point when eaten), while simply killing the snake earns fewer points[^ref-21][^ref-38]

### The Bridge Trap

The game's most notorious design element is a rickety bridge leading to the magical doors. The bridge can only be crossed seven times—just enough to complete the game, with a point awarded for each crossing[^ref-21][^ref-4]. There is no warning that the bridge will collapse[^ref-4][^ref-10]. Hardcore Gaming 101 called this "the most remarkably cruel aspect of King's Quest II," noting "it never clues you in, but you can only cross this bridge a very, very limited amount of times"[^ref-4]. Adventure Gamers described it as "a cruel setup that more or less ensures that new players will have to start over near the beginning of the game at some point"[^ref-10]. The bridge trap was later referenced in Sierra's Freddy Pharkas: Frontier Pharmacist[^ref-4].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

King's Quest II received enthusiastic reviews upon release. Computer Entertainer's December 1985 review said it had "all the magic of a favorite fairy tale" and called it "not to be missed"[^ref-43]. In Antic (May 1986), Brad Kershaw wrote: "If all adventures could be this enjoyable and visually stimulating, I would have been an adventure game freak long ago"[^ref-44]. Keith Campbell of Computer and Video Games (August 1986) agreed with Sierra's claim that it represented "a totally new approach to computer adventuring"—"Well, almost," he added, citing a similar technique in Melbourne House's Zim Sala Bim[^ref-45]. Aktueller Software Markt (ASM, July 1986) reviewed the Atari ST version, scoring it 10 for graphics, 10 for vocabulary, 9 for story and 10 for atmosphere[^ref-42]; MobyGames records this as 9.75/10[^ref-39]. Sierra's packaging quoted Consumer Software News ("if you liked King's Quest you'll love the sequel"), Computer Entertainment ("Roberta Williams has simply outdone herself") and QuestBusters ("the most lushly painted and highly detailed scenery seen since...well King's Quest I")[^ref-7].

**Contemporary Scores:**
- **ST Magazine (Atari ST, issue 3):** 4/5 (Intérêt ****; Graphisme ***, Bruitage **)[^ref-47][^ref-39]
- **Computer + Video Games (Atari ST, Aug 1986):** 6/10 (average of four category scores: Vocab 7, Atmosphere 7, Personal 5, Value 5)[^ref-45][^ref-14]
- **ASM (Atari ST, Jul 1986):** 9.75/10 (average of four category scores)[^ref-42][^ref-39]
- **Tilt (Atari ST, Jun 1987):** 16/20[^ref-39]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives acknowledge the game's historical importance while noting dated mechanics. Adventure Gamers rated it 3/5 ("Decent"), praising its nostalgic appeal but criticizing the bridge trap[^ref-10]. Hardcore Gaming 101 assessed it as "more of the same" that "does little to improve the formula, but doesn't really screw it up much either"[^ref-4]. The Adventure Gamer blog scored it 53 points on the PISSED rating scale[^ref-6].

**Modern Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 7.1/10 MobyScore, 78% critics (14 ratings), 3.5/5 players (109 ratings), as of 2026[^ref-1]
- **GOG:** 4.1/5 (42 reviews, KQ 1+2+3 bundle)[^ref-26]
- **Steam:** 87% Very Positive (331 reviews, KQ Collection)[^ref-27]
- **IMDB:** 7.2/10 (140+ votes)[^ref-15]
- **Adventure Gamers:** 3/5[^ref-10]
- **Techtite (Apple II, 2000):** 5/5[^ref-39]
- **Choicest Games:** 4/10 ("simply too damn difficult")[^ref-17]

## Development

### Origins

Development of King's Quest II served as a training ground for Sierra employees new to the AGI technology[^ref-3]. [[Scott Murphy]] and [[Mark Crowe]], who would later create the Space Quest series, joined the team along with [[Al Lowe]], future creator of Leisure Suit Larry[^ref-3][^ref-17]. As DOS Days documented: "These people included Scott Murphy, Mark Crowe and Al Lowe, who would later become famous adventure game creators themselves"[^ref-3].

Roberta Williams explained that ideas cut from the original game found their home in the sequel: "I couldn't fit some ideas into King's Quest 1, so I was happy to get a chance to include King Neptune, Dracula, everyone from Little Red Riding Hood, and that infamous rickety old bridge you could only cross so many times"[^ref-18].

### Production

**Development Credits (MobyGames, 1987 DOS release):**[^ref-1]
- **Design/Writing:** [[Roberta Williams]]
- **Story:** Annette Childs (manual backstory)[^ref-40]
- **Game logic:** Ken Williams, Sol Ackerman, Chris Iden, [[Scott Murphy]], Dale Carlson[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- **Development system:** Jeff Stephenson, Chris Iden, Robert Eric Heitman[^ref-1]
- **Graphics:** Doug MacNeill, [[Mark Crowe]][^ref-1]
- **Music:** Al Lowe[^ref-1][^ref-3]

The manual, credited "Story by Annette Childs", contains a prologue in which a vision of King Edward advises Graham to marry and provide an heir[^ref-40]. Al Lowe also wrote the official hint book[^ref-35].

### Technical Specifications

- **CPU:** 8088 or 8086 compatible[^ref-3][^ref-7]
- **RAM:** 256KB (DOS version); 128KB (booter version)[^ref-3][^ref-11]
- **DOS:** Version 2.11 or later[^ref-3]
- **Graphics:** Hercules, CGA, EGA, Tandy supported[^ref-3]; Sierra Help also lists VGA[^ref-30]
- **Resolution:** 320×200 pixels in up to 16 colors (EGA mode)[^ref-3]
- **Audio:** PC speaker or Tandy 3-voice sound[^ref-3]
- **Installed Size:** 588 KB[^ref-3]

The booter version plays music at a slower tempo and louder volume compared to the DOS conversion[^ref-11].

### Technical Achievements

- **Opening cutscene:** The minimal story is told mostly through the manual and an introductory cutscene[^ref-2]
- **Goal-sensitive events:** The game world dynamically changes as players advance—new characters appear, areas open and close based on story progression[^ref-10]
- **Linear story structure:** Showed an evolution toward "the more linear, story-driven model still widely used in adventure games today"[^ref-10]

### Music and Audio

Al Lowe composed and arranged the soundtrack—VGMPF lists fourteen pieces, several of them arrangements of public-domain works such as a Tchaikovsky piece (the "Kiss" cue, which VGMPF links to *Romeo and Juliet*), Bach, Chopin, Mendelssohn and Wagner, plus a Space Quest trailer theme by Mark Crowe and Tommy Shaw[^ref-20]. DOS Days noted that "KQ2 has over twice the number of songs as KQ1" while reusing some tracks including the Title, Danger, and Dead themes[^ref-3]. The opening screen features "Greensleeves" on the PC speaker[^ref-10]. When Graham encounters the ghosts, the game plays an unlicensed arrangement of Rod Temperton's "Thriller", and the Batmobile gag uses Neal Hefti's Batman theme[^ref-20].

The Mac version includes a backdoor: when prompted for the master disk, holding Command and typing "4givmeken" (asking forgiveness from Ken Williams) bypasses the check[^ref-22].

## Legacy

In February 1987, Computer Entertainer reported that the first two King's Quest games had each earned Software Publishers Association gold awards for certified sales of over 100,000 units[^ref-46], and by mid-1987 Sierra's packaging advertised the first three games as "Over 500,000 Sold"[^ref-2]. The game served as a training ground for future Sierra legends—Scott Murphy and Mark Crowe (Space Quest) and Al Lowe (Leisure Suit Larry) all worked on the project[^ref-3][^ref-17].

### Remakes

**Unmade Sierra Remake:** Accounts differ on whether Sierra ever planned to remake King's Quest II after its 1990 SCI remake of King's Quest I. Sierra Chest states that in 1990 Sierra planned to redevelop the game, but scrapped the plan because of the "rather disappointing sales" of the King's Quest I remake[^ref-19]. AGD Interactive's history of its own remake says instead that, "due to the commercial failure of Sierra On-Line's official SCI remake of King's Quest I, no plans were ever made to upgrade King's Quest II"[^ref-23]. The King's Quest Omnipedia cites Donald B. Trivette's 1993 *Official Book of King's Quest* (third edition) as saying plans were made to re-do II and III, "but at this writing that has not been done"[^ref-48].

**AGD Interactive VGA Remake (2002):** AGD Interactive (formerly Tierra Entertainment) released "King's Quest II: Romancing the Stones," an unofficial VGA remake that AGDI reported had been downloaded over 451,400 times[^ref-23]. Features include a point-and-click interface, full voice acting with Josh Mandel reprising King Graham[^ref-2][^ref-49], the Adventure Game Studio engine[^ref-50], a substantially rewritten plot with new characters, and the infamous bridge no longer collapsing[^ref-2][^ref-4][^ref-23]. Hardcore Gaming 101 declared the remake "far exceeds any of the work Sierra themselves put into any of their VGA remakes"[^ref-4].

**2015 Reboot:** The third chapter of the 2015 King's Quest reboot, titled "Once Upon a Climb" (April 2016), incorporates elements of the original game[^ref-25][^ref-2].

### The King's Quest Companion

*The King's Quest Companion* by Peter Spear retells the games as narrative accounts framed as being sent from Daventry through a device called the "Eye Between the Worlds", chiefly by the scribe Derek Karlavaegen[^ref-12][^ref-37]. Its first two editions included item and point lists in a chapter called "The Final Score"[^ref-37]. Roberta Williams endorsed the book, calling it "an interesting blend of fiction and helpful information" that anyone wanting the story behind King's Quest "will find ... invaluable"[^ref-37].

### Easter Eggs and Unused Content

**Easter Eggs:**
- **Batmobile:** The 1960s Batmobile occasionally drives out of Hagatha's cave, accompanied by a version of the Batman theme[^ref-1][^ref-15][^ref-20][^ref-21][^ref-35]
- **Space Quest Demo:** Looking in a hole in the cliff area plays a promotional demo for Space Quest, whose designers (Murphy and Crowe) worked on this game[^ref-1]
- **Hidden Sign:** A note behind a tree near the screen where Neptune's trident is found describes Space Quest I and King's Quest III; it appears in the 1987 re-release[^ref-21][^ref-38]
- **Personal Death Message:** When the player dies, a personal message from Roberta and Ken Williams displays[^ref-10]

**Trivia:**
- **Kolyma's Name:** Kolyma shares its name with a river region in north-eastern Siberia infamous for Soviet gulag camps[^ref-1]

**Unused Content (The Cutting Room Floor):**[^ref-22]
- An alternate, larger genie graphic—the final game uses a smaller genie attached to Graham's sprite
- Three different gold key designs, suggesting each door may have originally required a distinct key
- A "Dead Fish" item (Object ID 84) with the description: "The lovely fish is dead. The golden scales are already dulling"
- Unused text including a rejected risqué description of Valanice
- Debug commands activated by pressing ALT+D or typing "FAST TALK"
- Unused sounds: happy melody, digging, tide/waves, striking effects, and an early slower Batman theme
