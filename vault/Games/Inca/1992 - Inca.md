# Inca

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Inca is a science fiction adventure game that combines several gameplay styles in one package. It was developed by French studio Coktel Vision and released in 1992[^ref-1], published in North America by Sierra On-Line[^ref-2], and it presents an anachronistic blend of Incan mythology and space opera. The player takes on the role of El Dorado, a time-traveling space pilot charged with recovering the treasure of the Incas[^ref-3] and fulfilling a prophecy to restore the Inca Empire[^ref-6].

The Collection Chamber described its "schizophrenic conception", mixing adventure gameplay with space combat and first-person exploration[^ref-4]. As ClassicReload puts it, "Inca has been famous for combining many different genres to form a certain story-line"[^ref-5].

The game's premise starts from a historical question posed in its manual: "How can it be that only a handful of ill equipped conquistadors, some 170-200 men, were able to defeat a well trained army of tens of thousands of soldiers?"[^ref-6] The game's answer is an alternate history in which, as Hardcore Gaming 101 summarises, "the Incans also had access to space crafts, and routinely ventured into the sky to do battle with the magnificent floating galleons of the Spanish Armada"[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

The game's narrative centers on the prophecy of Huayna Capac, the last great Inca, who foretells the coming of El Dorado, "a mythical and legendary character chosen to restore the Inca Empire"[^ref-10]. The player controls this chosen warrior on a mission through time and space, as mysterious powers, "dormant for 500 years, wait to be unleashed"[^ref-2]. The quest involves traveling to South America at the time of the European invasion to find the treasure of the Inca civilization[^ref-12].

The player's goal is to find the three jewels of Time, Matter, and Energy[^ref-6] while piloting a spacecraft called the Tumi, a word meaning "knife"[^ref-7]. As the game manual explains: "You are the chosen one, it is up to you to fulfill the prophecy. The spirit of Huayna Capac will guide you on your quest as you attempt to rekindle Inti, the sun and bring about the rebirth of the mighty Incan empire"[^ref-6]. The antagonist is Aguirre, who in Computer Gaming World's words symbolizes "the Spanish conquistadors, and constantly in search of wealth and material power"[^ref-34].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Inca is played mainly with the mouse; the manual advises using a boot disk if free memory is below 558,680 bytes[^ref-6]. Computer Gaming World noted full mouse control "with no typing required", with joystick as an option[^ref-34]. The floppy version carries an OPL2 (AdLib) soundtrack[^ref-14], and the French magazine Tilt listed support for "toutes cartes sonores (sauf Roland)" (all sound cards except Roland), with digitized voices on the Sound Blaster[^ref-37]. GamePro described it as a combination of first-person space shooting, a maze shoot-'em-up and a puzzler[^ref-35].

### Structure and Progression

The game is divided into 16 sections in the CD version, compared to 12 in the original floppy disk release[^ref-9]. Rather than traditional save games, Inca uses a password system[^ref-4]; Computer Gaming World noted that each eight-digit save code is tied to the installation, so codes from one computer will not work on another[^ref-34]. Per The Sierra Chest, pressing SHIFT, CTRL and ALT together restores all lives and power (except in the final fight against Aguirre), and pressing F1 on the restore-game screen displays a save code that skips to the Mayan temple, bypassing the arcade sequences[^ref-16].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

ClassicReload describes the game as "mostly a fighting space simulator", in which "some levels were purely shooting, had maze exploration, and some included riddles that could be solved by combining inventory item[s]"[^ref-5]. Players pilot the Tumi, which "harnesses the power of the sun and uses spatiotemporal energy weapons to blast the enemies of the renaissance out of your space time continuum"[^ref-18]. The Collection Chamber called the second level "an obvious homage to Star Wars' trench run on the Death Star"[^ref-4]. The adventure elements take place in the first person and are often inventory-based[^ref-4].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| Computer Gaming World (Sept 1993) | - | "The game failed to achieve its full potential"[^ref-34] |
| Tilt #110 (Jan 1993) | - | Called it "plus un film interactif qu'un jeu" (more an interactive film than a game)[^ref-37] |
| GamePro #57 (Apr 1994, CD-i) | - | "The graphics are sometimes mesmerizing, sometimes mundane"[^ref-35] |
| Power Unlimited (CD-i) | 90/100 | "Inca is an example of how the CD-I player can produce a good game" (Wikipedia's translation)[^ref-9] |
| Next Generation #2 (Feb 1995, CD-i) | 1/5 | "A somewhat painful conversion to the CD-i"[^ref-36] |

Contemporary critics were divided. Computer Gaming World's Chuck Miller praised the presentation, noting the game had been "awarded Best Graphics at the Supergames Show Paris, 1992", but concluded that "Inca comes across as a loosely strung together series of action sequences, mazes and puzzles"[^ref-34]. The magazine reviewed it as "Sierra's latest import from their 'French connection' Coktel Vision", listing a price of $59.95 and a 17 MB hard-drive requirement[^ref-34].

In France, Tilt's Jean-Loup Jovanovic wrote that "les éléments graphiques et sonores sont intégrés comme cela ne l'avait jamais été auparavant" ("the graphic and sound elements are integrated as never before"), but added "C'est plus un film interactif qu'un jeu. Cela explique la note «modérée» que je lui attribue" ("It is more an interactive film than a game. That explains the 'moderate' score I give it")[^ref-37]. (Translations from French are ours.)

Reviewing the CD-i version, GamePro wrote that "the controls bring Inca's high-flying story line and effects down to earth" and that the game "constantly pauses during puzzles to read the disc"[^ref-35]. Next Generation gave the CD-i release one star, citing "ho-hum action sequences", "crippling CD-ROM access times and unconvincing voice acting"[^ref-36].

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames lists a critic average of 70% across 16 ratings[^ref-20], while MyAbandonware users rate it 4.25/5 (8 votes, October 2026)[^ref-21]. One MobyGames player review described it as "the weirdest game that I have played so far", and another noted: "If you can find a copy and a computer capable of playing it, it's worth the experience simply for the sheer oddness of the game"[^ref-20].

The Collection Chamber's retrospective, which covers both Inca games, noted that "Everything about these games are contradictory. The action elements are at odds with the adventuring. The science-fiction is jarringly anachronistic to the historical setting. Yet despite this and the unintentionally hilarious art and design choices, they are games that I highly recommend"[^ref-4]. Hardcore Gaming 101 was cooler on the space combat: "Let's be honest – this is no Wing Commander"[^ref-7].

## Development

### Origins

Inca was developed by Coktel Vision, which MobyGames describes as "a Paris-based developer and publisher of education and entertainment software"[^ref-23]. The game was completed and released in 1992, before Sierra acquired Coktel Vision in 1993[^ref-23]. Sierra's involvement in the original release was as North American publisher: the Sierra manual credits a separate "Sierra Team" for international project and product management, manual text, programming, quality assurance and manual design, alongside "The Coktel Team"[^ref-6].

Sources disagree on who should be credited as the game's designer. The Sierra manual credits "Created by Pierre Gilhodes", who is also credited for graphics[^ref-6]. French press of the time credited Yannick Chosse instead: Tilt listed "Conception et scénario Yannick Chosse ; Programmation : MDO" ("Concept and scenario: Yannick Chosse; programming: MDO")[^ref-37], and Joystick profiled Chosse as "scénariste et directeur artistique d'Inca" ("writer and art director of Inca")[^ref-38]. MobyGames' DOS credits list both: "Created by Pierre Gilhodes" and "An interactive movie by Yannick Chosse"[^ref-20]. Gilhodes was known as the writer and artist of Gobliiins and Gobliins 2[^ref-38].

### Release and Publishers

Release arrangements varied by region. MobyGames' release records show the DOS version published by Sierra On-Line in the United States (1992 floppy, 1993 multimedia CD-ROM), and in the UK and Germany via distributors Digital Integration and Bomico; the CD-i version was published by Coktel Vision with Philips Interactive Media France; and the 1994 Japanese DOS and Macintosh versions were published by Zoom[^ref-39]. In France, Coktel Vision advertised the game itself in Joystick ("Disponible sur PC HD, PC CDROM – septembre 1992"), with the CD-i version billed as "une coproduction PHILIPS Interactive Media et Coktel Vision"[^ref-38].

### Production

The Sierra manual credits Charles Callet for music and sound effects[^ref-6], while MobyGames credits Gilles Douieb with "music written and orchestrated"[^ref-20], and the 1992 soundtrack CD lists G. Douieb as composer and arranger with J.M. Marrier among the performers[^ref-25]. Wikipedia describes the introduction song "Inca People" as "by J.M. Marrier composed for the game"[^ref-9]. Joystick reported that Coktel held studio sessions with professional musicians, producing 35 minutes of music[^ref-38], and Computer Gaming World reported that "many radio stations in Europe are playing selections from Inca"[^ref-34]. The voice cast included Ivan Bond as Aguirre, Yasmine Moïra as the young Aclla, and Alberto Quintanilla as Inca Huayna Capac[^ref-20]. One player recalled on Reddit: "I warmly remember the soundtrack"[^ref-26].

Joystick reported in mid-1992 that the game was being developed on PC CD-ROM, with the hard-disk version described as "un peu remaniée, avec moins de graphismes" ("somewhat reworked, with fewer graphics")[^ref-38]. According to Wikipedia, the CD release "provides some longer or additional cutscenes and an updated soundtrack (as well as full voiceover soundtrack)"[^ref-9]. The Collection Chamber notes that on floppy "the cutscenes are often limited to static screens rather than full-motion video", while "the extra space on the CD allows for full speech and a far higher quality soundtrack"[^ref-4].

### Technical Achievements

Coktel promoted Inca as an interactive film: Joystick wrote "Plus qu'un jeu, Inca est un véritable film interactif" ("More than a game, Inca is a true interactive film")[^ref-38], and Tilt called it "le premier véritable film interactif sur PC" ("the first true interactive film on PC")[^ref-37]. Its ships and locations were synthetic images pre-calculated on dedicated hardware and then read from disc rather than computed in real time, and its actors were digitized and retouched in Deluxe Paint[^ref-38]. Joystick also reported that many in-house programming tools were developed for Inca for use in later Coktel products[^ref-38]. The Collection Chamber judged the graphics "pretty good for the early 90s, with digitised actors"[^ref-4]. PCGamingWiki lists VGA graphics with an Intel 286 processor and 640 kB of RAM as minimum requirements[^ref-28].

## Legacy

Inca was followed by a sequel, Inca II: Wiracocha (also known as Inca II: Nations of Immortality), released in 1993[^ref-30]. The series became part of Sierra's catalog when it acquired Coktel Vision: "Sierra's 1993 acquisition of Coktel Vision, a Paris-based developer and publisher of education and entertainment software, added titles such as Goblins and the Inca series to the Sierra family of products"[^ref-23].

### Cult Status and Rediscovery

The game has kept a following among retro enthusiasts. A 2003 MobyGames player review remarked: "I'm honestly surprised it doesn't have a cult following; it's the sort of game that lovers of the bizarre would flock to"[^ref-20].

### Commercial and Legal History

The current rights holder is not documented in a primary source. In a 2014 GOG forum thread, users speculated that "the rights to the Coktel games should lie with Activision, through Sierra"[^ref-31]. The game is not currently sold on major digital storefronts and remains accessible through preservation sites.

**Purchase / Digital Stores**

The Inca series is not currently available for purchase on major digital storefronts.
