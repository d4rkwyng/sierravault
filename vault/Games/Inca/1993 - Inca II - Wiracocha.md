# Inca II: Nations of Immortality

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Inca II: Nations of Immortality is the sequel to Inca, the surreal space combat/adventure game with a fantasy take on Incan mythologies[^ref-12]. Developed by Coktel Vision and published by Sierra On-Line, Inc.[^ref-12], this 1993 game combined adventure puzzles with arcade-style space combat sequences in an unusual blend that would prove both memorable and controversial.

The game opens with Atahualpa, son of El Dorado, before control passes back to El Dorado himself in the fight against his arch-enemy Aguirre[^ref-12], across settings that include the Australian desert and outer space[^ref-16][^ref-17]. Like its predecessor, Inca II used pre-rendered bitmaps and digitized video cutscenes[^ref-12]. Besides the standard release, a French "Série limitée" edition bundled the 3.5" floppy version with a soundtrack disc[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

Set in a fantastical interpretation of Incan mythology, Inca II: Nations of Immortality continues the adventures begun in its predecessor. The player starts as Atahualpa, El Dorado's "spunky and arrogant kid", whose joyride in the family starship lands him in trouble near a strange asteroid; the role then returns to El Dorado, who must save his son and stop Aguirre's plans for the asteroid[^ref-12]. The story takes players from the Australian desert to outer space[^ref-16][^ref-17].

The game's voice cast included Ivan Bond as the antagonist Aguirre and Yasmine Moïra as Aclla[^ref-12], though the voice acting would become one of the game's most notorious elements. As one player fondly recalled, "the voice acting is laughable but it's part of the fun like a hilarious b movie"[^ref-5].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Inca II supports keyboard and mouse input, with optional analog joystick support[^ref-21]. MobyGames lists minimum requirements of an Intel i386 processor, 2 MB of RAM and PC/MS-DOS 5.0, noting that Sierra's original release required at least a 486DX 33 MHz, 4 MB RAM and a 2X CD-ROM drive[^ref-21].

### Structure and Progression

The game combined adventure gameplay with arcade-style space combat and puzzle-solving[^ref-12], using pre-rendered bitmaps and digitized video cutscenes[^ref-12]. Some levels are purely shooting, some involve maze exploration, and others include inventory-based puzzles or riddles; an autosave lets the player resume from any chapter or replay previously seen cutscenes[^ref-17]. The game was distributed on both 3.5" floppy disks and CD-ROM[^ref-21].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

At a few points the player faces a dilemma whose outcome affects the difficulty of the final battle[^ref-17]. Graphics support covers MCGA and VGA[^ref-21].

Audio support included popular sound cards of the era: Adlib, Pro Audio Spectrum, Sound Blaster, Sound Blaster 16, and Sound Blaster Pro, and the disc carries audio CD tracks[^ref-21]. The musical score was composed by Gilles Douieb[^ref-12].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Inca II drew mixed reviews; MobyGames aggregates a 72% critic average from 17 ratings[^ref-12].

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| PC Gamer US (July 1994) | 81% | "Inca II is ultimately a winner"[^ref-22] |
| White Wolf #49 (Nov. 1994) | 3/5 | Review by James V. Trunzo[^ref-17] |
| MobyGames | 72% | Critic average, 17 ratings[^ref-12] |

PC Gamer praised the "great diversity of gameplay" and the $34.95 price, while noting that "the acting is pretty cheesy (not necessarily a bad thing!)"; its bottom line called it "a weird mix" that is "fun to play, easy on the wallet, and often stunning to look at"[^ref-22].

Computer Gaming World liked the game's graphics, action and music but criticized its "schizophrenic gameplay" and voice acting[^ref-17], writing that "the schizophrenic gameplay is aggravated by the voice acting, which is so bad in spots that you have to think it's a joke"[^ref-23].

In White Wolf, James V. Trunzo wrote that "Inca II was designed in France; a European sense of humor certainly comes through. While this arcade adventure has several unique features and a few [really] enjoyable segments [...] much of the game may strike you as bizarre"[^ref-17].

### Modern Assessment

The game has a small nostalgic following among retro players. One player commented, "This was a masterpiece of my childhood"[^ref-5], and another recalled it as "the first CD ROM game I owned"[^ref-5].

Inca II also earned a dubious honor: Computer Gaming World's November 1996 list of the worst games of all time placed it at #18, calling it "an indescribably incoherent action/adventure blend, with uneven graphics and ridiculous premise"[^ref-24].

## Development

### Origins

Developed by French studio Coktel Vision[^ref-3][^ref-7], Inca II was the company's follow-up to its 1992 original Inca[^ref-12]. It runs on Coktel's Gob engine[^ref-16].

### Production

The game's production involved digitized video cutscenes and pre-rendered graphics[^ref-12]. The voice cast featured Ivan Bond as the villainous Aguirre and Yasmine Moïra as Aclla[^ref-12], while Gilles Douieb composed the musical score[^ref-12].

The French "Série limitée" edition contained the 3.5" floppy version of the game plus a soundtrack disc[^ref-7].

## Legacy

Inca II is no longer sold commercially; the Internet Archive hosts an installer package that runs the game through DOSBox, tested on Windows 11[^ref-3]. The title was later included in the "Inca I & II: Collector's Edition" compilation released in 1995[^ref-8], which contained both games plus a soundtrack disc with music from both[^ref-8].

Players have requested an official re-release on GOG's wishlist[^ref-5].
