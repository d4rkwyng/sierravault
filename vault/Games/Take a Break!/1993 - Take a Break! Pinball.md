# Take a Break! Pinball

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Take a Break! Pinball was a 1993 pinball computer game collection for Windows 3.1, developed by [[Dynamix|Dynamix, Inc.]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line|Sierra On-Line, Inc.]][^ref-7][^ref-20] The game was the second and last entry in the Take a Break! series, designed as bite-sized experiences for Windows users[^ref-2]. Marketed with the tagline "Take a break and have some fun!", the game promised "Amazingly real Pinball action!" that was Windows compatible, allowing users to "jump from work to play in a nanosecond"[^ref-3][^ref-14].

The collection contains eight full-screen pinball tables, each themed on a Sierra or Dynamix game[^ref-20][^ref-21]: three Space Quest tables (Planet Pinball, Levels One to Three), two Nova 9 tables (Draxon, Levels One and Two), the King's Quest table Quest for Daventry, the Leisure Suit Larry table Larry's Big Score, and Flipped Out Willy, based on The Adventures of Willy Beamish[^ref-7]. Up to four people could play[^ref-20][^ref-23], and the box promised up to three balls in play at once[^ref-3]. The game is listed as a 1993 release in IGDB, GameFAQs, Giant Bomb and Wikidata[^ref-13][^ref-14][^ref-4][^ref-15].

## Story Summary

As a pinball collection, Take a Break! Pinball has no overall narrative. Instead, each table's "scoring opportunities" are structured after the adventure game it is based on[^ref-20].

Quest for Daventry is based on King's Quest V; Sierra's 1992 preview asked players to "rescue the kingdom of Daventry from the black wizard, Mordack"[^ref-20][^ref-23]. On it, an animated yeti picks up the ball and drops it into the sea, and a secret island rises out of the water to expose a special target[^ref-20]. The three-level Planet Pinball follows Roger Wilco and the Time Rippers; on Level Two, which follows Space Quest IV, a pterodactyl carries off a ball sunk in the Buttes of Estros trap hole; sinking a second brings the Sequel Police, whom the pterodactyl throws off the board, releasing the other ball for multiball[^ref-20][^ref-23]. MobyGames names the three levels Planet Xenon in the Beginning, Spaced Travel and Reformation Day[^ref-7].

In Larry's Big Score, "the objective is to score points with Larry's girlfriend, Passionate Patti"[^ref-23]. Flipped Out Willy has players try to save the city from the villainess Leona Humpford, and the Draxon tables send the player through the nine planets of the Nova 9 system against Gir Draxon[^ref-23]. On Draxon Two the player can load a ball into a gun and fire it at Gir Draxon's nostrils; two nostril shots and the game is "beaten"[^ref-20]. MobyGames names the two Draxon levels Nova 9 and Lots in Space[^ref-7]. Gir Draxon is the villain of Dynamix's Stellar 7 and Nova 9[^ref-26].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Take-A-Break! Pinball "puts 8 full-featured Pinball games at the tip of your mouse-clicking finger"[^ref-3][^ref-14]. The flippers are worked with the two mouse buttons or the two Shift keys, and up to four players can join by inserting extra coins[^ref-26]. Each machine spans the length of the screen, with a quarter-screen window beside it showing animations tied to play on the table, plus a "Silent Radio" box of scrolling text[^ref-20].

The game's version of nudging is called "grunching", done by pressing Ctrl and Shift together[^ref-20][^ref-25]. CGW wrote that grunching "simply pulls the ball in the desired direction" rather than jostling the table, and that Take A Break "seems to have the most sensitive Tilt mechanism of the bunch"[^ref-20]. MobyGames classifies the perspective as both 1st-person and top-down[^ref-7].

### Structure and Progression

The eight tables are[^ref-7][^ref-20]:

- **Planet Pinball: Level One – Planet Xenon in the Beginning** (Space Quest)
- **Planet Pinball: Level Two – Spaced Travel** (Space Quest IV)
- **Planet Pinball: Level Three – Reformation Day** (Space Quest)
- **Draxon: Level One – Nova 9** (Nova 9: Return of Gir Draxon)
- **Draxon: Level Two – Lots in Space** (Nova 9: Return of Gir Draxon)
- **Quest for Daventry** (King's Quest V)
- **Larry's Big Score** (Leisure Suit Larry)
- **Flipped Out Willy** (The Adventures of Willy Beamish)

PC Player counted these as five machines, two of which have several levels; a new level opens at a set score, but all eight can be picked straight from the main menu[^ref-26]. The game can pause and save mid-game and keeps high scores[^ref-23][^ref-26].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

According to Home of the Underdogs (HOTUD), whose review MyAbandonware reproduces, the game includes "a comprehensive help file for each table that lists all the combos you can get"[^ref-5]. Sierra's 1992 preview described the on-line Help as "Chock full of humorous cryptic messages, entertaining suggestions and a complete dinner menu"[^ref-23].

Lead programmer Louie McCrady's tips for InterAction describe the tables as loaded with "over 1000 animation sequences and 1.5 Megs of digitized speech and sound effects"[^ref-25]. On Planet Pinball Level II, for example, grunching to drop all the Galleria targets and then hitting the flashing Buckazoids target earns an extra ball, with no limit to the number of extra balls earned this way[^ref-25].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Reviews were mixed. Chris Lombardi's December 1993 *Computer Gaming World* pinball round-up, "Bumper Crop", found that structuring the machines like adventure games "lends them charm", but that the graphic and sound extras "carry a huge CPU overhead": "It can be so bad as to be unplayable on a 386, and irritating on a 486."[^ref-20] Apart from the speed problem, he judged the pinball mechanics "realistic for the most part", although if a ball starts up a ramp it continues up even without the momentum to make it[^ref-20]. He found the Quest for Daventry table "actually a bit drab" and Larry's Big Score "a fun machine to play"[^ref-20].

Boris Schneider of the German *PC Player* (September 1993) scored it 61, praising the table design and variety but criticising the ball movement: "Der hakelige Kugellauf kostet ganz schön Punkte" (the jerky ball movement costs quite a few points)[^ref-26]. *PC Review* (October 1993) compared it unfavourably with Pinball Dreams. Its reviewer couldn't resize the window, leaving "a doll-house sized table", called the music "not bad at all" but unlike a real pinball machine's, and concluded: "frankly I'd sooner play Solitaire"[^ref-27].

Home of the Underdogs later called it "Second and last in Sierra's underrated Take a Break! series" and "a good pinball game"[^ref-5].

### Modern Assessment

Retro365 (2025) judged that "The overall execution was lacking, with clunky gameplay and simplistic table designs that failed to capture the depth of traditional pinball simulations"[^ref-2]. HOTUD's review praises "Great table animations, plus a comprehensive help file for each table"[^ref-5].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 59% (critics, based on 4 ratings)[^ref-7]
- **MyAbandonware:** 4.67/5 (user rating, 3 votes)[^ref-5]

## Development

### Origins

Sierra introduced the Take a Break! line in InterAction's Summer 1992 issue as a series for Windows users who want a game that doesn't "take a huge investment of time or energy to get into or out of"[^ref-23]. The article announced Take-A-Break Pinball as "The first game in the series", "coming in April", with five pinball games "each based on a hit Sierra/Dynamix adventure game character", two of them with multiple playfields[^ref-23]. The game slipped: InterAction's Fall 1992 preview still described "five fast-loading pinball games for Windows" but said it was "Slated for late fall", while Take-A-Break! Crosswords was already on sale in the same issue[^ref-24]. Pinball thus shipped as the series' second title in 1993[^ref-2].

According to CGW, "Microsoft said 'it couldn't be done' when Dynamix approached the programmers of Windows about a pinball game for that operating system", and it "took several years of hard work (a lot longer than they had expected)" for Dynamix to prove them wrong[^ref-20].

### Production

Scott Wallin was the game's designer and director[^ref-20][^ref-6][^ref-7]. InterAction reported that the creative team was required "to play countless hours of actual pinball" during design, and that "The project's programmer had an actual pinball game installed in his office" while developing the physics. Wallin travelled to Chicago for the Pinball Expo, the annual trade gathering for pinball designers, where he "studied hundreds of the latest pinball games"[^ref-23].

MobyGames credits 26 people[^ref-7]. CGW's September 1993 "Inside the Industry" column noted that Dynamix had taken "a different approach by putting eight different pinball games in the same product, each built around the theme from a Sierra or Dynamix game"[^ref-21].

### Technical Achievements

The game required Windows 3.1[^ref-20][^ref-26]. PC Player noted that it detected the language of the installed Windows and ran in German, English or French accordingly[^ref-26]. The box copy marketed the ease of switching between work and play[^ref-3], and the game could be minimised to an icon in an instant if the boss came in[^ref-26].

### Technical Issues

A bug in the Quest for Daventry table made the ball invisible after it entered the Endless Desert Temple Stone Wall Trap. Sierra's patch replaced KQUEST.DLL (130 KB)[^ref-8].

The CD edition could show a "COULD NOT FIND DIB.DRV" error, also seen with the Lode Runner demo; a DIB.DRV patch (20.6 KB) fixed it[^ref-8].

Performance was the most criticised technical problem. CGW found the game unplayable on a 386 and irritating on a 486[^ref-20], and PC Player and PC Review respectively faulted the jerky ball movement and the small, fixed-size window[^ref-26][^ref-27].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The Help system includes a "Dinner Menu", an advertised feature: Sierra's 1992 preview promised "a complete dinner menu (don't ask why...just enjoy)"[^ref-23]. MobyGames lists its dishes, among them "Honey and Ginger Glazed Pheasant" and "Red Licorice Ropes"[^ref-7].

Additional trivia:
- Take a Break! Pinball was the second and final entry in the Take a Break! series[^ref-2]
- It was announced in 1992 as the *first* Take a Break! title, but Crosswords shipped first[^ref-23][^ref-24]
- MobyGames lists "Dynamix Pinball" as an alternate title[^ref-7]
- Tables drew on both Dynamix games (Nova 9, The Adventures of Willy Beamish) and Sierra games (King's Quest V, Space Quest, Leisure Suit Larry)[^ref-7][^ref-20]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Take a Break! Pinball appeared at No. 16 in the PC Games (MS-DOS) category of the PC Research Hits List of top-selling software for July 1993, printed in Computer Gaming World's November 1993 issue[^ref-22]. No unit sales figures have been found. The Take a Break! series ended with this second entry[^ref-2].

Used copies still appear on Amazon's marketplace listing[^ref-10].

### Critical Perspective

Computer Gaming World's verdict captured both the ambition and the problem: "While Dynamix has proven Microsoft wrong by creating realistic pinball action in Windows, they have, in a fit of over-enthusiasm, overloaded Windows' capabilities with all of the graphics and animation."[^ref-20]
