# F-14 Tomcat

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 19, 2026</small>

## Overview

F-14 Tomcat is a combat flight game for the Commodore 64, developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Activision]]. MobyGames dates the release to February 1989, and the player's guide carries a 1988–1989 copyright.[^ref-1][^ref-4] The player enlists as a U.S. Navy ensign, goes through flight training, and then flies a career of missions from the carrier *Nimitz* against MiG-21s, MiG-25s, MiG-29s and F-4s. A good record leads to Top Gun School and, eventually, retirement.[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-12] [[Kevin Ryan]] designed and programmed it with Nick Skrepetos, and [[Jeff Tunnell]] directed.[^ref-2][^ref-4]

The game predates Sierra's 1990 purchase of Dynamix. Dynamix made it for Activision as the studio was leaving Electronic Arts. In February 1989 Activision's parent company, Mediagenic, signed Dynamix as an affiliated publisher.[^ref-12][^ref-13] SierraVault covers it because the vault's inclusion criteria take in Dynamix's complete catalog, including work done before the acquisition. The real publisher was Activision, not Sierra.[^ref-1]

Critics generally saw it as more game than simulation. *The Games Machine* gave it 73% and described "pure Top Gun action with a moderate amount of depth of play". *Zzap!64* gave it 67%, calling it "a game, not a simulator".[^ref-9][^ref-8] Activision released a separate F-14 Tomcat for MS-DOS in 1990. MobyGames lists that version as a separate game, credited to Activision's own designers and programmers.[^ref-3][^ref-7]

## Story Summary

There is no fixed plot. The player's guide frames the game as a Navy career: "Enlist in the best Flight Training in the world." At the end of training an instructor gives the player a nickname "that will stick with you throughout your career", such as "Deadeye" or "Potato-Bug". The player then joins one of two Tomcat fighter groups on a U.S. carrier, the Jolly Rogers or the Black Aces.[^ref-4] On carrier duty, Admiral Hawk briefs and debriefs each mission.[^ref-4][^ref-12]

The guide promises R & R, medals and patches along the way, and a return to active duty after Top Gun School.[^ref-4] Brad Bombardiere's *Computer Gaming World* briefing explains that a pilot who reaches age 28 with a rating of 85% or better goes to Top Gun School, and can retire at 36.[^ref-12] Mistakes have consequences. *Zzap!64* noted that crashing or ejecting can leave the player "bobbing in the sea, up against a court martial or even rotting in a Middle Eastern prison".[^ref-8]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

On the Commodore 64 the plane is flown with a joystick in port 2: forward to dive, back to climb, left and right to roll. The fire button launches the selected weapon.[^ref-4] Other functions are on the keyboard:[^ref-4]

- **Weapons:** F1, F3, F5 and F7 select the AIM-54 Phoenix, AIM-120 AMRAAM, AIM-9 Sidewinder and M61A1 Vulcan cannon.
- **Countermeasures:** chaff against radar-guided missiles and flares against heat-seekers.
- **Radio:** request permission to fire, request a vector home, warn away enemy aircraft and request the range to a target.
- **Displays:** a head-up display, a Data Detail Display, a Tactical Information Display with 6, 12, 25, 50 and 100 nautical mile ranges, and a full-screen overview map.
- **Landing aids:** a key that lines the plane up with the runway or carrier, and an Automatic Landing System.

Before the main menu, the player must name a highlighted part of the aircraft, a weapon or a member of the crew. A wrong answer means being "quickly enlisted and just as quickly discharged", and the game has to be reloaded.[^ref-4] MobyGames lists the game among titles with manual-lookup copy protection.[^ref-1]

### Structure and Progression

The main menu offers **Enlist**, **Continue Career** and **Fly Mission**. Fly Mission skips the preliminaries and goes straight to a single mission in "one of 80 world hot spots", but the result cannot be saved.[^ref-4] A blank data disk holds up to 14 separate careers.[^ref-4]

- **Basic Flight Training:** three flights in a T-2 Buckeye from Whiting Naval Air Station, covering headings and altitudes, then maneuvers flown behind the commanding officer. The low-G yo-yo, spiral dive and split-S are among them. This stage decides whether the player becomes a jet pilot or settles for helicopters or propeller planes.[^ref-4]
- **Instant replay:** on the Commodore 64, the player can replay each maneuver and see the joystick movements.[^ref-4][^ref-10]
- **Advanced and Final Training:** dogfights against instructors. *Computer Gaming World* describes a single fight against Captain Vice, whose outcome decides the player's nickname, and then a fight against Captain "Bones", who must be beaten in two out of three dogfights for assignment to the *Nimitz*.[^ref-12]
- **Tours of duty:** missions are either "Fire At Will" or "Do Not Fire" until fired upon. In the second kind the pilot can ask permission or try to provoke an attack.[^ref-12][^ref-1]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The game has no puzzles. Its decisions are tactical. The guide sets out the missile envelopes: the Phoenix for ranges beyond 31 miles, the AMRAAM between 11 and 31 miles, the Sidewinder under 11 miles and the cannon for close dogfighting.[^ref-4] Too many G's can black out or red out the pilot.[^ref-4] Bombardiere warned that using the auto-landing key in training earns the label "wing weenie", and that ejecting from "a perfectly good working Tomcat" will destroy the pilot's reputation.[^ref-12]

Benn Dunnington of *Info* told readers: "Don't let the simplified display fool you." He wrote that the game gains speed by "opting to use animated sprites instead of solid-modelled objects and terrain", and praised the combat displays. His conclusion: "While the sound is nothing to write home about, the total effect is quite good. A very playable game."[^ref-10]

*The Games Machine* said the game "follows the MicroProse lead with detailed peripheral screens". It criticized the awkward enemy jets and the sparse scenery: "A green ground or blue sea and blue sky is all there is to see for much of the time." Its verdict was that "the immediate appeal and playability are retained", and that buyers wanting "pure Top Gun action with a moderate amount of depth of play" could not go far wrong.[^ref-9] *Zzap!64* scored presentation 87%, graphics 79%, sound 46%, hookability 79% and lastability 58%. Its summary read: "An attempt to combine arcade popularity with simulation skills which doesn't quite hit the mark."[^ref-8]

*Computer Gaming World* did not score the game. Its April 1989 feature by Brad Bombardiere, "'Tomcat' On The Prowl", was a strategy briefing that listed the C-64/128 version at $39.95, with Dynamix as designer.[^ref-12] In its December 1989 advertising, Activision called F-14 Tomcat "the best-selling combat flight simulator on the Commodore today". The advertisement quoted *Compute!'s Gazette*: "F-14 TOMCAT is one of the best combat flight simulators for the 64."[^ref-14] SierraVault has not found independent sales figures to check Activision's claim.

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames averages six critic ratings to 71%. Lemon64 averages its six listed magazine reviews to the same figure.[^ref-1][^ref-5] C64-Wiki users rate it 7.25 out of 10.[^ref-6]

## Development

### Origins

In a February 1989 trade-show report, *Computer Gaming World* noted that "Activision is pushing the graphic capability of the C-64 with their F-14 Tomcat, a game which emphasizes dogfighting".[^ref-11] In April 1989 the magazine reported that Dynamix had signed an "Affiliated Publisher" agreement with Mediagenic on February 14, 1989. It listed F-14 Tomcat as Dynamix's game for Activision, alongside Project Firestart for Electronic Arts. The magazine reported that, according to Jeff Tunnell, the firm planned "to complete several development projects for Activision in addition to releasing its own titles".[^ref-12] Jimmy Maher lists F-14 Tomcat among the 3D action games that followed Arcticfox and built Dynamix's reputation.[^ref-13]

### Production

The player's guide states that "F-14 TOMCAT was developed by Dynamix". It credits Kevin Ryan and Nick Skrepetos as programmers, John Burton and Tom Collie as artists, Jeff Tunnell as director, and Bryce Morsello for sound and music. Terry Ishida and John Skeel produced the C64 version.[^ref-4] A "Dynamix Team TOMCAT" page notes that Ryan "designed TOMCAT while a close friend attended A.O.C.S." It also introduces John Burton, Mike Edwards, Cyrus Kanga and Mark Brenneman, and thanks Skrepetos and Collie.[^ref-4]

MobyGames gives the full Commodore 64 credits:[^ref-2]

- **Director:** [[Jeff Tunnell]]
- **Design:** [[Kevin Ryan]], Jeff Tunnell
- **Programming:** Kevin Ryan, Nick Skrepetos
- **Art Director:** John Burton
- **Production Art:** Tom Collie, Dariusz Lukaszuk
- **Music & Sound:** Bryce Morsello
- **Producer:** Terry Ishida
- **Special Thanks:** Michael Giron, David Selle

### Technical Specifications

- **Platform:** Commodore 64 or 128, with a joystick.[^ref-4]
- **Media:** two double-sided disks. Disk 1 side 1 boots the game, and a separate blank disk stores careers.[^ref-4]
- **Price:** $39.95 in the US and £14.99 (disk only) in the UK.[^ref-12][^ref-8]

The release year is reported inconsistently. MobyGames and the Dynamix Wiki give February 1989.[^ref-1][^ref-7] Lemon64 and C64-Wiki give 1988, and the player's guide is copyright 1988 and 1989.[^ref-5][^ref-6][^ref-4] *Computer Gaming World* previewed the game in February 1989 and covered it as a released game in April.[^ref-11][^ref-12] SierraVault files it under 1989, the documented release.

### The MS-DOS F-14 Tomcat

Activision published an F-14 Tomcat for MS-DOS in 1990. MobyGames treats it as a separate game and does not list Dynamix as developer.[^ref-3] Its credits name Michael Latham and Michael Suarez for game design, Andre Gagnon for programming, Russell Lieblich and Matthew Berardo for music, and Glyn Anderson for the "OmniMusic and PC Audio sound drivers".[^ref-3] The Dynamix Wiki says Activision ported the game "without Dynamix's involvement".[^ref-7] The 1989 player's guide covers both Commodore and MS-DOS, credits development to Dynamix, and says the MS-DOS version was produced by Mark Johnson, Mike Suarez and Michael Latham. It describes MS-DOS-only features such as external views from the numeric keypad, and notes that the instant replay is Commodore-only.[^ref-4] MobyGames lists VGA and MCGA support for the MS-DOS version, and its inclusion in compilations such as *Air Commander* (1992) and *CD Flight Pack* (1993).[^ref-3] Because Dynamix is not credited with developing it, this page treats the Commodore 64 game as the Dynamix title.

## Legacy

When Dynamix signed with Mediagenic, *Computer Gaming World* reported Tunnell's plan for "several development projects for Activision".[^ref-12] Dynamix games that Activision published later in 1989 included [[1989 - MechWarrior|MechWarrior]], [[1989 - Die Hard|Die Hard]] and [[1989 - Ghostbusters II|Ghostbusters II]].[^ref-13][^ref-16][^ref-17] Sierra bought Dynamix on March 27, 1990.[^ref-13] Kevin Ryan, its designer, was one of the early Dynamix staff pictured in Maher's history of the studio.[^ref-13]
