# Rama

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Rama is a first-person science fiction adventure game released in late 1996 (Metacritic gives October 31, 1996; Wikipedia gives November 1996 for North America), developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line.[^ref-1][^ref-31] The game is based on Arthur C. Clarke's celebrated Rama novel series, primarily adapting the 1989 sequel *Rama II* co-written with Gentry Lee, who served as the game's designer.[^ref-2] Players assume the role of a replacement astronaut sent to explore an immense cylindrical alien spacecraft that has entered our solar system, tasked with deciphering complex alien puzzles and discovering the vessel's mysterious purpose.[^ref-3]

The game combines 3D-rendered scenery with live-action actors portraying the Newton Team crew members.[^ref-2] A year after release, *Libération* called it "une des adaptations les plus réussies" (one of the most successful adaptations) of genre fiction to CD-ROM.[^ref-41] Marketed in France as "l'événement SF de l'année 96" (the SF event of 1996), the project involved Gentry Lee as a writing consultant and Arthur C. Clarke as an on-screen performer.[^ref-5] Clarke appears in the game when the player dies, and in the epilogue, giving advice to the player.[^ref-2]

Critics noted its similarities to *Myst*.[^ref-2] Its puzzles lean heavily on mathematics and logic, including arithmetic in non-decimal number systems: GameBoomers' walkthrough notes that "the Myrmicats and the Avians of RAMA use a hexadecimal number system, and the Octospiders an octal one," and its primer also covers base-3 for the matrix tiles.[^ref-7] Reviews ranged widely, from PC Gamer US's 92% to Next Generation's 2/5.[^ref-25][^ref-2]

## Story Summary

Four years prior to the game's events, a gigantic cylindrical spaceship of unknown origin was discovered hurtling towards our sun.[^ref-10] The International Space Agency (ISA) named the mysterious vessel "Rama" and dispatched an expedition called the Newton Team to investigate its purpose and origins.[^ref-11] The mission was originally one of exploration, but circumstances quickly transform it into a desperate rescue operation when contact with the crew is lost.[^ref-10]

The player assumes the role of a replacement astronaut sent to join the expedition after mission commander Valeriy Borzov mysteriously died aboard Rama, following a routine medical procedure.[^ref-10] Upon entering the massive alien construct, the player discovers a startling world of bizarre architecture, engineering marvels, and alien wonders that presents humanity's greatest intellectual challenge.[^ref-3] Inside, the player explores the Plains, two Raman cities that the expedition crew nicknamed "London" and "Bangkok", and the island of "New York" in the Cylindrical Sea.[^ref-12]

As exploration progresses, the player encounters evidence of multiple alien species that inhabit or have previously inhabited Rama: the Myrmicats (seen in images but never encountered), the Avians, and the Octospiders.[^ref-2] The Myrmicats and Avians use a hexadecimal number system and the Octospiders an octal one.[^ref-7] The narrative reveals that the human crew members have scattered throughout the vessel, and the player must locate and interact with them through video communication terminals while piecing together what happened during the initial exploration.[^ref-4] The plot escalates dramatically as players uncover tantalizing clues about Rama's purpose, though the game deliberately maintains an air of mystery about the spacecraft's ultimate destination and the intentions of its unseen creators.[^ref-13]

The story draws heavily from characters and situations established in the novel *Rama II*, presenting them as if this were humanity's first encounter with the alien vessel—a creative choice that both honors and diverges from Clarke's literary universe.[^ref-14] The game's endgame sequence involves a timed escape as Rama prepares to leave the solar system, requiring players to complete their final objectives within six in-game hours as tracked by their wrist computer.[^ref-15]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Rama employs a first-person point-and-click interface in the style of its contemporary, Myst, utilizing snapshot-type movement between pre-rendered locations.[^ref-18] Players navigate through the cylindrical spacecraft by clicking on directional arrows to move forward or turn, with the mouse serving as the primary input device.[^ref-16] The game window occupies approximately 60% of the screen, with a persistent interface frame taking up the remaining area—a design choice that some reviewers criticized as unnecessary and space-wasting.[^ref-17]

The interface includes a wrist-mounted computer that serves multiple functions: tracking mission time, storing important data, and providing navigation assistance, though the in-game map was noted by some reviewers as being "all but useless" for orientation within Rama's complex interior.[^ref-18] Players communicate with crew members through video terminals scattered throughout the environment, though characters do not hold conversations with the silent protagonist.[^ref-4] GameSpot warned that players who enjoy character interaction are likely to be "left a little disappointed."[^ref-17]

Inventory management proves challenging due to the number of collectible items. GameBoomers' walkthrough notes that "inventory scrolling is very touchy,"[^ref-7] and Solo Gamer Reviews found the room provided "far too sparse for the enormous amount of trinkets and puzzle pieces you'll be collecting."[^ref-18] Instead of a Look command, players rely on Puck, whom GameSpot describes as a "holographic guide"; because Puck is stored in the inventory, players often have to scroll to find him.[^ref-17]

### Structure and Progression

The game spans three CD-ROMs, with two discs dedicated to gameplay and a third reserved for video interviews and supplementary content.[^ref-1] The structure divides into distinct phases, with the first portion allowing exploration at the player's own pace while the final section imposes a strict time limit requiring completion within six in-game hours.[^ref-15]

Rama's interior is organized into several major areas corresponding to different alien habitations and technological centers:

- **The Hub (Entry Point):** The southern entry point into Rama where the player begins their exploration[^ref-21]
- **London:** One of three named "cities" within Rama, featuring specific alien technologies and puzzles[^ref-7]
- **Bangkok:** A second major exploration zone with its own distinct architecture and challenges[^ref-7]
- **New York:** The third named location, on an island in the Cylindrical Sea[^ref-7][^ref-2]
- **Avian Lair:** Area associated with the Avians[^ref-23]
- **Octospider Lair:** Area associated with the Octospiders[^ref-23]

The game follows a largely linear progression, requiring certain items and tasks to be completed in sequence before new areas become accessible.[^ref-7] Inventory item locations, the timing of animation sequences, a few puzzles and some central-plain destinations are randomized; Balmoral Software judged that the randomization changes only details and adds "little to game replayability."[^ref-23]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Mathematics is central to the puzzles. Balmoral Software's review asked, "Should Rama be subtitled The Numbers Game?", adding that players will need "at least a rudimentary knowledge of octal and hexadecimal arithmetic, sequences of prime numbers, and pattern recognition."[^ref-23] GameBoomers' walkthrough includes a primer on base-3, base-8 and base-16 number systems.[^ref-7]

PC Gamer US describes the entrance exams in Bangkok as requiring "the Avians' base-sixteen math system, and the Octospiders' (naturally) base-eight system."[^ref-25] Color-coded displays feature prominently in Octospider puzzles, though some players reported difficulty due to color rendition issues on different monitor setups.[^ref-7]

Beyond mathematics, GameSpot notes "a good number of situational and object-based puzzles to solve that are equally challenging."[^ref-17] The game includes approximately 30 matrix tiles with geometric designs, though only about 10 prove essential for completion, and 10 rectangular yellow panels that must be assembled into two maps.[^ref-7]

Death (or being fired) is possible, followed by "an apology and warning from Clarke in a live-action video" and a forgiving retry function.[^ref-23] The game supports up to 99 saved game slots, encouraging frequent saving given the puzzle difficulty.[^ref-23]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Upon release, Rama received divergent critical assessments that reflected the polarized nature of its design philosophy. PC Gamer US awarded the game 92%, with reviewer William R. Trotter declaring it "one of the best sci-fi games ever to appear in the PC format, period" and "a monumental achievment [sic]; a landmark game that raises the bar several notches," while warning buyers to "be prepared for the workout of your life."[^ref-25]

Computer Gaming World's Keith Ferrell provided one of the most enthusiastic assessments; per Wikipedia's summary he praised it as the most convincing computerized world he had encountered, and he described "an environment studded with puzzles and enigma, challenges and mysteries."[^ref-2] Ferrell concluded that "unlike MYST, the story here outweighs its setting, a tribute to Clarke, Lee, and the team that supported them. RAMA is in virtually every way a triumph and another large step toward the creation of wholly convincing interactive SF novels."[^ref-2]

However, PC Gamer UK's Steve Owen offered a more measured verdict, awarding 70% and noting: "What I conclude from playing this graphic adventure is that it is the result of some fantastic imagination realised in a compelling story, put together using a good script and some genuinely fine acting, but let down because the game has no imagination."[^ref-26] Owen called it "such a shame that Clarke and Lee's book [...] has been recreated into your bog standard Myst copy."[^ref-26]

PC Games magazine gave a C+ overall rating, with reviewer Sam Marshall observing that "RAMA is a Myst play-alike that offers plenty of intellectual challenge. What it lacks is the imagination that is Myst's forte."[^ref-27] Marshall delivered the pointed criticism that "by the time you're finished, you feel like you've been doing homework instead of playing a game."[^ref-27]

Next Generation gave the game 2/5. Its reviewer allowed that "While all too Myst-like in design, Dynamix's RAMA manages to push past some of the inherent confines of its genre and provide players with some fairly balanced puzzles and a decently entertaining storyline," and praised the alien designs and musical score, but concluded he could not recommend the game because of its similarity to *Myst*.[^ref-2]

GameSpot's Stephen Poole awarded 6.9/10 (Fair), praising the game for adventure fans seeking challenges: "If what you're looking for in an adventure game is plenty of challenging puzzles, you'll be in high heaven with Rama" and "with its high production values and fidelity to the Rama books, this is an excellent choice for both adventure gamers and Arthur C. Clarke fans."[^ref-17]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have largely validated the game's quality while acknowledging its demanding nature. The Collection Chamber's Biffman 101 praised the production values, noting that "Live actors blend seamlessly with the CGI backgrounds and contain some of the best FMV performances of the era" and described it as "a high-end production conceived by some high-end novelists that require a high-end mind to complete."[^ref-4] However, the review concluded "I cannot recommend Rama to casual gamers, which is a shame as the story ramps up dramatically."[^ref-4]

Solo Gamer Reviews' e. magill, writing in 2018, observed that "despite being over twenty years old, it holds up surprisingly well and remains absolutely playable, as long as you can accept the more primitive 3D graphics and hokey Full Motion Video stuff."[^ref-18] The review noted it was "a more faithful adaptation than you'd expect, but most of the plot happens off-camera and gets confusing for anyone not familiar with the books."[^ref-18]

MobyGames user Greg Wells offered particular praise for the puzzle design: "The logic of this game is brilliant, from the puzzles themselves (some logical in the most direct manner, i.e. mathematical), to the way those puzzles relate to each other" and "More than any other game, of any genre, Rama manages to motivate the player, move its own modest plot and keep things interesting by simply providing a fascinating setting."[^ref-19] Wells also noted the demanding nature: "Rama is very hard, often assuming the player has general skills and knowledge not covered in most high school careers."[^ref-19] Another MobyGames user, Boston Low, concluded: "I think that Rama is a must-see game, which deserved to be more well known."[^ref-19]

Old PC Gaming's retrospective echoed the mathematical challenge: "Put your abstract thinking caps on – Rama isn't for the mathematically challenged" while concluding "if you're smart enough, enjoy a good story and are a fan of Arthur C. Clarke's works then this here's pretty much a five-star adventure game."[^ref-29]

## Development

### Origins

The genesis of the Rama video game came from Gentry Lee, who conceived the idea of adapting the novel series he had co-authored with Arthur C. Clarke into an interactive experience.[^ref-25] Lee's credentials extended far beyond fiction writing—he had served as chief NASA engineer for the Galileo Jupiter mission and director of mission planning for the Viking mission to Mars, bringing genuine scientific expertise to the project.[^ref-25]

Lee approached a skeptical Clarke with the idea and also took the proposal to Sierra, which "threw all its corporate resources behind the project."[^ref-25] Sierra acquired the rights to develop three games based on the Rama literary cycle, which had sold seven million copies across 18 languages.[^ref-5] Clarke became enthusiastic after seeing the preliminary artwork, exclaiming in the interview that comes with the game that "those animations were almost as good as those in Jurassic Park!"[^ref-25]

The project was developed at Dynamix, Sierra's subsidiary studio known for technical innovation and diverse game development.[^ref-1] Lee served as consultant for the writing and Clarke appeared as an actor.[^ref-5] PC Gamer UK noted that "Gentry Lee also had a hand in designing the game, and Clarke seems to have helped here and there."[^ref-26]

### Production

Development of Rama spanned approximately 18 months, with a team of 115 people contributing to the final product.[^ref-5] The project was directed by J. Mark Hood, who had previously served as project leader on Sierra's controversial FMV adventure Phantasmagoria.[^ref-5] The ambitious scope required a development budget of $2 million (10.3 million French francs), reflecting Sierra's confidence in the property.[^ref-5]

The visual design drew upon the talents of Richard Hescox, a renowned science fiction cover artist whose work had graced numerous novels in the genre.[^ref-5] Hescox worked on the game's 3,000 backgrounds (*décors*) covering the 4,000 km² of Rama that the player explores,[^ref-5] and PC Gamer UK describes the locations as "3D-Studio-generated."[^ref-26]

Blue screen filming for the live-action sequences took place in two locations: Seattle, Washington for the astronaut character scenes, and Colombo, Sri Lanka for Arthur C. Clarke's appearances.[^ref-10] Clarke's involvement went beyond mere cameos—his segments were filmed specifically to provide in-game guidance and commentary, creating a unique connection between the celebrated author and players experiencing his fictional universe.[^ref-6]

*Libération* reported that the game was rendered with a new 3D technology called "3-Space".[^ref-5]

### Technical Achievements

Rama uses Sierra's SCI32 interpreter, which, according to the Sierra Chest, "was for the first time applied to present a first-person point of view." It was released for both DOS and Windows.[^ref-10] Data Driven Gamer notes the game is made with SCI 3.0 and assumes the engine targeted Windows 95 as its primary platform.[^ref-9]

The visuals combine 3D-rendered scenery with live-action actors in 256 colours.[^ref-2] The Windows 95 version calls for local bus or PCI video.[^ref-16]

The soundtrack was composed by Charles Barth. Wikipedia notes that the game was praised for its music, and Next Generation's reviewer praised its musical score.[^ref-2] Balmoral Software found the "Music is above average, but rather repetitive," while praising "excellent stereo sound effects."[^ref-23]

### Cut Content

A sequel was planned, and the game hints at it; according to IMDb's trivia, work on the sequel began but the project was soon scrapped.[^ref-21] Clarke refers to a sequel at the end of the game, but it was never made.[^ref-10] By November 1997 Sierra Coktel admitted it was disappointed with Rama's sales and said there were no plans to repeat the experience.[^ref-41]

A third CD included with the game contained supplementary content including in-depth video interviews with Arthur C. Clarke and Gentry Lee, as well as an exclusive chapter from Clarke's then-upcoming novel *3001: The Final Odyssey*.[^ref-36]

The Cutting Room Floor documents unused content in the game files, including a line of text in 206.msg marked "*** Not used, according to randy - mgl".[^ref-37] The Mac application also contains seven Finder icons from Sierra's *Hoyle Classic Games*, including a Crazy Eights icon that went unused there too.[^ref-37]

### Technical Issues

The game exhibited numerous technical problems that affected player experience, particularly on systems meeting only minimum specifications. Reported issues included:[^ref-39]

- Game crashes occurring before animation sequences on minimum-spec systems
- Animations leaving colored pixels behind on screen
- Sound dropouts when interacting with game elements
- Audio halting completely after periods of inactivity
- Poor lip synchronization in video sequences
- Crashes triggered by using numeric digits, parentheses, or more than 19 characters in saved game names
- Slow performance when accessing saved game lists

A MobyGames reviewer reported that on a newer system the game only worked windowed with no sound, and "even that setup frequently reverts to a negative-image color-scheme."[^ref-19]

Sierra's installers proved problematic for emulation and compatibility layer software. A 2022 winevdm issue lists Rama among the Sierra games whose installers "have issues running with OTVDM."[^ref-40] Community-developed solutions emerged, including DOSBox configurations and ScummVM support, with The Collection Chamber releasing an updated package in September 2020 utilizing ScummVM instead of DOSBox.[^ref-4]

SquirtTheCat released an XP/Vista installer in 2008 that fixes exceptions and script errors under XP/Vista, scales the game to full screen with Direct3D bilinear interpolation, and stores save games per user to work under Vista.[^ref-38]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Arthur C. Clarke Death Scenes:** When players die during gameplay, Arthur C. Clarke appears and gives advice; Balmoral Software describes "an apology and warning from Clarke in a live-action video."[^ref-23] Clarke's appearances were described as humorous, with one example showing him "provoking a Biot into a fight."[^ref-2]
- **3001 Preview:** The third CD includes an exclusive chapter from Clarke's novel *3001: The Final Odyssey*, which was still forthcoming at the time of the game's release.[^ref-36]
- **Creator Cameo Data Cube:** Nicole des Jardins leaves the player a data cube with a snippet of *3001* on it;[^ref-35] Balmoral calls the note and datacube "shameless plugs for Clarke's upcoming book 3001."[^ref-23]
- **Hoyle Icons:** The Macintosh application contains seven Finder icons from Sierra's *Hoyle Classic Games*, including a Crazy Eights icon that wasn't used in Hoyle either.[^ref-37]
- **Compass Discrepancy:** In Clarke's novels, the North Pole serves as Rama's entry point with the South Pole housing the engines. The game reverses this, with the entry hub referred to as the "South" and confirmed by the in-game compass.[^ref-21]
- **Ramans Do Everything by Threes:** A key element from Clarke's novels is preserved in the game—PC Gamer US advises players to "always remember that the Ramans do everything by threes, since they have, among other characteristics, three eyes."[^ref-25]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sierra initially projected sales of 500,000 units within the first three months of release across five language versions.[^ref-5] However, actual performance fell significantly short of expectations. By November 1997, approximately one year after release, only 25,000 units had sold in France.[^ref-41] Computer Gaming World reported that the game "appears to be selling reasonably, but still isn't generating the kind of business Sierra sees from a new Leisure Suit Larry."[^ref-2]

Sierra Coktel said it was "déçu" (disappointed) with the sales, judging the game in hindsight somewhat "déroutant, pas violent, et un peu intello, voire plus difficile que Myst" (disconcerting, non-violent, a bit highbrow, even harder than *Myst*), and said there were no plans to repeat the experience.[^ref-41] Although the rights had been bought for three games,[^ref-5] no sequel was made.[^ref-10]

### Awards and Recognition

Rama received several award nominations recognizing its quality and ambition:[^ref-1][^ref-42]

- **Computer Gaming World Adventure Game of the Year** – Finalist
- **Computer Game Developers Conference 1996 'Best Adaptation of Linear Media' Spotlight Award** – Finalist
- **CNET Gamecenter 'Best Adventure Game'** – Finalist

### Collections

Rama was included in the *PC Gamer Editors' Choice Collection*,[^ref-10] and MobyGames also lists it in the *Fantasy Pack* (1998).[^ref-28] It is not sold on GOG.com; a GOG Dreamlist entry lets users vote for a re-release.[^ref-6]

### Fan Projects

The primary fan preservation effort came through The Collection Chamber, which released an updated package in September 2020 utilizing ScummVM for improved modern compatibility.[^ref-4] SquirtTheCat's 2008 XP/Vista installer fixed script errors and exceptions on those Windows versions.[^ref-38]

The soundtrack has been uploaded to soundtrack archives such as Khinsider.[^ref-22][^ref-34] A Stellaris Steam Workshop mod added the Rama soundtrack, crediting Ross Scott of Accursed Farms "for unearthing this gem"; Steam has since removed the item from the community.[^ref-43]

### Related Publications

- **Rama II (Novel):** Co-authored by Arthur C. Clarke and Gentry Lee, 1989. The primary source material for the game's characters and plot.[^ref-2]
- **Rendezvous with Rama (Novel):** Arthur C. Clarke's original 1973 novel establishing the Rama universe.[^ref-47]
- **Limited Edition Bundled Novel:** A limited edition of the game also included the novel.[^ref-10]
- **Third CD Supplementary Content:** Included video interviews with Arthur C. Clarke and Gentry Lee, plus an exclusive chapter from *3001: The Final Odyssey*.[^ref-36]
- **Development Interviews:** The third CD includes "some amusing video interviews with Clarke and Lee."[^ref-23]
