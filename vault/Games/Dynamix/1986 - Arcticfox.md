# Arcticfox

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 19, 2026</small>

## Overview

Arcticfox is a first-person science-fiction tank game developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Electronic Arts]]. It was written first for the Commodore Amiga, where MobyGames dates the US release to February 1986, and was then ported to the Apple II, Commodore 64, MS-DOS and Atari ST, with later versions for the PC-98, ZX Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and MSX.[^ref-1][^ref-2] The player drives a single experimental supertank into an alien-held zone of Antarctica, where invaders are converting Earth's oxygen into an atmosphere of their own, and must destroy the aliens' Main Fort before the air runs out.[^ref-3][^ref-4]

The game predates Sierra's 1990 purchase of Dynamix by four years, and Sierra never published it. Electronic Arts did, with Ariolasoft handling some European releases.[^ref-1][^ref-12] SierraVault covers it because the vault's inclusion criteria take in Dynamix's complete catalog. Arcticfox was the studio's first deal with Electronic Arts, and Jimmy Maher credits it with "establishing Dynamix's identity as a purveyor of cutting-edge 3D graphics".[^ref-6][^ref-7]

*Computer Gaming World*'s Roy Wagner called the Amiga version's sound and display "Sensational!", and a decade later the same magazine ranked it 138th in its "150 Best Games of All Time", describing it as "Damon Slye's update of Stellar 7 for the Amiga" and "the seminal 3D polygon-based shooter".[^ref-4][^ref-8] Several British reviewers were much harsher on the slower 8-bit conversions.[^ref-9][^ref-10]

## Story Summary

The manual, written by Nicholas Lavroff, sets the scene in a briefing from headquarters. An alien task force has been active in Antarctica since about 2005, but humanity learned of it only when a member of the "doomed Hagerty Expedition" escaped through the aliens' force field. The invaders, from a planet called STV-7X, have built oxygen converters to replace Earth's atmosphere with their own mix of ammonia, methane and chlorine, and the reaction is throwing off violent electrical storms.[^ref-3]

No weapon can break the force field directly, but a magnetic disturbance can weaken one side long enough to slip a single vehicle inside. That vehicle is "the new supertank from General Polemics, the Slye-Hicks MX-100, Codename: Arcticfox". Once inside, the operator loses radio contact with headquarters and has to work alone.[^ref-3] The goal is to destroy the Main Fort, which takes "two direct hits with missiles or ten direct hits with cannon shells".[^ref-3][^ref-4]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The whole game is played from the tank's cockpit. Wagner described a single tank armed with a 150mm cannon, a missile launcher and a mine dispenser, with radar and a turbine engine.[^ref-4]

- **Cannon:** fired with the joystick button. It reloads automatically between shots, which the manual warns can take up to five seconds.[^ref-3]
- **Guided missiles:** firing one switches the small viewport to the missile's nose camera, so the player can steer it "like a mini flight simulator" and use it for reconnaissance before locking it onto a target.[^ref-3][^ref-5]
- **Mines:** dropped from the rear of the tank. They are live, and they self-detonate after a time.[^ref-3]
- **Radar and aft view:** the small viewport toggles between an overhead radar, with north always at the top, and a rear camera.[^ref-3]
- **Cannon inclination and relative reverse:** the joystick can be switched to aim the gun up and down, and a reverse toggle keeps the tank moving while the gun is being aimed.[^ref-3]
- **Dig in:** the tank can bury itself in snow to hide from all but the most sophisticated radar.[^ref-3]
- **Instruments:** a three-colour warning light shows whether the enemy has spotted the tank locally or across the whole area, alongside readouts for remaining oxygen, compass heading, position, clock, speed and damage.[^ref-3]

Wagner found that the Amiga version's mouse option "did not work very well" on his copy and recommended a joystick.[^ref-4]

### Structure and Progression

There are four skill levels:[^ref-3]

- **Enemy Preview:** each alien unit parades past the cockpit with a label.
- **Training:** the player can roam and shoot freely with tougher armour and plenty of ammunition, but cannot finish the game. Wagner listed its optional aids: turbo speed, invisibility and indestructibility, a blizzard toggle, a larger radar map, a smart bomb and a missile-eye view.[^ref-4]
- **Beginner:** a real game with more supplies, tougher armour and weaker, fewer enemies.
- **Tournament:** the enemy is smarter and tracks the tank constantly. The player starts from one of eight preset locations and can choose the enemy configuration or let the computer pick it.

Time pressure comes from the oxygen readout. Destroying the aliens' Air Converters slows the conversion and buys time, and destroying the Communications Fort stops the enemy calling in strike forces.[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Arcticfox has no puzzles. Its challenge lies in terrain and tactics:

- **Terrain:** ridges give vantage points; crevices destroy the tank; mud flats make it slide; tundra halves speed for both sides. The force field shows on radar but not on the ground. According to the manual, every terrain feature except the "Pole of Relative Inaccessibility" is fictional, though "named after historic arctic explorers".[^ref-3]
- **Enemy units:** heavy and light tanks, recon sleds and recon flyers, fighters, rocket launchers, radar stations that can cloak other units and jam missiles, and floating mines that home in on the tank.[^ref-3]
- **Damage:** Antic's John Manor noted that when the tank is finally destroyed, the view window cracks and the game reports the score, the units destroyed and how the player died.[^ref-5]

Wagner reviewed the Amiga original in *Computer Gaming World*'s "Amiga Preferences" column. He called it "the first original new game from Electronic Arts (EA) that uses the distinct features of the Amiga (digitized stereo sound and fast graphics)", and singled out the sound of tank treads and of "the crunching compression" of wrecks under them.[^ref-4] He thought the game "feels slow", but put that down to realism, judging it "more of a tank simulation requiring strategic movement than a shoot-em-up arcade game".[^ref-4]

Robert J. Stumpf, writing in *Compute!*, praised the "3-D full-color graphics" and sound that changes "when you drive up a hill or over a destroyed enemy vehicle". He concluded that it "should appeal not only to those with lightning-quick reflexes and uncanny eyesight, but also to those with a taste for both strategy and action".[^ref-11]

The Atari ST version drew the strongest praise. John Manor of *Antic* opened: "If you could buy only one game for your Atari ST, it might be Arctic Fox", comparing it to Atari's *Battlezone* coin-op "but better in every way". His complaints were that the training mode ended too quickly and that "more could have been done with the ST's sound and music capabilities"; he closed, "This is a game you will return to play time and time again."[^ref-5]

British reviewers of the 8-bit versions were far less taken. *Zzap!64*'s team found the Commodore 64 version slow and sparse, complained that it was a multi-load, and pointed readers to Novagen's *Encounter* instead.[^ref-9] *Crash* criticised the ZX Spectrum version's sluggish controls and slow monochrome graphics, with one reviewer calling it "a very disappointing release from Electronic Arts"; its overall score was 41%.[^ref-10] *Sinclair User* summed it up as "Quite disappointing conversion. Fails to hold the interest", and *Your Sinclair* as a "Fairly dire conversion of a 3D tank game that could have had a lot going for it".[^ref-21][^ref-22] Wikipedia records a four-plus-star rating from *Info* for the Commodore 64 version, and a qualified verdict from Charles and Sydney Barouch of *Gateways*, who called it "very reminiscent of the arcade game *Battlezone*".[^ref-13]

### Modern Assessment

In November 1996 *Computer Gaming World* ranked the Amiga version 138th in its "150 Best Games of All Time": "Damon Slye's update of Stellar 7 for the Amiga was the seminal 3D polygon-based shooter." A Sierra advertising section in the same anniversary issue described Slye as having "honed his skills on 3D action games like Arcticfox, Skyfox and Stellar 7", although Ray Tobey wrote the original *Skyfox*; Dynamix ported it to the Amiga, Atari ST and Macintosh in 1986 and later made the sequel, *Skyfox II*.[^ref-8][^ref-18][^ref-23] Lemon64's aggregate of four Commodore 64 magazine reviews comes to 56%.[^ref-12]

## Development

### Origins

Damon Slye had written *Stellar 7* on the Apple II and co-founded Dynamix with Jeff Tunnell. Slye told Talkin' Games that EA producer Joe Ybarra had been talking to him since *Stellar 7*. Ybarra first had Dynamix pitch a movie-style game similar to *Karateka*, then, at the end of the meeting, said: "Nope, we want you to do a tank game instead!"[^ref-14] Ybarra told them about a new computer that EA could not yet name. "So, that was where we came up with the idea for Arcticfox, which was more epic", Slye said. In his view *Stellar 7* had been "just seven arcade levels", while Arcticfox aimed for "real epic and repeatable gameplay".[^ref-14]

Jimmy Maher writes that Dynamix's experience with the C language gave it "an in with Electronic Arts", which needed developers for the Amiga before it launched. Slye began writing Arcticfox "on an Amiga that didn't even have a functioning operating system", compiling on an IBM PC and sending the code over by cable.[^ref-6]

### Production

Slye told Gamasutra in 2010 that "EA funded Arcticfox for $35,000", with milestone payments, and that the target was to finish "shortly after the Amiga launched so we would be a launch title".[^ref-7] Richard Hicks, Dynamix's third partner, set up the development environment: "a C compiler, linker, and cross development from the PC to the Amiga". Kevin Ryan "handled all the graphics, sounds, and Amiga specific interfaces".[^ref-7] Slye later recalled one week on the project when he "worked 105 hours" and slept at the office.[^ref-15]

The package credits list programmers Damon Slye, Richard Hicks, Jeff Tunnell and Kevin Ryan; artwork by John Burton; world creation by Paul Bowman; and producer Joe Ybarra. John Mattos did the package illustrations.[^ref-3][^ref-2] The package pictures Kevin Ryan, Jeff Tunnell, Damon Slye and Richard Hicks and gives Dynamix's motto, "We build Worlds". It says they built the game's three-dimensional world "with a proprietary graphics package called 3-Space", and that the Frisbee golf course next to their office in Eugene, Oregon kept them "sane during the fast-paced development of this program".[^ref-3]

### Technical Specifications

- **Graphics:** solid-filled 3D on the Amiga. Maher contrasts these "full-color solids" with *Stellar 7*'s monochrome wire-frame. The Commodore 64 and ZX Spectrum versions used wire-frame vector graphics.[^ref-6][^ref-9][^ref-10]
- **MS-DOS:** 256 KB RAM; CGA, Hercules and Tandy graphics; PC speaker; keyboard or analog joystick; 3.5" and 5.25" disks.[^ref-1]
- **Apple II:** Apple II+ or later, 64 KB, DOS 3.3, 5.25" disk.[^ref-1]
- **ZX Spectrum:** 48K and 128K, tape and disk.[^ref-1][^ref-16]
- **Prices:** $39.95 for the Amiga and Atari ST versions in the US. In the UK, £9.95 cassette and £14.95 disk for the Commodore 64, and £12.99 cassette and £14.99 disk for the Spectrum.[^ref-11][^ref-5][^ref-12][^ref-10]

## Legacy

Arcticfox sold well by the standards of the day, though the two figures on record differ. Maher puts it at "over 100,000 copies in all"; Slye told Talkin' Games it "sold, across all different computers, about 150,000 units".[^ref-6][^ref-17] Maher writes that its success led to "many more 3D blast-em games" from Dynamix: *Skyfox II*, *F-14 Tomcat*, *Abrams Battle Tank*, *MechWarrior*, *Deathtrack* and *A-10 Tank Killer*.[^ref-6]

Slye also described the limits of the deal. Advance-and-royalty terms left developers "captive", though he said this "wasn't a problem particular to Electronic Arts" but one "all developers were experiencing". EA also soon lost interest in the Amiga: Slye recalled a letter from Trip Hawkins telling developers, "if you know 6502, start coding."[^ref-17] When Sierra agreed to buy Dynamix in March 1990, *Computer Gaming World*'s news report listed Arcticfox first among the games Dynamix had built for Electronic Arts.[^ref-18] Wikipedia notes it as one of the first games for the Amiga.[^ref-19]
