# Deathtrack

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 19, 2026</small>

## Overview

Deathtrack (styled *DeathTrack* in its packaging and reviews) is a first-person vehicular combat racing game for MS-DOS, developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Activision]] in December 1989.[^ref-1][^ref-2] The player is a rookie in a future American racing circuit where drivers arm their cars with missiles, lasers, mines and spikes. Prize money from a circuit of ten tracks pays for repairs and upgrades.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

The game comes from Dynamix's independent years, after it left Electronic Arts and began working with Activision, and it predates Sierra's purchase of Dynamix in 1990. Sierra never published it.[^ref-4][^ref-12][^ref-5] SierraVault covers it because the vault's inclusion criteria take in Dynamix's complete catalog.

*Computer Gaming World*'s Chris Lombardi called it "an outstanding new action game" and "gratuitous violence at its therapeutic best".[^ref-3] In 1996 the same magazine ranked it 124th in its "150 Best Games of All Time", calling it "this seminal car-to-car combat simulation".[^ref-6]

## Story Summary

There is no story beyond the premise. MobyGames summarizes it: "In the future, races are held across America where the drivers race for money, and their lives. You are the rookie."[^ref-1] *Computer Gaming World* described the field as ten other drivers "whose minds are as twisted as their own".[^ref-3]

The manual is written in the voice of the sport's own media, opening with a commentator's column, "From the Pit: DeathTrack for Beginners", and filling its margins with profiles of the drivers: their home towns, favourite weapons, average eliminations per year and favourite foods.[^ref-9] The rival drivers include Slye, Motor Maniac, Wrecker, Melissa, Megadeath, Killer Angel, Lurker Logan, Preying Menace and Crimson Death, shown in digitized photographs of a credited cast. Dynamix co-founder Damon Slye appears as "Slye".[^ref-7][^ref-3]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

- **View:** a cockpit view of 3D polygon cars and tracks, with city backdrops behind some tracks. *Computer Gaming World* pointed to the Golden Gate behind San Francisco and skyscrapers behind New York.[^ref-3][^ref-1]
- **Instruments:** fuel, damage and speed gauges; an overhead track map with the player's car in red; a panel showing the driver in weapons range and his or her place; and a position and lap counter.[^ref-3] *The Games Machine* also listed a rear-view mirror and an autopilot indicator.[^ref-11]
- **Autopilot:** the manual warns that it "doesn't drive fast and it doesn't drive well".[^ref-9]
- **Input:** joystick, keyboard or mouse.[^ref-1][^ref-9] *Computer Gaming World* recommended a joystick.[^ref-3]
- **Detail control:** the + and − keys raise or lower screen detail, trading detail for a faster frame rate.[^ref-9]

### Structure and Progression

The player chooses one of three cars, the fast Hellcat, the all-round Crusher or the heavily armoured Pitbull, and starts with $10,000 to spend.[^ref-9][^ref-2] The menus offer a single practice track or a "Full Circuit Run" through all ten tracks. The player can set the number of laps and a difficulty level "from punk to megadestroyer", and save a circuit in progress.[^ref-9]

Money is the core of the game. *Computer Gaming World* reported prize money from $13,000 for first place and $9,500 for second down to $250 for ninth. A "shady character" sometimes offers $2,000 to $7,000 to eliminate a particular driver. A wrecked car brings a huge repair bill, and a player who cannot afford a race-worthy car is forced out of the circuit.[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Deathtrack has no puzzles. Strategy lies in the shops:

- **Parts shop:** engine, brakes, transmission, tires, airfoils and armour, each in three grades.[^ref-3][^ref-9] Used parts cannot be traded in.[^ref-9]
- **Weapons shop:** nine weapon types in three grades each. The manual lists caltrops, lasers, guns, mines, missiles, particle beams, ram spikes, "Terminators" and wheel spikes. Old weapons can be traded in against new ones.[^ref-9][^ref-3]
- **Copy protection:** each start asks a question about one of the drivers, answered from the manual. A wrong answer plays a short demonstration and returns to DOS.[^ref-9][^ref-3]

Lombardi found that buying a large engine left a car "weak in armor", while heavy armour and weapons made it "slow, unmaneuverable, and at the rear of the pack". He also advised that "one really need not graduate beyond the middle-sized engine because the largest engine simply goes too fast".[^ref-3]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Date | Verdict |
|-------------|------|---------|
| *Computer Gaming World* (Chris A. Lombardi) | February 1990 | Strongly positive[^ref-3] |
| *Compute!* (Denny Atkin) | March 1990 | Positive[^ref-8] |
| *The Games Machine* | March 1990 | 82%[^ref-11] |
| *Computer Gaming World* readers' Top 100 | May 1990 | 49th[^ref-5] |

Lombardi's review, "Murder on the Backstretch", opened with a confession about commuting rage on the Santa Monica freeway. He praised the cars' "very realistic feel", especially when airborne after a jump, and the sense of speed that he thought most racing games lacked.[^ref-3] His one technical complaint was that passing cars could collide and "merge together so that they are sort of inside each other". He concluded: "Deathtrack is excellent, a must for action gamers who want high speed action, quick decision-making, and strategic planning engineered into a high-performance package destined for the winner's circle. Also, for anyone on the brink of traffic induced lunacy, it is gratuitous violence at its therapeutic best."[^ref-3]

Denny Atkin of *Compute!* called it "sort of a combination of a Grand Prix race and a Rambo movie". He rated the graphics "excellent", with cars that show dents when rammed, and wrote that "Screen updates are incredibly fast on a turbo XT or AT." His conclusion: "This game is just plain fun. And it's excellent therapy after a long freeway commute."[^ref-8]

*The Games Machine* gave the PC version 82%. Its reviewer's only real problem was "the enormous amount of dials that have to be looked after", and he added: "this is probably the most fun I've had playing a PC game for a long while."[^ref-11]

### Modern Assessment

*Computer Gaming World*'s November 1996 list of the "150 Best Games of All Time" placed Deathtrack 124th: "Dynamix developed this seminal car-to-car combat simulation where Car Wars-style action had become a national sport."[^ref-6] Jimmy Maher lists it among the 3D games that built Dynamix's name in the late 1980s.[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

Wikipedia's history of Dynamix notes that after its action games for Penguin Software and Electronic Arts, "Later titles were developed for Activision."[^ref-16] A 1997 *Computer Gaming World* history of the BattleTech licence describes the same moment: "Dynamix was defecting from EA and began building the 3Space engine", and Activision struck a deal to use that technology for *MechWarrior*.[^ref-17] Damon Slye told Talkin' Games that Dynamix "had done a deal with Activision to do five games for them". He also recalled that Dynamix hired Terry Ishida, a producer from Activision, who took over *MechWarrior* and *V-22 Osprey* from him. The four games Slye said he was then directing were *A-10 Tank Killer*, *David Wolf*, *MechWarrior* and the unreleased *V-22 Osprey*; he did not mention Deathtrack.[^ref-12] Deathtrack's credits list Ishida as co-designer with Dariusz Lukaszuk, and Steven Ackrich and John A. S. Skeel as producers.[^ref-7]

### Production

The manual's "Creators of DeathTrack" page names Tom Collie, Mark Brenneman, Darek Lukaszuk and Bryce Morsello (credited in MobyGames as Morcello),[^ref-7] and describes the division of labour: "Darek wrote the code, Tom did the art and Bryce sound effects, and Mark built all the 3-space objects. Jeff got us pizza late nights and ran errands (he was also company president in his spare time)."[^ref-9] Its "pit crew" thanks Piotr Lukaszuk "for fast graphics", Dave McClurg "for 3-space etc." and Paul Bowman "for playing foosball with us", and the game is "Dedicated to the memory of Enzo Ferrari".[^ref-9]

MobyGames' archived credits add art by Kobi Miller, Alan J. Murphy, Steve Snyder and Ron Winnick; music by Russell Lieblich; photography by Randy Dersham; casting and make-up by Sher Alltucker; and direction by Jeffrey Tunnell.[^ref-7] *Computer Gaming World* credited Dynamix's "'3-space' poly-filled graphics system", already used in *David Wolf: Secret Agent* and *MechWarrior*.[^ref-3]

### Technical Specifications

- **Requirements:** 8088 or better, DOS 2.0, 512 KB RAM.[^ref-1][^ref-8]
- **Graphics:** CGA, EGA, Hercules and Tandy. *Compute!* noted that CGA runs in high-resolution black and white, while EGA and Tandy are full colour.[^ref-1][^ref-8]
- **Sound:** PC speaker, AdLib and Roland MT-32.[^ref-1]
- **Media:** 3.5" and 5.25" floppy disks.[^ref-1]
- **Price:** $44.95 in the US; £24.99 in the UK.[^ref-3][^ref-8][^ref-11]

## Legacy

When *Computer Gaming World* reported Sierra's purchase of Dynamix in May 1990, it listed "F-14 Tomcat, MechWarrior and DeathTrack for Activision" among Dynamix's work. The same issue's readers' Top 100 had Deathtrack at 49th.[^ref-5] Unseen64 documents a cancelled Game Boy version, *Trail Blazer*, which Argonaut developed under licence from Activision.[^ref-14]

Nearly two decades later, the Russian studio SkyFallen Entertainment made a sequel, *Death Track: Resurrection*, published by 1C Company in Russia in February 2008 and later by 505 Games and Aspyr Media.[^ref-15]
