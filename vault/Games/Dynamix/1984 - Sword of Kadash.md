# Sword of Kadash

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Sword of Kadash is an action role-playing game written for the Apple II by Chris Cole, a teenager who frequented The Computer Tutor, Jeff Tunnell's computer store in Eugene, Oregon.[^ref-1][^ref-3] Its release year is disputed: the CRPG Addict, Retro Games Trove and Giant Bomb date it to 1984 (Lemon64 also lists 1984 for the C64 version), while MobyGames, Retro365, the Commodore Wiki and the Sierra Fandom wiki give 1985 for the Apple II original.[^ref-6][^ref-5][^ref-12][^ref-16][^ref-2][^ref-4][^ref-1][^ref-7] The cover art is signed "Wedemeyer '84".[^ref-23] The game was published by Penguin Software, with a Commodore 64 version in 1985 and Atari ST and Macintosh versions following in 1986; the Macintosh edition was published by Polarware.[^ref-2][^ref-9] The Digital Antiquarian describes it as "an adventure/CRPG/platformer hybrid," and Cole himself later called it "a genre-buster."[^ref-3][^ref-6]

Magazines of the time praised it as an original action-adventure, and it was notorious for its difficulty.[^ref-4][^ref-12] Set in the deserts of Persia, it sends the player through a dungeon of roughly 250 rooms filled with traps, monsters and hidden passages.[^ref-5][^ref-9] Players are, in the CRPG Addict's words, "manifestly meant to make maps, take notes, and through a long process of dying and learning, eventually plot a path through the game to the end."[^ref-6]

Sword of Kadash was Dynamix's second game, released while the young company was signing whatever contracts it could find to stay afloat; [[Dynamix]] later became known for its flight simulators and adventure games and was acquired by Sierra On-Line in 1990.[^ref-6][^ref-3][^ref-7]

## Story Summary

The narrative of Sword of Kadash places players in the role of a fortune-seeker who has traveled from the Mediterranean to the deserts of Persia in search of wealth.[^ref-9] Betrayed and captured by brigands, the hero is spared only on condition that he enter the Fortress of the Dragon and return with the Sword of Kadash.[^ref-9]

The Sword of Kadash itself is an enchanted two-handed weapon once wielded by a Templar Knight during the Crusades.[^ref-10] It is said that the sword is powered by the blood of those it slew during that era, and those who use it are susceptible to its enchantment.[^ref-10] The weapon lies deep within the dungeons of the Fortress of the Dragon, guarded by a powerful Liche.[^ref-10]

A dragon named Aladag guards the sword.[^ref-11] The dungeon is full of skeletons, demons, bats and other creatures; Giant Bomb calls the game "an incredibly difficult action RPG."[^ref-12] The Persian setting was a late change: Chris Cole had intended a high-fantasy theme, but someone at Penguin Software associated "Kadash" with the ancient city of Kadesh and insisted on a Persian framing story.[^ref-1][^ref-6]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Sword of Kadash is a top-down game with real-time combat.[^ref-9] The Atari ST version supports keyboard, joystick or mouse control.[^ref-8] The primary attack is firing "darts" in three-round bursts, and only three darts can be fired at a time.[^ref-6][^ref-9] Darts ricochet off some walls and are absorbed by, or blast holes in, others; the CRPG Addict describes banking them off walls.[^ref-9][^ref-6]

There is no character creation process, just a choice among three difficulty levels.[^ref-6] Every game begins the same way: the player starts at the entrance with a dagger, 2000 hit points, armour and fear spells.[^ref-14] Because the game is deterministic, victory comes through accumulated knowledge of the dungeon rather than chance.[^ref-6][^ref-14]

### Structure and Progression

The CRPG Addict counted 256 discrete rooms in the dungeon; Retro Games Trove gives the figure as 250.[^ref-6][^ref-5] AtariCrypt praised the "freedom to roam without following any linear path."[^ref-14] Its look recalled earlier games of the type: AtariCrypt was reminded of the Apshai games by its modest visuals and top-down display, and the CRPG Addict compared its basic interface to Caverns of Freitag, Sword of Fargoal and Gateway to Apshai.[^ref-11][^ref-6]

Progression comes from collecting better equipment, including daggers, morning stars, axes and swords.[^ref-14] Armour upgrades can raise the player's armour class to a maximum of 50.[^ref-5] Experience raises the character's level; the Retro Games Trove reviewer was level 66 when he defeated the dragon and lich and recovered the sword.[^ref-5] Cole says he deliberately set out to make a game that required an accumulation of experience to finish: "I meant to make a tough game."[^ref-6] AtariCrypt felt the design "seems focused on replayability, meaning you'll die often, encouraging you to take notes."[^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The gameplay emphasizes exploration and survival over traditional puzzle-solving. Many rooms contain traps and hidden passages that must be discovered through experimentation.[^ref-14] Cursed items appear throughout the dungeon, adding risk to treasure collection.[^ref-13] As AtariCrypt put it, "Sword of Kadash has a cruel design to be learned by the player bit by bit."[^ref-11]

Death is costly: a character who dies starts over at Level 1 with a sword and shield, which the CRPG Addict calls "not a survivable condition in the late game."[^ref-6] A fan-written solution posted to the Atari SIG in 1990 advises: "Your best chance lies in paper and pen. Map well, my fool!"[^ref-15] The CRPG Addict observed that "victory almost always comes through experience rather than skill alone."[^ref-6]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

According to the CRPG Addict and Retro365, magazines at the time praised Sword of Kadash and called it an original action-adventure.[^ref-6][^ref-4] No specific contemporary review has been located for this page.

### Modern Assessment

The CRPG Addict's 2016 retrospective gave the game a final rating of 18 on his GIMLET scale and rated its difficulty "Hard (4/5)". He criticized its heavy reliance on trial and error while acknowledging that players are "manifestly meant to make maps, take notes, and through a long process of dying and learning, eventually plot a path through the game to the end."[^ref-6]

User reception has been more generous, with community members on Lemon64 expressing fondness for the title.[^ref-16] One user called it "Fantastic game. One giant dungeon to explore. Incredibly hard due mostly to some poor gameplay quirks, but still an excellent game."[^ref-16] Another noted it was "Very dependent on trial and error, but compelling somehow. Lots of secrets so learning the dungeon is part of the strategy."[^ref-16]

## Development

### Origins

The creation of Sword of Kadash began with a challenge at The Computer Tutor, the Eugene, Oregon computer store where Cole and his friends spent their time.[^ref-1][^ref-3] Chris Cole began development in 1983, at 15, after growing frustrated with the quality of commercial software available at the time.[^ref-4] As Cole recalled: "We didn't have any money and $30 was a big deal for us. We felt burned when we got a bad one...and we seemed to get burned a lot. So I started mouthing off that I could write a better game in two weeks."[^ref-1][^ref-6]

Jeff Tunnell, who ran The Computer Tutor and founded Dynamix, threw down the gauntlet: if Cole thought he could create a better game than what was available on store shelves, Tunnell would publish it.[^ref-1] Cole was 15 when he started work on the game and 17 when he finished it.[^ref-6]

### Production

Cole was influenced by the action games Berzerk and Robotron: 2084, as well as Caverns of Freitag and tabletop Dungeons & Dragons.[^ref-1][^ref-6] Cole wrote the game's interface, and his friends Paul Bowman, Damon Slye and Bryce Morsello helped design most of the rooms.[^ref-1]

Cole had hoped that Electronic Arts would publish the game, but Tunnell's relationship with EA was strained at the time, so Penguin Software was approached instead.[^ref-4][^ref-6] Penguin insisted on changing the game's setting from fantasy to a Persian theme, quite late in development.[^ref-4] Cole thought it a bad marketing decision: "I tried, unsuccessfully as a 16-year-old, to have them change the setting back to high fantasy."[^ref-6]

### Technical Achievements

AtariCrypt singled out the walls as a mechanic: blue walls can be destroyed to create a new pathway, while green walls repel all shots.[^ref-14] The Atari ST conversion was done by Kenneth Hurley, who said: "It only took 3 months to convert it to the Atari ST."[^ref-19]

### Technical Issues

The game was intentionally unforgiving, and one Lemon64 user blamed its difficulty mostly on "some poor gameplay quirks."[^ref-16] One user on Atarimania asked: "Is there a version out there that features a trainer for inf hit points?"[^ref-8]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game contains many hidden passages that reward careful exploration.[^ref-14]
- Cole named the game after a girl: "I had a teenage crush on a girl in my school whose name was 'kinda' like 'Kadash.'"[^ref-6]
- Kenneth Hurley, who ported the game to the Atari ST, went to work for Central Point Software right after finishing the conversion.[^ref-19]
- Chris Cole was 17 years old when the game was finally completed and published.[^ref-1]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sales figures are not publicly documented, but Sword of Kadash earned enough to cover Chris Cole's living expenses through five years of undergraduate study and two years of graduate school.[^ref-4] Cole estimated his royalties at perhaps $25,000 to $30,000 in total.[^ref-6] Cole was somewhat disappointed with Penguin Software's marketing and distribution.[^ref-4]

### Place in Dynamix History

Sword of Kadash belongs to Dynamix's early catalog, made years before Sierra On-Line acquired the company in 1990.[^ref-7] It dates from a period when Tunnell "signed contracts wherever he could find them to keep his head above water."[^ref-3]

### Fan Projects

AtariCrypt made a map of the dungeon for modern players to use as a guide.[^ref-14] The game is preserved and playable through Virtual Apple and the Internet Archive.[^ref-22][^ref-20]

### Related Publications

- **Game Solution/Walkthrough:** A room-by-room fan solution by Len Stys (1990), archived by Atari Freenet[^ref-15]
- **Walkthrough by Keighn:** An incomplete walkthrough published on GameFAQs in 2016[^ref-21]

### Critical Perspective

Retro Games Trove places the game among the founders of the action RPG: "The Tower of Druaga, Courageous Perseus, Hydlide and Sword of Kadash heralded what would become an entirely new subgenre."[^ref-5] Cole himself saw it as mixing "legitimate elements of RPG, arcade, adventure, and maybe even a little open world."[^ref-6]

The title is also a snapshot of early-1980s home-computer development. Dynamix grew out of a Eugene computer shop, and the Digital Antiquarian calls Kadash the work of "another kid from The Computer Tutor."[^ref-3] Chris Cole returned to Dynamix in 1991.[^ref-4]

A Lemon64 user called it "a bit repetitious" and "kinda pointless," though it still has fans.[^ref-16] One MyAbandonware commenter wrote: "my favorite game on the c64. i still have my disk copy, but no drive to read it from."[^ref-18]
