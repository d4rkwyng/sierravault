# Stellar-Fire

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Stellar-Fire is a first-person hovercraft combat game in the Stellar 7 series, released for the Sega CD in late 1993; GamePro's February 1994 review listed it as "Available December"[^ref-18][^ref-16]. MobyGames credits Infinite Laser Dog as the developer and Dynamix as the publisher[^ref-2]. Period coverage presented it as a Dynamix production: Sierra's InterAction magazine called it "the first title Dynamix created especially for Sega CD," and both GamePro and Electronic Gaming Monthly credited the game to Dynamix[^ref-17][^ref-18][^ref-19]. The game continues the series' war against the Draxon, putting the player in the experimental Raven hovermorph as the last surviving pilot of an attack squadron[^ref-13][^ref-2].

Set in the year 2206, Stellar-Fire uses 3-D polygon graphics that run on the standard Sega CD hardware, a CD audio soundtrack, and a full-motion video introduction narrated by Star Trek: The Next Generation actor Michael Dorn[^ref-16][^ref-3]. Players cross the surfaces of five Draxon moons collecting Moon Crystals before fighting each moon's Guardian, then assault the Draxon homeworld of Arctura[^ref-13]. Reviews were mixed: magazines of the day praised the sound and the opening cinematic but found the shooting action thin, and later reviewers have been harsher[^ref-18][^ref-19][^ref-3][^ref-10].

## Story Summary

The manual sets the game in 2206. The Draxon, "a cybernetically linked race," seek to absorb the sentient life of the galaxy and "have conquered over ten-thousand worlds"; having shattered most of Earth's fleets, they are now advancing on Earth itself[^ref-13]. A last-ditch squadron of Earth's best remaining pilots is sent to strike at the Draxon home world, Arctura. The player flies a craft built by the secret Raven Projects, alongside wingmates Raela and Kritek[^ref-13].

The plan is to slip through a weak shield at Arctura's outermost moon, Xarz Voor, and work inward moon by moon, disabling each layer of the shield around the home world[^ref-13]. The mission goes wrong in the asteroid field: Draxon interceptors kill Kritek, and a Draxon cruiser destroys Raela's ship, leaving the player as the only pilot left[^ref-13]. This setup is told in the game's FMV introduction, which Michael Dorn narrates[^ref-3][^ref-9]. MobyGames describes the craft as a "hovermorph" that serves as a starfighter in space and transforms into a hovership when it approaches a moon's surface[^ref-2].

Each moon generates part of the shield around Arctura, so the player must defeat every moon in turn to reach the home world, where "whatever it is that binds the Draxon together" must be destroyed[^ref-13].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Stellar-Fire is played from a first-person view inside the Raven's cockpit[^ref-2]. The manual's cockpit indicators include a radar (enemies in red, power-ups in yellow, obstacles in blue), a Crystal Compass that points to the next Moon Crystal, cannon and laser strength bars, a Fat Boy counter, shield status, an impact detector, score, lives remaining, and a Guardian Meter that appears during boss fights[^ref-13]. Sega-16's reviewer said he was "at a loss as to where to go" until he read the manual and understood the gauges[^ref-3].

Button A fires the cannon (slower but stronger), button B the laser (faster but weaker), and button C the Fat Boy smart bomb; the D-pad's up and down set speed and its left and right turn the Raven[^ref-13]. Weapons fire only forward, and the Raven cannot strafe[^ref-8]. GamePro noted that the Raven "doesn't move quickly enough, nor does it go in reverse or spin sharply"[^ref-18]. Because of these limits, one 2024 reviewer fought bosses by "staying stationary in front of the boss, as far away as possible while it was visible," and moving only to dodge secondary attacks[^ref-8].

### Structure and Progression

The game has six stages: five moons and Arctura itself[^ref-13][^ref-18]. On each moon the player follows the Crystal Compass, collects the Moon Crystals, then blasts the Guardian Crystal to release that moon's Guardian, who must be defeated before the Raven can move on[^ref-13].

**Stages** (order and terrain from the manual's map)[^ref-13]:

- **Xarz Voor:** The outermost moon, where the attack begins
- **Zoreq:** Spire terrain
- **Nibor-Fren:** An ice moon, where the Raven slides on the slick surface[^ref-16]
- **Ta Eliab Vee:** A fire moon
- **Mok Zoh:** A desert moon, and the last before Arctura
- **Arctura:** The Draxon home world, with high-tech terrain

Sierra's own InterAction preview gave a different order and called Zoreq "the first moon you will encounter"[^ref-16]. The manual and GamePro's review both have the attack begin on Xarz Voor[^ref-13][^ref-18].

When the Raven reaches each new moon, a spoken mission briefing introduces the moon's enemies. The player can cycle through enemy profiles and tips or skip the briefing entirely[^ref-1]. The Video Game Critic noted that "the lady's voice" delivers the first mission briefing[^ref-10].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Each stage follows the radar's pointer to the next crystal while a steady stream of ground and air enemies attacks[^ref-8][^ref-2]. The landscape is effectively endless, but each moon looks the same throughout[^ref-9]. The manual lists power-ups for weapon strength, stacked cannon shells, double shots, 25 seeking shells, zig-zag lasers, extra Fat Boys, damage repair, and extra lives[^ref-13]. It also notes that some ground aliens, such as Zoreq's Gyrosled, leave Damage Repair power-ups when killed[^ref-13]. One player found the off-path power-up caches necessary only on Ta Eliab Vee and Arctura[^ref-8]. Without strong weapons, boss fights can take a long time to "whittle-down their life bars"[^ref-5].

The manual lists three difficulty levels: easy, normal, and difficult. On easy, obstacles do no damage, aliens fire less often, and shield power-ups are more frequent. Difficult "reeks of absolute death and is intended only for the truly insane"[^ref-13]. The player starts with two extra lives and gets 3, 2 or 1 continues depending on difficulty[^ref-13]. Sega-16's reviewer complained that dying "anywhere on the stage" sends the player back to the start of that stage[^ref-3].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

**GamePro** (#55, February 1994, p. 78) reviewed it as a Sega CD "ProReview" by Captain Squideo, scoring Graphics 3.5, Sound 4.0, Control 3.0 and FunFactor 3.5. The verdict: "This eagerly awaited CD doesn't quite deliver the intense, varied shooting action everyone had hoped for." GamePro praised the "great opening sequence" and sounds that were "consistently strong," but criticized the stiff controls and the "ineffectual Laser and Cannon." It concluded: "It's still a good shooter, just not a space ace."[^ref-18]

**Electronic Gaming Monthly** (#55, p. 42, "Major Mike's Game Roundup") gave scores of 6, 7, 4, 4 and 6 from its five reviewers, calling it "a first-person perspective shooter that looks a little like Battlezone, but doesn't really hack it. The sounds are alright and the cinemas are dynamite, but they are so good that they make the game a real letdown."[^ref-19]

**Game Players** (#40, May 1994, p. 124, "Now Playing" capsule of its earlier review) gave it 82%, citing "lots of variety from planet to planet, a sneaky bunch of bosses and hot music." It added that "the game is hard and even the easy mode has enough enemies to keep you busy."[^ref-20]

Sierra's own **InterAction** magazine (Holiday 1993, p. 50) ran a promotional feature by Barbara Ray. It highlighted the 3-D polygon graphics running on "the hardware found inside every Sega CD console" and the "killer techno-rock/industrial soundtrack," and closed with "Looks like Dynamix has another hit on its hands!"[^ref-16] As a house magazine, InterAction was promoting its own product, not reviewing it.

### Modern Assessment

A 2002 GameFAQs user review called it "perhaps the coolest Sega CD game I've ever played." It scored graphics 6, sound 9, story 6 and concept 6, with a 7 overall, and wrote of the soundtrack: "if you love base [sic], you'll love this soundtrack"[^ref-9].

Sega-16's 2009 review by Doug Jackson scored it 4 out of 10. It found the gameplay "too vague to make it last long enough" and wrote that "every objective is the same for each stage, and each one feels like a more difficult repeat of the last stage except with new backgrounds"[^ref-3]. The Video Game Critic gave it a grade of F in 2018: "Stellar-Fire does not live up to its name"[^ref-10]. A 2024 review on Blaqthourne's site rated it 4/10 ("I beat it on Easy, and nothing about it made me want to try the higher difficulties") and said that, compared with Nova 9, "it came well short"[^ref-8].

A 2025 MobyGames user review, titled "Better Than the Reviews Would Have You Think," praised the "early polygon graphics" as "quite impressive back in 1993" and called it a "rockin' soundtrack that deserves more attention"[^ref-5]. A 2016 retail customer review said "the soundtracks on this game make it worth playing in my opinion"[^ref-11].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 6.0/10 player score; critic average 52% (10 ratings)[^ref-2]
- **The Video Game Critic:** F[^ref-10]

## Development

### Origins

Stellar-Fire belongs to the Stellar 7 series, which began with the 1983 Stellar 7 and continued through Arcticfox, the 1990 Stellar 7 remake, Nova 9: The Return of Gir Draxon and the 3DO game Stellar 7: Draxon's Revenge[^ref-12]. InterAction described it as "based on Damon Slye's classic space shooter Stellar 7"[^ref-17], and GamePro noted its "stellar heritage (it's based on an acclaimed PC game)"[^ref-18].

MobyGames lists Infinite Laser Dog as developer and Dynamix as publisher, and Sega-16 and GameFAQs list the same split[^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-9]. At the time, Sierra's InterAction called Stellar-Fire "the first title Dynamix created especially for Sega CD" and credited "the techno-wizards at Dynamix" and "the sensational artistic talents at Dynamix," while GamePro and EGM attributed the game to Dynamix[^ref-17][^ref-16][^ref-18][^ref-19]. The Sierra Chest also lists Dynamix as developer[^ref-7]. The sources do not explain how the work was divided between Infinite Laser Dog and Dynamix. IMDb credits John Garvin as writer[^ref-6].

### Production

InterAction emphasized that the game's 3-D polygon graphics needed no extra chip: "Stellar Fire, however, makes use of the hardware found inside every Sega CD console"[^ref-16]. The full-motion video introduction, which GamePro called a "great opening sequence," mixes footage of actors in a cockpit with computer-generated space battles[^ref-18][^ref-10]. Michael Dorn, Worf on Star Trek: The Next Generation, narrates the opening cinematic[^ref-3][^ref-9]. A GameFAQs trivia note calls it "a bit part" that was "still promoted on the back of the box"[^ref-9].

**Development Credits:**

- **Team (MobyGames):** Randy Thompson, John Garvin, Rhett Anderson, Robert Kraft, Christopher Stevens, Tim Midkiff, Julie Anderson, Ken Rogers, Gregg Giles, Peter Lewis, Ron Clayborn, Damon Mitchell, David McClurg, Gerald Azenaro[^ref-21]
- **Writer:** John Garvin[^ref-6]
- **Music and Sound:** Ken Rogers, Christopher Stevens. The Dynamix Wiki gives them this role; MobyGames lists both in the team[^ref-1][^ref-21]
- **Narrator:** Michael Dorn[^ref-21][^ref-6]

### Technical Achievements

InterAction promoted the game's use of "3-D polygon technology to create a hyper-realistic 3-D environment" on stock Sega CD hardware[^ref-16]. A player review describes the graphics as simple 3-D geometric figures, often "different shade[s] of one color," and notes that "some enemies are simply wire frame (you can see right through them)"[^ref-9]. Sega-16 likewise found the enemies and bosses "done with simple wire frame builds and in limited colors"[^ref-3]. GamePro singled out the windshield cracks after collisions and the "splat" when a flying enemy hits the glass[^ref-18].

The soundtrack is on CD audio tracks that can be played in a standard CD player[^ref-2][^ref-9][^ref-3]. GamePro called the sounds "consistently strong" and Game Players cited the "hot music"[^ref-18][^ref-20]. Sega-16 was more reserved, describing it as rock and techno that was "worth the few plays" it got outside the game[^ref-3].

### Technical Specifications

**Sega CD Version:**
- **Media:** CD-ROM[^ref-2]
- **Audio:** CD Audio tracks[^ref-2]
- **Input:** Control pad; buttons A, B and C fire cannon, laser and Fat Boy[^ref-13]
- **Players:** Single-player only[^ref-2][^ref-18]
- **Launch price:** $49.95[^ref-18]

### Technical Issues

The main complaints concern design, not bugs. The Raven cannot strafe or reverse, and fires only forward[^ref-8][^ref-18]. Sega-16 found that dying anywhere sends the player back to the start of the stage, and that lost power-ups make later stages and bosses even slower[^ref-3].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The manual encourages players to take "short cuts" by collecting Moon Crystals out of the order the compass suggests[^ref-13]. The Retro Game Wiki also describes a large power-up stash on Ta Eliab Vee, reached by turning around at the start of the stage[^ref-4]. Sega-16's reviewer compared the collectible crystals to "rupees from a Zelda game"[^ref-3].

Sega-16 considered the FMV introduction "one of the better FMV intros on the Sega CD," with "nice resolution on limited hardware"[^ref-3].

### Cheat Codes

The game includes a stage-skipping cheat entered on the difficulty selection screen[^ref-4].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures have been found. Sega-16 wrote in 2009 that "it's definitely been lost with the passing of time"[^ref-3].

### Collections

MobyGames lists only the original 1993 Sega CD release[^ref-2].

### Related Publications

- **Stellar-Fire Manual:** Included with the game; covers the story, controls, difficulty levels, cockpit indicators and power-ups[^ref-13]
- **InterAction Holiday 1993:** Sierra's house magazine ran a feature on the game, with a guide to Arctura's moons[^ref-16]

### Critical Perspective

Reviewers of the day agreed on what Stellar-Fire did well, praising its sound and its opening cinematic, and on what it lacked: GamePro found "not enough action," and EGM said the cinemas "make the game a real letdown"[^ref-18][^ref-19]. Game Players was the most positive of the three, at 82%[^ref-20]. Later reviews have been harsher, from Sega-16's 4/10 to The Video Game Critic's F, although some players still defend its polygon graphics and soundtrack[^ref-3][^ref-10][^ref-5].
