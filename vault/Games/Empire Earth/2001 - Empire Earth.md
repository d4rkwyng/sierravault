# Empire Earth

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

Empire Earth is an ambitious real-time strategy game that spans 500,000 years of human history, from the prehistoric age through a speculative Nano Age in the 22nd century[^ref-3]. Released on November 13, 2001 in the United States[^ref-8], the game was developed by Stainless Steel Studios under the direction of Rick Goodman, who had previously served as lead designer on Age of Empires[^ref-2]. The game features fourteen historical epochs, more than 200 different kinds of units, and 21 predefined civilizations[^ref-3]; GameSpot judged it to have "by far the broadest scope and the greatest breadth of content of any real-time strategy game to date"[^ref-4].

The game represented Stainless Steel Studios' debut title and their ambitious goal to "become the industry's premier developer in real-time strategy games"[^ref-35]. Building upon the foundation laid by Age of Empires, Empire Earth expanded the scope dramatically while retaining the accessible interface that made its predecessor successful[^ref-5]. IGN noted that anyone familiar with Age of Empires would "hit the ground running in Empire Earth" due to the similar economic model and interface design[^ref-6].

Empire Earth achieved significant commercial success, selling over one million copies worldwide by May 2002[^ref-7]. The game won GameSpy's 2001 PC Game of the Year award[^ref-7][^ref-47] and spawned a franchise that would continue with an expansion pack and sequels[^ref-1].

## Story Summary

Empire Earth presents its narrative through four single-player campaigns, the Greek, English, German, and Russian, whose scenarios must be played in order[^ref-3].

The **Greek Campaign** opens with two scenarios about the founding of Greek cities such as Argos, moves on to the Trojan War and the rise of Athens, and ends with the conquests of Alexander the Great[^ref-11].

The **English Campaign** "runs from William to Wellington"[^ref-6].

The **German Campaign** "picks up in the First World War and concludes with the realization of Hitler's grand empire"[^ref-6], while the **Russian Campaign** presents a unique fictional future scenario where Soviet Russia reunites under a single banner to conquer the world using advanced robotic technology[^ref-12]. This final campaign showcases the speculative future epochs of the game, featuring a tyrant named Grigor who attempts world domination[^ref-13].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Empire Earth utilizes a familiar RTS interface that builds upon the Age of Empires template while introducing several innovations[^ref-6]. It borrows most of Age of Empires II's controls and interface features, and even some of its keyboard shortcuts[^ref-4]. The interface supports group formations, though critics noted these controls were sometimes poorly implemented[^ref-6].

The game features a fixed isometric camera with zoom functionality, though camera rotation is not available outside of cutscenes[^ref-9]. The 3D engine renders through Direct3D 7[^ref-51]; how it drew hundreds of 3D models without access to shaders was later the subject of a GameDev.net forum thread[^ref-14].

### Structure and Progression

The core gameplay loop requires players to collect resources to construct buildings, produce citizens and conquer opposing civilizations[^ref-2], while advancing through the game's epochs[^ref-3]. Players manage five resources: Food, Wood, Stone, Gold, and Iron[^ref-3].

**The Fourteen Epochs:**
- **Prehistoric Age (500,000–50,000 BC):** The discovery of fire and primitive tools[^ref-3]
- **Stone Age:** Early human settlements and hunting
- **Copper Age:** First metalworking and organized warfare
- **Bronze Age:** Advanced civilizations and chariots
- **Dark Age:** Fall of empires and feudal beginnings
- **Middle Ages:** Castles, knights, and siege warfare
- **Renaissance:** Gunpowder revolution and exploration
- **Imperial Age:** Colonial expansion and naval dominance
- **Industrial Age:** Mechanized warfare and railroads
- **Atomic Age - World War I:** Trench warfare and early aircraft
- **Atomic Age - World War II:** Tanks, bombers, and naval carriers
- **Atomic Age - Modern:** Cold War technology and jet aircraft
- **Digital Age:** Computer-guided weapons and stealth technology
- **Nano Age (2100–2200 AD):** Futuristic robots called "cybers" and advanced weaponry[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-13]

### Game Mechanics

The game employs a rock-paper-scissors combat system where each unit type has specific strengths and weaknesses[^ref-17]. Sword units defeat archers, spearmen defeat swordsmen, and archers defeat spearmen, requiring players to maintain balanced armies.

The **Hero System** offers two kinds of heroes: Strategist heroes heal nearby units and can demoralize enemies, while Warrior heroes give morale to the troops they lead and have greater attack power[^ref-10][^ref-3].

The **Morale System** makes units tougher and harder to kill. Town Centres and Capitals give morale to nearby units, each House built within their area of effect raises it up to a maximum, and Warrior Heroes add morale to the troops they lead[^ref-3].

**Prophets** represent a unique unit type capable of casting devastating "Calamities" including Malaria, Fire Storm, Earthquake, Volcano, Plague, and Hurricane[^ref-3][^ref-19]. These supernatural disasters can devastate enemy forces and structures, though areas protected by Temples are immune to such attacks.

Each of the 21 predefined civilizations has its own bonuses and a specific set of epochs in which it is more powerful[^ref-15]. The **Civilization Builder** lets players create a civilization from 100 different attributes[^ref-3]. In custom-civ games players spend 100 points on these bonuses, and each further purchase in the same area costs more[^ref-50].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Empire Earth received generally positive reviews upon release, with critics praising its ambitious scope while noting some technical shortcomings. **GameSpy** awarded the game a score of **94/100**[^ref-20] and later named it its 2001 PC Game of the Year[^ref-47]. Its review said "What impresses us the most is the sheer size of it -- each epoch feels fleshed out and playable, and every era has its own nuances, so it's almost like getting 14 games in one"[^ref-20].

**IGN** gave Empire Earth an **8.5/10** with an Editor's Choice award, stating it was "ambitious, addictive and more than just a 3D Age of Empires II clone"[^ref-6]. Reviewer Steve Butts observed that "the game lives up to its own ambitions" while acknowledging it "sometimes paints with a very broad brush."

**GameSpot** provided a more measured assessment with a **7.9/10**, with Greg Kasavin noting that "Empire Earth is massive—more than most real-time strategy games would dare to be" but warning that "you can actually have too much of a good thing"[^ref-4]. The review emphasized that "Empire Earth has by far the broadest scope and the greatest breadth of content of any real-time strategy game to date."

**Game Informer** offered a lower score of **6.25/10**, with reviewer Kristian criticizing that "the game's performance couldn't equal its scope"[^ref-22]. A second opinion from Kato in the same publication gave the game a **7/10**[^ref-22].

**G4 TV** scored the game **60/100**, describing it as "the most ambitious and complicated real-time strategy game ever made," noting this would "make it a joy to devout fans of the genre, but an overwhelming mess to everyone else"[^ref-20].

### Modern Assessment

The game holds a **Metacritic score of 81/100** based on 23 critic reviews, with a user score of **8.3/10** from 215 ratings[^ref-20].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **Metacritic:** 81/100 (23 reviews)[^ref-20]
- **GameRankings:** 82%[^ref-2]
- **MobyGames Critics:** 83%[^ref-1]
- **IMDb:** 7.9/10 (389 ratings)[^ref-23]
- **GOG.com Users:** 4.4/5 (1,438 reviews)[^ref-24]

Hardcore Gaming 101's retrospective, drawing on IGN's December 2000 interview with Goodman and Stefan Arnold[^ref-53] (which it misdates to 2010), says "the goal from the start was to combine the best of RTS gameplay (...) with that of empire-building", though it finds the visuals "have not aged well at all"[^ref-9]. One GOG.com user review states "I swear this thing has me more addicted than WoW"[^ref-24].

## Development

### Origins

Empire Earth's development began in 1998 when Rick Goodman founded Stainless Steel Studios, after co-founding Ensemble Studios with his brother and creating Age of Empires[^ref-5]. The studio was founded in January 1998 with the explicit goal of creating "the next generation of real-time strategy games"[^ref-25].

The concept for Empire Earth emerged from Goodman's desire to expand upon the historical RTS formula he had helped pioneer. The May 1998 announcement called it "a real-time strategy game for the next millennium" that "will set new standards in the genre, and offer gamers an experience that goes far beyond anything that strategy games released, thus far, have offered"[^ref-5].

The game was formally announced via press release on May 28, 1998, revealing plans for a title covering 500,000 years of history from 500,000 BC to 2500 AD with 12 unique epochs (later expanded to 14)[^ref-5].

### Production

Development of Empire Earth required building an entirely new game engine, which Stainless Steel Studios named "Titan"[^ref-9]. Rick Goodman emphasized that "without compromising the easy-to-understand player interface that Age of Empires offered, or the mass market appeal of the game, Empire Earth will be a much bigger game in every aspect of the word. We're designing an entirely new engine for this title, one that will offer features and design elements never seen in a real-time strategy game"[^ref-5].

Goodman hand-picked talented designers, artists, and programmers for the development team[^ref-5]. A notable addition to the team was Damon "Stratus" Gauthier, a StarCraft tournament veteran who was brought in on January 18, 2001 specifically for multiplayer balancing[^ref-2]. Goodman also described "a strike team of 20 of the top Age of Empires players" to help develop and fine-tune the game and its AI[^ref-25].

The game appeared at E3 2000 and E3 2001, and a beta test was released in early August 2001[^ref-2]. Later that month IGN was told the game was "pretty much in final QA"[^ref-26].

One of Goodman's most ambitious features was "interactive unpredictability," which he described as including "earthquakes, plagues, tax revolts, religious persecution, famine, pollution, civil war, global warming and nuclear disasters, among other natural and manmade events" that would "impact a player's empire both directly and indirectly"[^ref-5].

### Technical Achievements

The Titan engine renders fully 3D environments through Direct3D 7[^ref-51]. Designer Jon Alenson told IGN, "Our gigantic map takes a standard unit twenty minutes or half an hour to walk across"[^ref-26].

The game utilized the Miles Sound System by RAD Game Tools for audio and Bink Video for cutscenes[^ref-27][^ref-51]. The engine was designed with photo-realistic graphics rendered from an isometric perspective[^ref-5], though structures were not fully rendered except for walls and docks[^ref-9].

### Cut Content

A **Global Ranking System** was originally planned and even described on the box of the European release, but was never implemented[^ref-29]. IGN's reviewer regretted that the campaigns represent nothing of "the 1300 years between the death of Alexander the Great and the Battle of Hastings. Where's Rome? Where's Charlemagne?"[^ref-6] Julius Caesar and Charlemagne do appear in the game as Dark Age heroes[^ref-3].

### Technical Issues

The game has experienced various compatibility issues with modern operating systems. GameSpot noted that pathfinding "isn't quite perfect", with units sometimes "taking detours from point A to point B"[^ref-4]. A single MobyGames user review further complains of an AI that cheats with free resources, screen jumping when alt-selecting teams, aircraft flying in circles, and AA guns that are ineffective against atomic bombers[^ref-1].

The GOG.com release has addressed many compatibility issues through DirectX wrapper updates and stability validation for Windows 10 and 11[^ref-31]. Users may experience black blocks in The Art of Conquest main menu, which can be resolved by switching to Direct3D Hardware TnL renderer in video options[^ref-31].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Empire Earth includes numerous cheat codes accessible through the in-game chat window:[^ref-32]
- **"my name is methos"** – All Resources & Reveal Map
- **"all your base are belong to us"** – +100,000 of all resources
- **"boston food sucks"** – +1000 Food
- **"asus drivers"** – Reveal Map
- **"somebody set up us the bomb"** – Win Game
- **"brainstorm"** – Fast Build
- **"coffee train"** – Restores Health of Player Units

Prophet units yell different Latin phrases depending on the epoch: "Deus vult!" (God wills it) from Copper Age to Dark Age and Industrial Age onwards, while Dark Age to Industrial Age Prophets yell "Deus absconditus!" (the hidden God)[^ref-19].

The Cutting Room Floor notes the game "is full of references to unused items"[^ref-33], and voice work includes David J. Fielding, known for his work on Power Rangers[^ref-9].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Empire Earth was a commercial success, selling over one million units worldwide by 2002[^ref-7]. In the United States alone, the game sold 390,000 copies generating $16.7 million in revenue by August 2006, ranking as the 41st best-selling computer game in the US from January 2000 to August 2006[^ref-2].

The game earned a Silver award from ELSPA for sales exceeding 100,000 copies in the United Kingdom, a Gold prize in Spain for 40,000 sales in its first year, and sold 110,000 units in France by March 2005[^ref-2].

### Collections

Empire Earth was released in several compilation editions:
- **Gold Edition** – 2003 (GameFAQs gives March 6; Steam and Wikipedia give May 6) – Original game plus The Art of Conquest expansion[^ref-29][^ref-48][^ref-54]
- **Platinum Edition** (March 2003) – European equivalent of Gold Edition[^ref-29]
- **Ultimate Edition** (December 2009) – Complete collection[^ref-34]

### Expansion and Sequels

**Empire Earth: The Art of Conquest** (September 17, 2002)[^ref-29] was developed by Mad Doc Software and added a 15th epoch (Space Age)[^ref-13], the Japanese and Korean civilizations[^ref-2], and three new campaigns set in ancient Rome, the Pacific Theater of World War II, and a futuristic Asia[^ref-7]. The expansion received mixed reviews with a Metacritic score of 63 from 13 critics[^ref-49], with GameSpot's Tom Chick giving it just 5.2/10 and concluding that it "doesn't add enough to make much of a difference"[^ref-36].

**Empire Earth II** (April 26, 2005) was developed by Mad Doc Software, the studio that had also made The Art of Conquest[^ref-52][^ref-37][^ref-7]. It received a 79% Metacritic score and introduced weather effects, seasonal cycles, and a Picture-in-Picture feature[^ref-38].

**Empire Earth III** (November 6, 2007) received a Metacritic score of 50 ("Mixed or Average") from 40 critic reviews, none of them positive[^ref-39]. Lead designer Matthew Nordhaus admitted in a 2007 GameSpot feature that the game had "very little, if anything, from the original" Empire Earth[^ref-40]. German publication GameStar included it in their "10 Most Terrible Sequels" list[^ref-40].

### Fan Projects

Following the shutdown of official multiplayer servers on November 3, 2008[^ref-2], the fan community established **NeoEE** (neoee.net), which provides replacement multiplayer servers allowing players to continue enjoying online matches[^ref-41]. The NeoEE project supports Windows 7 through Windows 11 and offers multilingual patches.

Community modders have created the "Refinement Patch" which combines multiple popular mods into a single package[^ref-42].

For the sequel, an **Unofficial Patch** for Empire Earth II (version 1.6) adds support for all screen resolutions including 4K, compatibility fixes, and even Tobii Eye Tracker device support[^ref-43]. Its changelog warns that "the first changes were written by a 14-year-old non-native English speaker"[^ref-43].

### Related Publications

- **Empire Earth User's Manual** – included with the game; the Internet Archive scan runs to 239 pages[^ref-44]
- **Empire Earth: Prima's Official Strategy Guide** – Included with Gold Edition[^ref-45]
- **Empire Earth Technology Tree** – Poster-size reference included with Gold Edition[^ref-45]

### Critical Perspective

Critics split on whether the game's scale was a strength. GameSpot warned that "Empire Earth will overwhelm casual players" and that "you can actually have too much of a good thing"[^ref-4], while IGN called it "ambitious, addictive and more than just a 3D Age of Empires II clone"[^ref-6].
