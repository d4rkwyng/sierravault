# Merchant Colony

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Merchant Colony is a strategy and business simulation game released in 1991. Developed by [[Impressions Games]] and published by Merit Studios in North America, the game challenges players to build a successful trading empire during the Age of Exploration[^ref-1]. The game's scope is notably ambitious—rather than managing a single colony as the title might suggest, players are tasked with developing an entire colonial empire[^ref-2].

Set in the 18th century, Merchant Colony simulates the naval trading and colonial expansion that characterized the era of great merchant companies like the East India Trading Company[^ref-3]. Players take on the role of a beginner shipowner with limited capital who must develop their business through shrewd trading, colony establishment, and strategic resource management[^ref-4]. The game encompasses colonization, industrial development, trading, and warfare[^ref-18], and was marketed as a "simulation of geo-politics"[^ref-5].

Merchant Colony received mixed reviews from contemporary gaming magazines[^ref-13]. A HOTUD review called it a "boring precursor to Impressions' vastly improved High Seas Trader"[^ref-9], and it was released three years before Sid Meier's Colonization[^ref-6].

## Story Summary

Merchant Colony does not feature a traditional narrative structure but instead places players within a historical simulation set during the 18th century colonial period. The player assumes the role of a merchant prince whose ambition is to build a dominant trading company capable of rivaling the great mercantile organizations of the era[^ref-3]. Beginning with modest resources, the player must navigate the complex world of international trade and colonial expansion.

The game's setting encompasses the entire globe, with trading cities spread from North America through Europe and Asia to Australia, and long sea voyages exposed to pirate attacks[^ref-4]. Computer-controlled nations compete with the player through trading, diplomacy or war[^ref-10]. The ultimate objective, as described by players, is essentially world domination through economic and colonial supremacy[^ref-9].

The player begins as a novice shipowner with little cash[^ref-4]. As the empire grows, the merchant prince must balance exploration of new territories, establishment of profitable colonies, management of trade routes, and military defense against both pirates and rival nations[^ref-10]. Success or failure depends entirely on the player's ability to manage these interconnected systems effectively.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Merchant Colony is presented from a top-down perspective and supports keyboard and mouse[^ref-1][^ref-11]. The game presents information through a series of screens showing statistics, maps, and management options. CU Amiga's May 1991 review criticized "a cumbersome control system that involves accessing numerous subscreens"[^ref-29].

The game operates in two primary phases: a management phase where players make strategic decisions about their empire, and a sailing phase where ships traverse the global map[^ref-4]. Players use the keyboard and mouse to navigate between different screens and issue commands to their ships, colonies, and personnel[^ref-1]. Speed controls are available through keyboard shortcuts, with ALT+S activating slow speed and ALT+R returning to normal speed[^ref-6].

### Structure and Progression

The game unfolds as an open-ended simulation without predetermined chapter breaks or linear progression. Players must organically build their empire by:

- **Ship Acquisition:** Purchasing and maintaining a fleet capable of global trade and exploration[^ref-4]
- **Colony Establishment:** Founding settlements in strategic locations to produce valuable goods[^ref-2]
- **Trade Development:** Creating profitable trade routes connecting colonies with markets[^ref-10]
- **Industrial Expansion:** Developing production of goods like coffee, tea, sugar, diamonds, and gold[^ref-12]
- **Military Operations:** Defending trade routes and potentially conquering rival trading posts[^ref-12]

Progression is measured by the growth of the player's trading company, the extent of colonial holdings, and overall wealth accumulated. Players can hire various specialists including teachers, engineers, and settlers to expand their operations[^ref-4].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Rather than traditional puzzles, Merchant Colony presents players with complex economic and strategic challenges. The core mechanics involve:

**Trade Management:** Players must understand supply and demand across different regions, buying goods cheaply in colonies and selling them at profit in distant markets. Commodities include natural resources and manufactured products, with different colonies specializing in different goods[^ref-6].

**Naval Operations:** Ships must be purchased, crewed, and sent on voyages around the world. Long sea expeditions carry significant risk, as they are vulnerable to pirate attacks[^ref-4]. Players must balance the potential profits of distant trade routes against the dangers and time required.

**Colonial Administration:** Each colony requires management attention, with players deciding what goods to produce and how to develop local industry[^ref-10]. Sending soldiers can allow players to conquer trading posts and dominate specific commodities[^ref-12].

**Diplomacy and Warfare:** Computer-controlled nations compete with the player through trading, diplomacy, or outright warfare[^ref-10]. Managing these relationships adds another layer of strategic complexity.

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Merchant Colony received mixed reviews from gaming publications, with scores ranging from 23% to 89%[^ref-13].

**Magazine Reviews:**
- **Joystick** gave the game **89%** in issue 15 (April 1991), reviewed by Moulinex[^ref-13]
- **Amiga Format** awarded **67%**, reviewed by Trenton Webb in the June 1991 issue[^ref-13]
- **Amiga Action** scored the game **60%** in their May 1991 issue[^ref-13]
- **CU Amiga** gave **59%** (Graphics 52%, Sound 10%), with Dan Slingsby reviewing for the May 1991 edition[^ref-13][^ref-29]
- **Amiga Joker** was notably harsh, awarding only **23%** in a review by Joachim Nettelbeck in May 1991[^ref-13]

CU Amiga's Dan Slingsby wrote that "the game suffers from poor quality graphics, virtually no sound, and a cumbersome control system that involves accessing numerous subscreens," while adding that as an educational tool "its value should not be underestimated"[^ref-29].

### Modern Assessment

Some modern write-ups regard Merchant Colony as underrated. Games Nostalgia noted that "the game is not easy, and this is probably the reason why it didn't have a huge success, but it's a game that has definitely been underestimated"[^ref-7]. User comments on preservation sites reflect fond memories, with one commenter on Lemon Amiga stating: "Merchant Colony got a bad review and score from game mags, but I thought this was one of the best games on the Amiga"[^ref-12].

However, HOTUD's review called it a "boring precursor to Impressions' vastly improved High Seas Trader" and concluded that "very limited options and bland graphics seriously erode long-term play value"[^ref-9].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames Critic Average:** 61% (7 ratings)[^ref-15]
- **MobyGames Player Average:** 3.4 out of 5 (2 ratings)[^ref-15]
- **Atarimania:** 7.7/10[^ref-16]
- **Abandonware DOS:** 4.00/5.00[^ref-11]
- **OldGames:** 60%[^ref-10]

## Development

### Origins

Merchant Colony was developed by Impressions, a company founded in 1989 that focused on strategy and business games[^ref-18] and based in the United Kingdom[^ref-22]. The company was acquired by Sierra On-Line in 1995[^ref-18]. The game represented an early effort by the studio to create a comprehensive historical trading simulation.

The design drew inspiration from the historical reality of 18th-century colonial trade, aiming to simulate the complex economic and political dynamics of the era. According to the game manual, the goal was to recreate "a complex, sophisticated trading game set in the age of expansionism" comparable to running organizations like the East India Trading Company[^ref-3].

### Production

Lemon Amiga, Atarimania and Atari Legend credit the Amiga and Atari ST versions' development to Video Vulture[^ref-13][^ref-16][^ref-27], while MobyGames lists Impressions Games as developer[^ref-1]. MobyGames credits David Lester with the game concept, with Edward Grabowski as project director and programming by Simon Bradbury[^ref-28]. The game was released for three contemporary platforms: DOS, Amiga, and Atari ST[^ref-1].

**Development Credits:**[^ref-28]
- **Game Concept:** [[David Lester]]
- **Project Director:** Edward Grabowski
- **Programming:** [[Simon Bradbury]]
- **Graphics:** Jill Lawson, Jon Baker
- **Sound & Music:** Christopher J. Denman

The game was localized for multiple European markets, with versions available in English, French, German, Italian, and Spanish[^ref-13][^ref-16].

### Technical Achievements

While Merchant Colony was not known for technical innovation, it did achieve a comprehensive global scope. The game allowed players to sail across the entire world map, establishing colonies and trade routes spanning multiple continents[^ref-4][^ref-12].

The game's information screens were criticized by HOTUD for "long, tedious lists of statistics presented in static screens"[^ref-9].

### Technical Issues

CU Amiga found the control system cumbersome[^ref-29], and one Lemon Amiga user described the game as "slow paced" and requiring "a lot of patience and effort"[^ref-12]. Players running the DOS version in DOSBox have reported it running far slower than remembered; a VOGONS forum reply pointed to the game's own speed settings (ALT+R for normal speed), which resolved the issue[^ref-6]. Modern players require emulation software such as DOSBox to run the game on contemporary systems[^ref-11].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game was released three years before Sid Meier's Colonization, and unlike it was real-time based[^ref-6]
- Players could dominate specific commodities by conquering trading posts, allowing monopolistic control over goods like coffee, tea, sugar, diamonds, and gold[^ref-12]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Merchant Colony did not achieve major commercial success; Games Nostalgia suggests its difficulty was "probably the reason why it didn't have a huge success"[^ref-7]. No specific sales figures have been documented in available sources.

Impressions went on to make High Seas Trader, which HOTUD described as "vastly improved" over Merchant Colony[^ref-9].

### Collections

Merchant Colony was included in the 1992 compilation Strategy Masters[^ref-1]. The game is also preserved through abandonware sites and digital archives, including the Internet Archive, which hosts a browser-streamable version[^ref-2].

### Fan Projects

No significant fan remakes or modifications have been documented for Merchant Colony.

### Related Publications

- **Game Manual:** The original manual described the game as "a complex, sophisticated trading game set in the age of expansionism" and compared the player's objective to building organizations like the East India Trading Company[^ref-3]
- **Historical booklet:** CU Amiga noted the game "comes complete with an authoritative booklet which helps put the game into some sort of historical context"[^ref-29]

### Critical Perspective

Merchant Colony occupies an interesting position in gaming history as an ambitious but flawed early attempt at the colonial trading simulation genre, preceding Colonization by three years[^ref-6]. Period reviews faulted its graphics, sound and cumbersome subscreen-driven controls[^ref-29], while some later players remember it fondly as an underdog[^ref-12].

The wide spread of contemporary review scores—from 23% to 89%[^ref-13]—shows how differently critics responded to it. Impressions later revisited the seafaring trade theme with High Seas Trader[^ref-9].
