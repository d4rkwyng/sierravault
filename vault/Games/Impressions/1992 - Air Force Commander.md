# Air Force Commander

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Air Force Commander is a real-time air-war strategy game from [[Impressions Games]], released in 1992 for DOS, with an Amiga version developed by Golden Sector and published by Impressions[^ref-1][^ref-2]. The game places players in command of a Middle Eastern country's air force, tasking them with gaining air superiority over the enemy[^ref-1]. Computer Gaming World's Michael Peck called it "a simple yet relatively thoughtful treatment of a vital but overlooked topic"[^ref-16]. A 2009 retrospective by Let's Play Oldies noted that it was made in the same year as Dune 2 and three years before the first Command & Conquer[^ref-5].

Computer Gaming World counted 28 scenarios in the DOS release[^ref-16], while the Amiga packaging blurb advertised "26 modern Middle East scenarios"[^ref-6]. Aircraft range from older types such as the SU-17 to the F-117A Stealth Fighter[^ref-8]. Players must manage resources, bases, pilots and aircraft: every base and unit consumes resources, and the player loses if public morale collapses[^ref-1][^ref-16]. Scenarios include esoteric match-ups such as Bahrain vs. Qatar[^ref-17][^ref-16].

Impressions Software was founded in 1989 and focused on strategy and business games; it was acquired by Sierra On-Line in 1995[^ref-8]. According to My Abandonware's description, Impressions later improved the game's engine and used it for Global Domination and When Two Worlds War[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

Air Force Commander is set against the backdrop of contemporary Middle Eastern conflicts. According to MobyGames, about half of the 43-page manual consists of an outlined history of the Middle East, with sections on the roots of Islam, colonial influences in the region, the history of Israel, regional wars and Operation Desert Storm[^ref-1].

Computer Gaming World described scenarios ranging from "a mini-war between the pygmy states of Bahrain, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, to a full-scale conflict between a U.S.-Israeli alliance and a strong Arab coalition"[^ref-16]. A state concedes defeat when its public opinion crumbles, from strikes on food and power sources, or when its air force runs out of supplies[^ref-16].

MobyGames' trivia singles out defending Lebanon against Syria, with only 4 SAM units, 1 helicopter and an F-4 unit, as probably one of the hardest scenarios[^ref-1].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Players launch aircraft by clicking on an aircraft icon or an airfield and cycling through a list of squadrons and support units[^ref-16]. The map can be viewed in three modes, from a high-altitude strategic view to a close-up satellite view, but most play takes place on the radar map[^ref-16]. Computer Gaming World found the interface "generally adequate" but wanted a single display listing all aircraft, and judged the real-time workload "the most frustrating aspect of the game"[^ref-16]. Let's Play Oldies wrote that "giving orders to a unit is fiendishly difficult"[^ref-5], and My Abandonware's description cites a "cumbersome user interface"[^ref-7].

All control is by mouse[^ref-1]. Players cannot queue orders to aircraft still in the air[^ref-11]. Certain aircraft such as the Mirage F-1 cannot patrol, so they must be assigned manually to intercept each incoming aircraft[^ref-11].

### Structure and Progression

Each scenario pits the player's air force against a neighbour's. The DOS version is exclusively about Middle East conflicts, and a VOGONS player found that eliminating the enemy's major airbase was enough to win[^ref-11].

- **Scenario Selection:** 28 scenarios per Computer Gaming World[^ref-16]
- **Force Management:** Squadrons, fuel and munitions stockpiles, and installations that can be shut down to conserve supplies[^ref-16]
- **Combat Operations:** Ground strikes, defensive patrols and strike escort, plus cruise missiles, SAM batteries and mobile radar[^ref-16]
- **Victory Conditions:** Collapse of enemy public opinion or of its air force's supplies[^ref-16]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

A VOGONS reviewer described it as a real-time wargame in which the resource management aspect is limited[^ref-11]. The game focuses solely on aircraft and rocket bases[^ref-1][^ref-12]. When a squadron is given an order it launches one plane at a time and continues the mission until told to stop, wearing down its efficiency rating and its fuel and munitions; as Computer Gaming World put it, "a player who flies every aircraft around the clock will lose the game"[^ref-16].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

**Computer Gaming World #101 (December 1992), Michael Peck:** reviewed on IBM PC at $59.95. Peck praised the strategic viewpoint as "so refreshing" but warned arcade and flight-sim fans that "there are virtually no combat graphics in this game." He criticised the workload and the documentation, and concluded it was "a simple yet relatively thoughtful treatment of a vital but overlooked topic"[^ref-16].

**Amiga User International Vol 7 No 9 (October 1993):** 90%[^ref-22].

**Amiga Format 53 (December 1993), Stephen Bradley:** 69%, at £25.99. Bradley found it "almost too close to real life," noted the two disks, one of Middle East scenarios and one of WWII, and concluded it was "a complex and intriguing simulation which, despite some gripes, is worth the considerable effort required to get the most of it"[^ref-19][^ref-3].

**CU Amiga (December 1993), Jonathan Sloan:** 48%, at £29.99. Sloan found the gameplay simple and the control system intuitive, but opened: "I am getting pretty sick of reviewing strategy games that have all the graphic sophistication of a three-year-old's first attempt to paint." He closed with "It is really sad to think that so much imagination has gone into the overall design and so little in its execution"[^ref-20][^ref-3].

**Amiga Joker (November 1993), Michael Schnelle:** 37%, at DM 79. The German magazine treated the Amiga release as a renamed re-release of an earlier Impressions air-strategy title that it said it had reviewed at 61% ("Fighter Command" in the review), now with new packaging, some new menu graphics and 28 additional WWII missions. Schnelle complained that Impressions had carried over the "unausgegorenen Steuerung" and "unübersichtlichen Karte" (translation: "half-baked controls" and "confusing map"), plus the lack of a two-player mode and minimal sound effects. Sub-scores: graphics 39%, sound effects 3%, handling 41%, long-term fun 44%[^ref-21][^ref-3].

**Other scores:** Joystick (France, November 1993) 70/100, and Amiga Action 54 (February 1994) 59/100[^ref-6]. Amazing Computing also reviewed the Amiga version (Vol 8 No 11, November 1993, Jeff James)[^ref-22].

### Modern Assessment

In his survey of wargames in Computer Gaming World #114, M. Evan Brooks gave the game two stars: "Offering multi-scenario options for strategic air supremacy in the Middle East, the scenarios themselves are detailed and somewhat esoteric, e.g. Bahrain vs. Qatar (actually not that unrealistic given the political realities)... Overall, an interesting effort with little potential for enduring play value"[^ref-17].

A 2001 MobyGames user review by Shadowcaster stated: "If you enjoy real time strategy, and you want to play something that was years ahead of C & C, try this on for size"[^ref-9]. A Let's Play Oldies review from 2009 awarded 5/10, concluding "Realism is boring"[^ref-5].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 6.2 Moby Score, 58% critics (7 ratings), 2.7/5 players (3 ratings)[^ref-1]
- **My Abandonware:** 4.5/5 (20 votes)[^ref-7]

## Development

### Origins

The DOS version was developed and published by Impressions; MobyGames credits Golden Sector as developer of the Amiga version, with additional programming by Novotrade Software Ltd.[^ref-1] Computer Gaming World lists the designers as Chris Bamford, Simon Bradbury and David Lester[^ref-16]. MobyGames' DOS credits list Simon Bradbury as programmer, Erik Casey and Stephen Redpath on graphics, Christopher J. Denman on music and sound, and Mark Saunders on research[^ref-1]. Amiga Format's credits box listed the programmers as "Simon Bradbury, Novocade Software Ltd"[^ref-3].

### Technical Issues

According to Brooks, the speed of the game could not be slowed down; eventually a patch was distributed over electronic bulletin boards[^ref-17]. Computer Gaming World's patch listing includes an "Air Force Commander Slowdown Patch" dated 11/24/92 that "makes the game playable on high-powered machines"[^ref-18].

Additional issues included:
- Cannot queue orders to aircraft still in the air[^ref-11]
- Certain fighters lack patrolling capability, requiring manual intercept assignment[^ref-11]
- Overwhelming amount of clicking required during early phases[^ref-11]
- Weak AI opponents[^ref-7]
- No combat graphics to speak of[^ref-16]
- Minimal sound effects and no two-player mode (Amiga)[^ref-3]

- MobyGames' trivia calls defending Lebanon against Syria with only 4 SAM units, 1 chopper and an F-4 unit probably one of the hardest scenarios[^ref-1]
- The manual mentions a planned next installment focused on WW2-era air warfare[^ref-11]; WW2 Air Force Commander followed in 1993[^ref-1][^ref-10]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures are available. MobyGames lists WW2 Air Force Commander (1993) as the next game in the series[^ref-1].

### Collections

The game has been preserved by various abandonware and retro gaming archives[^ref-4][^ref-7][^ref-13][^ref-14].

### Sequel: WW2 Air Force Commander

WW2 Air Force Commander was released in 1993 for DOS and Amiga as a follow-up to the original[^ref-10][^ref-15]. The sequel moves the setting to World War II[^ref-10].

On the Amiga, the two-disk Air Force Commander release already included WWII scenarios: Amiga Format described one disk of Middle East scenarios and another of WWII scenarios, 56 in total, ranging from the United Arab Emirates vs. Kuwait to the Battle of Britain[^ref-3]. Amiga Joker described the Amiga version as a re-release with new name, packaging and menu graphics, plus 28 WWII missions[^ref-21][^ref-3].

### Related Publications

- **Air Force Commander Manual:** 43-page manual, about half of it an outlined history of the Middle East, including sections on the roots of Islam, colonial influences, the history of Israel, and technical comments on various aircraft[^ref-1]

### Critical Perspective

Air Force Commander was an early real-time strategy title: Let's Play Oldies placed it in the same year as Dune 2 and three years before the first Command & Conquer[^ref-5], and Shadowcaster called it "years ahead of C & C"[^ref-9]. Contemporary critics liked its design more than its execution. Computer Gaming World credited it with a rare theatre-level perspective[^ref-16], while CU Amiga lamented "so much imagination" in the design and "so little in its execution"[^ref-3]. A VOGONS reviewer concluded, "Air Force Commander is the only game of its kind by far. And being the only one of its kind, I still feel that the game is quite entertaining"[^ref-11].
