# Front Lines

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Front Lines is a tactical wargame set in the "near" future, developed and published by [[Impressions Games]] in 1994.[^ref-1] It is a turn-based, hex-based game of futuristic ground combat; Wikipedia, drawing on a 1995 interview in *The One*, describes it as Impressions' first hex-map strategy game.[^ref-21] IMDb lists the United Kingdom as its country of origin,[^ref-3] and *Computer Gaming World* gave the publisher as Impressions Software of Cambridge, Massachusetts.[^ref-16] Sources place its setting in the 21st century but disagree on the exact date (see [Story Summary](#story-summary)).[^ref-1][^ref-17][^ref-18]

Reviewers of the day measured it against QQP's *The Perfect General*: *Computer Gaming World* called it "clearly an attempt to go beyond QQP's" game,[^ref-16] and *PC Games* (UK) found the feel "very similar to that fine old warhorse Perfect General's."[^ref-19] The game's European theater includes some real-world geography, such as the Fulda Gap.[^ref-1]

Period opinion was split. *Electronic Entertainment* called it "one of the best war games of the year,"[^ref-18] while *Computer Gaming World* criticised its "feeble, piecemeal" computer opponent and the absence of modem play at launch.[^ref-16] Later write-ups describe it as an accessible wargame for newcomers.[^ref-5][^ref-12]

## Story Summary

Front Lines presents a hypothetical future conflict in a European theater of war.[^ref-1] Sources differ on the date. MobyGames says the game is set "in 2020 or 2044, depending on whether you play the U. S. or German version."[^ref-1] *Computer Gaming World* (US) described its battlefield as roughly fifty years in the future,[^ref-16] *Electronic Entertainment* gave the time as "Mid-21st century,"[^ref-18] and the DOS Games Archive's description says "mid-2040s."[^ref-13] No primary source has been found that confirms a version-specific date.

*Electronic Entertainment* summarised the backstory: after a period of global unity, "renegade cartels—powerful multinational corporations equipped with their own mercenary armies" go to war with each other.[^ref-18] MobyGames likewise notes that some of the opposing forces "answer to a corporate conglomerate," and *Computer Game Review* described the player commanding either a futuristic United Nations army or its enemies.[^ref-1][^ref-20]

According to MobyGames, the setting includes powered armor, practical heavy laser weapons, unmanned ground strike aircraft, hovering tanks, and more powerful, accurate artillery.[^ref-1] The map has "some limited representation of Europe's real-world geographical features," including the Fulda Gap.[^ref-1]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Front Lines uses a top-down, hex-based tactical map.[^ref-1][^ref-18] *Computer Gaming World* found the mouse-driven interface generally straightforward and intuitive but said the three manuals left out some important details. One example is the undocumented click sequence needed for opportunity fire.[^ref-16] A *Computer Game Review* panelist wrote that "the interface is surprisingly convenient—most wargames have, at best, a rather unwieldy feel to them,"[^ref-20] and *PC Games* listed "The interface is a bit fiddly" among its negatives.[^ref-19]

### Structure and Progression

Each scenario opens with a briefing of objectives and suggested strategies. Players then either accept the default forces or buy their own units from a points pool.[^ref-16] According to MobyGames, each battle has objectives and a time limit, and the side with the most victory points wins.[^ref-1] The CD-ROM version shipped with 33 scenarios, 13 more than the disk version, ranked easy, medium and hard.[^ref-16]

- **Scenarios:** pre-built battles ranked by difficulty[^ref-16]
- **Scenario builder:** players can lay out maps, edit terrain and change unit values.[^ref-16] Maps can be up to 100 by 100 hexes.[^ref-20]
- **Two-player:** "hot seat" play on one computer at launch. Modem play was advertised but missing from the initial release (see [Production](#production)).[^ref-16]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

*Computer Gaming World* counted 14 combat units in four groups: tanks (including the Ferret and SCAB hover tanks and the Mega Tank), armored personnel carriers, infantry, and artillery. Infantry includes engineers, which can build bridges and lay mines.[^ref-16] Each turn represents one minute of real time. Movement alternates between sides, and fire can be resolved alternately or simultaneously, with optional opportunity fire.[^ref-16] A "one shot, one kill" option makes combat quicker and deadlier.[^ref-16]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

- ***Computer Gaming World*** (#129, April 1995; Patrick C. Miller): The reviewer praised the SVGA graphics as "outstanding" and the scenario builder as "an excellent addition." He faulted the combat model and the AI, whose "feeble, piecemeal attacks" could be crushed with massed firepower. Modem play "wasn't available in the initial release."[^ref-16]
- ***Electronic Entertainment*** (#18, June 1995; Barry Brenesal): "Within its genre, though, Front Lines is excellent." The review called it "one of the best war games of the year—for either grizzled veterans of countless campaigns or novices firing their first artillery round."[^ref-18]
- ***PC Games*** (UK, #11, June 1995): "a snappy, easy-to-play wargame with the emphasis strictly on fun and not at all on realism." The magazine's main complaint was that the computer AI "just isn't subtle enough."[^ref-19]
- ***Computer Game Review*** (#44, March 1995): A three-reviewer panel called it "an admirable attempt at making an entry-level wargame" and judged its AI "flawed as well, but not fatally." Another panelist concluded it is "a good wargame that let's you play it the way you want."[^ref-20]
- MobyGames lists a critics' average of 66% from 13 ratings.[^ref-1]

By January 1996, *Computer Gaming World*'s year-in-review of science-fiction wargames was harsher. It called Front Lines "perhaps a little too much like the Perfect General with a graphic overlay." It added that this, "coupled with a few bugs in the initial release meant Front Lines came and went off most hard drives at escape velocity."[^ref-17]

### Later Write-ups

Several later, undated site write-ups cover the game. The Home of the Underdogs write-up, which MyAbandonware also reproduces, calls it "a hypothetical wargame that strongly resembles QQP's venerable The Perfect General series" and "an excellent design that is ideal for novice and intermediate wargamers." It criticises "Flawed AI and some questionable realism" and concludes: "Not as good as The Perfect General, but a worthy attempt. Recommended!"[^ref-12][^ref-4]

A user-written review on Old-Games.com describes the game as "the offspring of a male Warlords and a female Command and Conquer." The writer complains of slow, jerky play, recalling "waiting in front of my computer for approximately seven minutes before the computer player decided it was done moving its tanks around."[^ref-6]

Squakenet calls it a "Polished and satisfying wargame." It says the game "finds a perfect balance between simplicity in execution and depth of the game sessions you can have" and "can be a good gateway game."[^ref-5]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 66% critics' average (13 ratings); Moby Score 6.6[^ref-1]
- **Home of the Underdogs:** 8.12 (41 votes)[^ref-12]
- **MyAbandonware:** 4.26/5 (19 votes)[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

Front Lines was developed by [[Impressions Games]], a studio founded by David Lester in 1989 and later known for the Caesar city-building series.[^ref-11] Christopher J. Foster is credited as designer.[^ref-1][^ref-16] Wikipedia, citing *The One*, reports that Impressions' James Hunter said the team wanted to improve on the simplistic graphics of other hex wargames. He explained the near-future setting as "far enough into the future to have some things a little bit different, but not so far that everything has become unrecognisable."[^ref-21]

### Production

*Computer Gaming World* reported that because of technical difficulties, modem play "wasn't available in the initial release" even though the box and manuals referred to it. Impressions said a patch would follow once the problems were resolved. Until then, two players could share one computer in "hot seat" mode.[^ref-16] Other reviews list modem play. *Computer Game Review* gave "1-2 Players (modem possible)," and *Electronic Entertainment*'s June 1995 review described modem and null-modem play with a chat mode.[^ref-20][^ref-18]

The CD-ROM version's scenario builder shipped with a bug that made it nearly useless. *Computer Gaming World* noted that Impressions released a patch "within days after the game's release."[^ref-16] Impressions also said Front Lines was "the first in a series of hypothetical and historical games" built on its engine.[^ref-16] According to Wikipedia, a planned American Civil War follow-up became *Robert E. Lee: Civil War General*. Impressions' Jeffrey Fiske later said Front Lines "if it had done a little more time in development, would have had much better gameplay."[^ref-21]

Impressions was acquired by Sierra On-Line in 1995.[^ref-11] An Amiga conversion, programmed by Andrew Prime, was announced around the PC release and previewed in magazines. It slipped into 1995 and, according to Wikipedia, was quietly cancelled after the Sierra acquisition.[^ref-1][^ref-21]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-1]
- **Designer:** Christopher J. Foster
- **Amiga Coder (unreleased):** Andrew Prime

### Technical Achievements

Front Lines was sold on 3.5" floppy disks and CD-ROM.[^ref-1] The CD-ROM version added digitized voice and longer animated sequences.[^ref-16] Reviewers singled out its SVGA graphics, which *Computer Gaming World* likened to finely sculpted miniatures.[^ref-16][^ref-19]

### Technical Specifications

**DOS Version:**[^ref-16]
- **Requirements:** 386 or better, 4 MB RAM, SVGA graphics, hard drive, mouse; CD-ROM drive for the CD version[^ref-16]
- **Sound:** AdLib, Gravis Ultrasound, Roland, Sound Blaster compatibles[^ref-16]
- **Media:** 3.5" Floppy Disk, CD-ROM[^ref-1]
- **Price:** $59.95 (CGW),[^ref-16] $69.95 list (*Electronic Entertainment*),[^ref-18] £39.99 (UK)[^ref-19]
- **File Size:** 5 MB (MyAbandonware)[^ref-4] / 4.84 MB easy setup, 25.5 MB full download (Old-Games.com)[^ref-6]
- **DOSBox Support:** Supported on version 0.61+[^ref-4]

### Cut Content

The planned Amiga conversion, coded by Andrew Prime, was previewed in several magazines but never released.[^ref-1] Wikipedia reports memory as the main obstacle, since the PC game used 4 MB of RAM. It says the port was quietly cancelled after Sierra acquired Impressions.[^ref-21]

### Technical Issues

- **Modem play:** advertised but missing from the initial release, with a patch promised.[^ref-16]
- **Scenario editor bug:** CD-ROM editor bug patched within days of release.[^ref-16]
- **AI:** criticised by *Computer Gaming World*, *PC Games* and *Computer Game Review*.[^ref-16][^ref-19][^ref-20]
- **Speed:** *PC Games* found it too slow even on a decent machine,[^ref-19] and an Old-Games.com user recalled a wait of about seven minutes for the computer's turn.[^ref-6]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- Sources disagree on the setting's date: MobyGames gives 2020 or 2044 depending on version, while period reviews and other descriptions place it in the mid-21st century.[^ref-1][^ref-16][^ref-18][^ref-13]
- The map includes the Fulda Gap.[^ref-1]
- *Computer Game Review* said the Mega Tank sports a gun "bigger than Meat Loaf's waistline."[^ref-20]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures have been found. *Computer Gaming World*'s January 1996 retrospective said the game "came and went off most hard drives at escape velocity."[^ref-17]

### Collections

No known compilation releases have been documented for Front Lines.

### Fan Projects

The included scenario builder supports user-made scenarios.[^ref-16] No major standalone fan projects or remakes have been documented.

### Related Publications

No hint books, strategy guides or novelizations have been documented. *Computer Gaming World* notes that the game came with three manuals.[^ref-16]

### Critical Perspective

Front Lines was received as a good-looking, accessible take on *The Perfect General* formula. It was let down by its AI and by launch problems. Some reviewers recommended it to novices,[^ref-18][^ref-20] while *Computer Gaming World* judged it "a game with much promise and potential."[^ref-16] Within Impressions' history, Wikipedia identifies it as the starting point for what became *Robert E. Lee: Civil War General*.[^ref-21]
