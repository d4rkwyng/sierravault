# Edward Grabowski's The Blue & The Gray

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 19, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Edward Grabowski's The Blue & The Gray* is a wargame of the American Civil War, published by Impressions for DOS in 1993 and for the Amiga in 1994.[^ref-1][^ref-12] Players command either the Federal (blue) or Confederate (gray) army from 1861. On a strategic map they move divisions in weekly turns, and when opposing forces meet the battle can be fought out in Impressions' real-time "Micro Miniatures" system.[^ref-1][^ref-6] Edward Grabowski designed and programmed the game, and MobyGames credits the DOS version to his company, Edward Grabowski Communications Ltd., together with Impressions.[^ref-1]

Impressions, founded by David Lester, was bought by Sierra On-Line in 1995.[^ref-18] Sierra's company history on MobyGames names *The Blue and The Gray* among the products Impressions was "known for".[^ref-17] In 1998 Sierra republished the game for Windows 95, both on its own and in *The Great Civil War* collection.[^ref-15][^ref-16]

Reception was sharply divided. Electronic Games gave the DOS version 87%, while Computer Gaming World's M. Evan Brooks concluded: "It can be fun, but you must first quash all hopes that the game will follow anything like a historical pattern."[^ref-6][^ref-9] The Amiga version scored between 5% and 72%.[^ref-12] The game's engine was reused for Impressions' *D-Day: The Beginning of the End* in 1994.[^ref-8]

## Story Summary

The game has no story in the adventure-game sense. It is a historical campaign that begins with the war's opening months.[^ref-1] According to MobyGames, the player "control[s] either the Federal (blue) or Confederate (gray) army, starting in July 1861" and has four years to win by capturing the other side's cities and defeating its armies.[^ref-1] The computer uses historical records to set the recruitment of forces and the availability of weapons over time, so that both sides have roughly the resources they had in the real war.[^ref-1] At significant dates, a screen describes a historical battle of the actual war. Brooks found these interludes interesting but noted that they rarely matched what was happening in the player's own campaign.[^ref-6]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The game is mouse-driven. On the strategic map, the right mouse button selects a unit and the left button issues movement orders, in what Computer Gaming World likened to a Windows-style interface.[^ref-6] Ships and railroad units often have to be moved hex by hex, and an "undo" command reverses the most recent order.[^ref-6] Amiga reviewers found the controls the weakest point. Amiga Format complained that the system was "so insensitive you find yourself having to click three or four times", and CU Amiga's Paul Presley said the battle interface needed to be "more useable".[^ref-11]

### Structure and Progression

Computer Gaming World described the game as two separate games in one package:[^ref-6]

- **Strategic level:** Weekly turns on a map running from Maine to Florida and from the East Coast to west of the Mississippi River. Units can travel by rail and ship, and railroads can be destroyed.[^ref-4][^ref-6]
- **Tactical level:** Battles fought "using the familiar Impressions' Micro-Miniatures system", with an "Autoplay" option that resolves them automatically.[^ref-6]
- **Victory:** Each battle won and each city held is worth a victory point, and Washington and Richmond are worth ten. The game ends when one side leads by 100 points.[^ref-6]
- **Options:** Advanced options add fog of war and supply rules.[^ref-6][^ref-9]
- **Historical battle:** The First Battle of Bull Run is included as a standalone battle, which Electronic Games described as "a semi-tutorial".[^ref-6][^ref-9]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

In battle, units can be grouped into large formations or used individually. Amiga Computing noted that they can form two-deep lines, skirmish formations or squares, and that each unit has firepower, attack, defence, quality and morale ratings.[^ref-11] Troop quality matters a great deal: Brooks advised players to fight small, lopsided battles to "see the elephant" and raise their troops' quality.[^ref-6] The battle display is limited to 115 units regardless of army size.[^ref-6] With "over 100 battles to fight", Brooks found it "all too easy to use the 'Autoplay' function".[^ref-6] Players can also customise the colours of their troops' uniforms, a cosmetic touch that Brooks said reveals "the designer's eye for detail".[^ref-6]

Electronic Games' Ed Dille called it "a very satisfying look at the Civil War". He added that purists "will appreciate the loyalty the system maintains to the essence of combat during the period".[^ref-9] PC Zone concluded: "Despite these criticisms and a strange bug that prevented units from leaving trains this is a great effort from Impressions that will go a long way towards erasing the memory of the earlier games."[^ref-10]

Computer Gaming World's M. Evan Brooks praised the graphics as "well executed" and the sound as "superb". He noted that the background music includes "Battle Hymn of the Republic, Roll, Alabama, Roll, Fall of Charleston and Goober Peas".[^ref-6] His objection was historical: "It looks like A House Divided, but it plays like Parcheesi." He argued that missing economics, foreign recognition and leadership rules "make any similarities of B&G to the American Civil War merely coincidental".[^ref-6] His verdict: "First 'impressions' do count, and I initially was very impressed with B&G. It was only after delving into the system that I discovered its shortcomings."[^ref-6]

The Amiga press was split. CU Amiga's Paul Presley called it "A good wargame, let down by the actual combat section", and Amiga Power's Steve Faragher summed it up as "Interesting, informative and uniformly dull".[^ref-11] Amiga Format's Rob Mead found it "too tedious for anyone but the most ardent wargamers". Amiga Joker gave it 19% and the verdict "Schrott" ("junk").[^ref-11] Amiga Computing's Stevie Kennedy, by contrast, decided that "the war gaming fan will find enough in this game to warrant buying it".[^ref-11]

### Commercial Performance

The game appeared at number 10 in the PC Research list of top-selling software printed in Computer Gaming World's February 1994 issue.[^ref-7]

### Modern Assessment

Home of the Underdogs calls it "Probably one of the worst Civil War games ever made" and quotes a later synopsis by Brooks: "As a game, it could be fun; as history, it was a disaster."[^ref-13] MobyGames lists a critic average of 57% from 13 ratings, and Lemon Amiga calculates an average of 51% across eight Amiga reviews.[^ref-1][^ref-12]

## Development

### Origins

Computer Gaming World reported on the game in its June 1993 issue as "the next proposed title in Impressions' 'Miniature Series'", due in the second quarter. Impressions promised "superb graphics, a campaign game and fully animated battles", with up to 200 animated figures in each battle.[^ref-2] At the European Computer Trade Show that year, Grabowski told CGW: "We are using some of the best people available to ensure the most historical accurate miniatures wargame...this will be the best release, and we are very excited by it."[^ref-3] By November 1993, CGW reported that the title would carry the designer's name, "not to be outdone by Sid Meier in the name recognition department".[^ref-5]

In an interview printed with the Electronic Games review, Grabowski said: "I've played miniatures for about 20 years, so this system has been influenced by a lot of different rules. The closest ancestor of The Blue & Gray model is a game I did about three years ago called Rourke's Drift."[^ref-9] He added that a first battleset with Gettysburg, Antietam and Second Bull Run was in the works, "as well as an option for modem play".[^ref-9]

### Production

MobyGames lists the DOS team as follows:[^ref-1]

- **Design and programming:** Edward Grabowski
- **Development:** Christopher Bamford, Steven Serafino, Matthew Karl
- **Art:** Erik Casey, Rob Land, Jennifer Schlickbernd
- **Cartography:** Margaret Grabowski, John Allen, Robert Snowden
- **Music:** William Sherman Barnard
- **Manuals:** Christopher J. Foster, Hosea Battles Jr.

The package contained four manuals: a technical supplement and tutorial, a campaign manual, a Micro Miniatures battle manual, and a history of the Civil War.[^ref-6] Impressions' advertising promised "a FREE copy of a specially commissioned book on the Civil War". Brooks reported that limited editions also included a video about the war.[^ref-4][^ref-6] Amiga Computing described the history as "an excellent 180-page history of the Civil War".[^ref-11]

### Technical Specifications

- **DOS:** 286 or better, VGA graphics, 2.6 MB hard drive space and a mouse. Sound support included AdLib (Gold), Aria, Gravis UltraSound, Roland and Sound Blaster (Pro). One or two players, no copy protection.[^ref-6]
- **Amiga:** 1 MB RAM. Lemon Amiga and Amiga Computing list it on two disks, while CU Amiga lists three. It could be installed to hard disk.[^ref-11][^ref-12]

## Legacy

The Blue & The Gray's engine became the basis for Grabowski's next Impressions wargame. In its CES report for March 1994, Computer Gaming World said that *D-Day* was "Based on an improved The Blue & The Gray engine" and would add distinctions between leaders.[^ref-8]

After Sierra acquired Impressions, the game returned under the Sierra name. *The Great Civil War* (1998), published by Sierra On-Line, bundled "an updated, native Windows 95 version" of The Blue & The Gray with Impressions' *Robert E. Lee: Civil War General* and *Grant – Lee – Sherman: Civil War Generals 2*.[^ref-15] Sierra also sold it on its own as a 1998 Windows 95 CD-ROM.[^ref-16]
