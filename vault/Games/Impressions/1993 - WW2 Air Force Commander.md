# WW2 Air Force Commander

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

WW2 Air Force Commander is a real-time air war strategy game released in 1993 by [[Impressions Games]], a follow-up to the original Air Force Commander (1992)[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-20]. Set during World War II, the game lets players choose between historical scenarios of aerial combat between the Allies and the Axis powers[^ref-1][^ref-3]. It is unusual among wargames in dealing almost exclusively with the leadership of an air force[^ref-4], an idea *Computer Gaming World* described as separating "the air war from other aspects" and boiling "the decisions down to the basics"[^ref-20].

The box invited players to "Reach for the Sky with this Epic World War 2 Air Warfare Simulation" and advertised "28 historic WWII scenarios"[^ref-5]. In fact the game has 14 scenarios, each playable from either side, which is how the box reaches 28[^ref-20][^ref-21]. *Game Players PC Entertainment* called that count Impressions' "rather disingenuous claim"[^ref-21]. The scenarios range from Germany against the RAF in Norway, the simplest, to the entire air war in Europe, and they include Germany against Poland and the Battle of Britain[^ref-20]. Unlike its predecessor's modern Middle Eastern setting, the game deals entirely with the Second World War[^ref-1][^ref-21].

In the United States the game was a standalone release, and *Game Players PC Entertainment* reviewed it at $19.99[^ref-21]. In Europe the World War II scenarios appeared instead in a re-released *Air Force Commander* that bundled them with the original Middle East scenarios (see [[#Release History|Release History]])[^ref-22][^ref-23].

## Story Summary

WW2 Air Force Commander casts the player as an air force commander in World War II who leads aerial operations across several theatres of the conflict[^ref-3]. There is no narrative beyond the historical setup of each scenario. The 14 scenarios cover "everything from the Battle of Britain and the Allied bomber offensives of 1944-45 to various what-if situations"[^ref-21].

Players can command either the Allies or Germany[^ref-4][^ref-20]. *Computer Gaming World* noted that each side of each scenario is rated for difficulty. The ratings run from "a 'two' for playing Germany against the outmatched Poles" up to a "nine" for one side of the full Battle of Britain scenario[^ref-20].

According to the manual, as reported by *Computer Gaming World*, prolonged bombing of civilian targets will make the enemy population "so unhappy that they will overthrow the government and sue for peace"[^ref-20]. A scenario ends when one side has "attained complete air superiority or wreaked so much havoc on the other's infrastructure that its leaders capitulate"[^ref-21].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

WW2 Air Force Commander presents the war from a top-down perspective[^ref-1]. The game "uses the mouse heavily": the left button zooms in or gives more information, and the right button zooms out[^ref-20]. A Microsoft-compatible mouse was recommended[^ref-5]. The map fills most of the screen. Land is black and oceans dark blue, with airfields shown as white dots, military targets as orange dots and civilian targets as blue dots. Squadrons appear as columns whose height represents altitude[^ref-20].

Play is in real time, not turn-based[^ref-20][^ref-21]. The game clock has five speeds, and *Computer Gaming World* warned that the default, speed four ("normal"), is "far too fast for the beginning of any scenario"[^ref-20]. The game also "won't pause to let you give orders to squadrons", because it "unpauses" automatically as soon as an order is given[^ref-20]. *Game Players PC Entertainment* found the interface "badly suited to the demands of real-time game play", mainly because orders can be issued to only one squadron at a time[^ref-21].

### Structure and Progression

- **Scenario Selection:** 14 scenarios, rated for complexity from one (Germany vs. the RAF in Norway) to nine (the entire air war in Europe)[^ref-20]
- **Side Selection:** Each scenario can be played from either side, giving "28 choices in all"[^ref-20]
- **Single-player only:** "Your only option is to play against the computer"[^ref-20]. *Game Players PC Entertainment* called it "strictly a solitaire affair"[^ref-21], and FreeGameEmpire lists "No Multiplayer"[^ref-8]
- **Resource Management:** Managing bases, pilots and aircraft[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Core Mechanics

**Air Force Management:** Players manage bases, pilots and aircraft types including bombers and fighters[^ref-1][^ref-4]. Every squadron in every scenario "starts in mothballs". Once the first one is activated, enemy planes rise from their bases and head for the player's[^ref-20].

**Offence and Defence:** Most scenarios require the player "to act in both an offensive and defensive capacity, dispatching bombers to strike the enemy's installations while sending up fighter patrols to protect friendly air space"[^ref-21].

**Economy:** "There's also an economic component thrown in to add a bit more intricacy to planning". Nations generally cannot keep their whole air force operational, so the player must decide which squadrons to activate and when to divert resources to civilian needs[^ref-21]. *Computer Gaming World* framed one of the basic decisions as fuel allocation: "Since I don't have enough fuel to put all my planes in the air, how much should I allocate for fighters and how much for bombers?"[^ref-20]

**Abstractions:** *Computer Gaming World* noted that there are no weather rules or day/night considerations. A squadron in the air is represented by a single aircraft, so it can lose at most one plane per mission. Destroyed airbases disappear from the map permanently[^ref-20].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

**Computer Gaming World** (Issue 116, March 1994, Bob Proctor, "Bombs Away!") was broadly positive about it as a game and critical of it as a simulation[^ref-20]. Proctor called the box's "simulation" claim "just marketing flak", but concluded: "In spite of its failure as a simulation, AFC is a good game". He summed it up as "an interesting and playable game. As long as you don't believe what you read on the box and expect a state-of-the-art simulation, you won't be disappointed"[^ref-20]. He placed it "in the same category as Empire". He also noted that for anyone on a "low end" system, "this may be one of the better wargames you can still run"[^ref-20].

**Game Players PC Entertainment** (Vol. 7 No. 2, March/April 1994, Jeff Seiken) was negative. Seiken observed that apart from the change of era, "the game plays exactly the same as the first, and therein lies the problem"[^ref-21]. He criticised the "spartan graphics and lack of data displays" and the "clumsy interface". He also noted that Impressions "slashed the price to $19.99", calling this "a reasonable amount to pay for leftover goods"[^ref-21].

**Amiga Joker** (November 1993) reviewed the European Amiga re-release, titled *Air Force Commander*, and scored it 37%[^ref-22]. The reviewer criticised Impressions for carrying over the earlier game's control system, confusing map, missing two-player mode and minimal sound effects unchanged. They called it "ein klarer Fall von Beutelschneiderei" ("a clear case of profiteering")[^ref-22].

**PC Player** (April 1994, Thomas Werner) reviewed the European PC release, which offered both the Second World War over Europe and the Middle East[^ref-23]. Werner liked the real-time factor and the concept. However, he advised against even installing the World War scenario: "Von einer Installation des Weltkriegs-Szenario kann ich sogar gänzlich abraten, da hier absolut nichts mehr stimmt" ("I would even advise against installing the World War scenario altogether, since nothing at all is right here"). He gave two examples: the German side's airfield screen shows British fighters, and Hannover sits on the same latitude as Essen[^ref-23].

*German quotations are given in the original, with SierraVault's own English translations; obvious OCR errors in the archive.org scans have been corrected.*

### Modern Assessment

Modern coverage is largely limited to abandonware sites. FreeGameEmpire writes that "if you are a big fan of combat games and wish to fight in the air, then this game is basically made a must try for you"[^ref-8]. My Abandonware users rate it 4.44/5 (9 votes)[^ref-10].

## Development

### Origins

Air Force Commander (1992) was sold as "an expandable system". Impressions' 1992 catalogue describes it as "supplied with a Middle East War Module" and adds: "A second War Module covering World War 2 will be available seperately" [sic][^ref-24]. WW2 Air Force Commander is that World War II follow-up. *Game Players PC Entertainment* described it as a sequel that "shifts the focus from the modern Middle East to the great aerial campaigns that raged across Europe during the Second World War"[^ref-21].

Impressions Games was founded by David Lester. He sold the company to Sierra On-Line in 1995[^ref-25], two years after this game's release.

### Production

*Computer Gaming World* credits the design to David Lester and Mark Saunders[^ref-20]. *Game Players PC Entertainment* noted that "the game plays exactly the same as the first", and that the original's shortcomings were "harder to excuse now that Impressions has had more than a year to tweak the design"[^ref-21]. According to My Abandonware, Impressions later improved the Air Force Commander engine "somewhat" and used it for Global Domination and When Two Worlds War[^ref-9].

The Amiga version of the original Air Force Commander was developed by Golden Sector[^ref-9].

### Technical Specifications

**DOS Version:**[^ref-5][^ref-20]
- **Minimum CPU:** 286 or better
- **Recommended CPU:** 386 or better
- **RAM:** 640K
- **Graphics:** 256-colour VGA[^ref-20]
- **Operating System:** MS-DOS 3.0 or higher
- **Storage:** Hard drive required
- **Audio Support:** PC speaker, AdLib, Sound Blaster[^ref-20]
- **Input:** Microsoft-compatible mouse recommended
- **Media:** 3.5-inch high-density diskette[^ref-5]

*Computer Gaming World* noted that the game was small enough that it "did not require that I remove something else from my hard disk", and told owners of 286 systems: "here's one you can actually play!"[^ref-20]

### Technical Issues

*Computer Gaming World* reported several quirks[^ref-20]:
- There is no way to turn the sound effects off, and "the mouse goes dead whenever the sounds play"
- There is no "Quit" command; "The only way to stop playing is to reboot", unless running under Windows or a memory manager. The game "runs nicely under Windows", but needs a PIF file, and there were no instructions on how to make one
- The computer will not warn when a target is out of range. It launches the mission, flies it to the limit of its range and then aborts it

**US vs. European releases:** The 1992 catalogue planned the World War II content as a separately sold "War Module"[^ref-24]. In the US it shipped as the standalone *WW2 Air Force Commander*, which *Computer Gaming World* and *Game Players PC Entertainment* reviewed as a game in its own right[^ref-20][^ref-21]. European magazines instead reviewed a combined *Air Force Commander* that carried the World War II scenarios alongside the original Middle East ones. Amiga Joker described it as having "neuem Namen, neuer Verpackung" ("a new name, new packaging")[^ref-22]. MobyGames lists "Air Force Commander: WW II" as the game's European title[^ref-1].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game came with a 34-page user manual[^ref-5]
- WW2 Air Force Commander is also known as "Air Force Commander: WW II"[^ref-1], and *Game Players PC Entertainment* reviewed it as "Air Force Commander: WW2"[^ref-21]
- It was Impressions' second game about air force command, after the original Air Force Commander[^ref-4][^ref-24]
- The original Air Force Commander's manual included 43 pages of Middle East history[^ref-11]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for WW2 Air Force Commander are available. Impressions later became best known for its City Building Series, including Caesar and Pharaoh (1999), and was part of Sierra On-Line from 1995[^ref-25].

### Preservation

The game is playable through browser-based emulation on several abandonware and retro gaming sites[^ref-4][^ref-7][^ref-14]. No fan remakes are known.

### Related Publications

- **User Manual:** 34-page manual included with the game[^ref-5]
- **Original Air Force Commander Manual:** 43 pages of Middle East history included with the predecessor game[^ref-11]

### Critical Perspective

WW2 Air Force Commander is a small, low-specification real-time wargame that its contemporary critics judged very differently. *Computer Gaming World* found "an interesting and playable game" once the box's simulation claims were set aside[^ref-20]. *Game Players PC Entertainment* and the German press saw it as recycled material[^ref-21][^ref-22][^ref-23]. Its main historical interest is as an early Impressions experiment in air-war strategy, from before the studio's later city-building success[^ref-24][^ref-25].
