# Breach 3

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

Breach 3 is a squad-level tactical combat game set in a futuristic science fiction universe, released in 1995 for MS-DOS.[^ref-1] Developed jointly by Omnitrend Software and Impressions Games, the title represents the third entry in the Breach series that began with the original 1987 game.[^ref-1] The game places players in command of an elite squadron of marines in the Federated Worlds Special Forces, tasking them with missions of grave strategic importance including defending their home against the United Democratic Planets, obtaining critical data, rescuing political prisoners, and seizing control of vital emplacements.[^ref-2]

The game distinguishes itself from contemporaries like X-COM through its top-down perspective rather than an isometric viewpoint, featuring real-time combat that pauses automatically when players need to make important decisions.[^ref-1] Each marine under the player's command possesses a unique personality that determines how far they'll actually follow orders, adding a layer of unpredictability to tactical planning.[^ref-3] Breach 3 was marketed as "easy to play yet challenging to master," promising to set "new standards in playability" for the genre.[^ref-3]

Perhaps most notably, Breach 3 was designed as part of Omnitrend's "Interlocking Game System," allowing it to link up with the space combat game Rules of Engagement 2.[^ref-21] Character attributes and experience could be transferred between the two games, creating a comprehensive science fiction warfare experience spanning both capital ship combat and ground-based squad operations.[^ref-5] Omnitrend had signed with Impressions in 1992 to release Breach 3 and Rules of Engagement 2,[^ref-21] but Breach 3 did not reach shelves until late 1995, a delay *Computer Gaming World* reckoned at two years.[^ref-19]

## Story Summary

The narrative of Breach 3 unfolds within Omnitrend's expansive Universe setting, a science fiction universe that spans multiple games including Universe, Rules of Engagement, and the Breach series.[^ref-8] The game's lore establishes that the story begins with The First Stellar Expansion, which commenced on May 10, 2029 CE with the first practical demonstration of the hyperdrive field effect.[^ref-18] This pivotal test was carried out in Building 4 of Area 117 at the Franklin Labs Research Range in Hawthorne, Nevada.[^ref-18]

Five years later, NASA deployed the unmanned Odysseus I and II vehicles to test the practicality of hyperdrive for interplanetary and interstellar travel.[^ref-2][^ref-18] Humanity subsequently expanded across the stars, eventually forming competing interstellar governments: the Federated Worlds and the United Democratic Planets. By the time of Breach 3, these two powers find themselves locked in strategic conflict.

Players assume the role of a squad leader in the elite Federated Worlds Special Forces, commanding a team of highly trained marines on missions critical to the security of the Federated Worlds.[^ref-2] The campaigns involve operations against both human enemies from the United Democratic Planets and more exotic threats including aliens and robots.[^ref-1] The squad leader's career spans multiple missions, with the commander gaining experience from each successful operation—assuming they survive, as the squad leader's death ends the campaign.[^ref-1][^ref-18]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Breach 3 employs a top-down perspective for its tactical combat, distinguishing it from the isometric view popularized by X-COM: UFO Defense.[^ref-1] The interface supports both keyboard and mouse input,[^ref-1] and orders can be given to marines "individually or as a group."[^ref-9] Combat unfolds in real-time, but the game automatically pauses when players need to make important decisions, creating a hybrid system that maintains tension while allowing thoughtful tactical planning.[^ref-1]

The game features a real-time environment that permits stopping time to plan strategies, giving players the opportunity to carefully consider their options before committing to action.[^ref-9] Marines can be selected and commanded individually, though the personality system means they may not always follow orders exactly as given—some marines will go further for their commander than others based on their individual characteristics.[^ref-3]

### Structure and Progression

Breach 3 features multiple distinct campaigns; strategy tips compiled by the game's playtesting squad (reproduced on ClassicReload) describe them as follows:[^ref-10]

- **Foxhunt:** A less combat-intensive campaign featuring wide-open country and city complexes, serving as an accessible introduction to the game's systems.
- **X Campaign:** A moderately difficult campaign involving cooperation with the mysterious X-Group to stop an alien threat.
- **Owls Campaign:** Described as a very hard campaign requiring substantial resources including many medkits, rockets, and grenades for survival.
- **Dole Campaign:** Considered the hardest campaign of all, featuring over 34 possible endings and requiring multiple playthroughs for even experienced squad leaders to achieve complete victory.[^ref-10]

The playtesters' tips note that "Any squad leader who can successfully complete both Owls and Dole deserves a week vacation on the planet Humidor," emphasizing the extreme difficulty of the later campaigns.[^ref-10]

A unique progression element involves the squad leader character, who advances through ranks from Lieutenant Commander up to Fleet Admiral.[^ref-10][^ref-18] The leader gains experience from completed missions and can earn medals including the FW Cross, Distinguished Service, Purple Heart, Distinguished Career Cluster, and the prestigious FW Medal of Honor.[^ref-10] However, the death of the squad leader automatically ends the campaign, though the manual notes that the leader's permanent record remains on file so he may be reused for another campaign.[^ref-18]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Resource management forms a core gameplay pillar, with the playtesting squad's tips stating that "Breach 3 is a game where sound tactics and good resource management can make the difference between a successful squad leader and a dead one."[^ref-10] Players must carefully manage equipment including medkits, ammunition, rockets, grenades, and specialized gear.

Environmental hazards add tactical complexity, including hull breaches that can suck marines into space during starship-based missions.[^ref-10] Each marine possesses individual statistics and characteristics that affect their combat effectiveness and behavior.[^ref-9] Equipment recovery after missions allows successful commanders to build up their arsenal over time.

The game includes a powerful scenario builder for creating custom missions and campaigns, along with a campaign builder whose missions can also be chained to Rules of Engagement 2 as planetary landing and ship boarding scenarios.[^ref-9][^ref-18] This feature positions Breach 3 as both a standalone game and a platform for user-generated content.

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Breach 3's reception was largely negative, with critics noting that while the game engine remained solid, the long-delayed title had failed to keep pace with competitors such as X-COM in the tactical combat genre.[^ref-19][^ref-12]

*Computer Gaming World* (January 1996) ran Martin E. Cirulis's review under the headline "Explosive Decompression". Recalling an ad for the game from early 1994, Cirulis wrote that after an hour or so he had "only one burning thought: 'This thing took two bloody years?!'"[^ref-19] He judged that "sometimes a game that takes two years to reach the shelves is just a game that is 730 days behind the times," and concluded that "Breach 3 runs well on a 386, so did X-COM; and the former isn't half the game of the latter."[^ref-19] He did credit the scenario and campaign builder and the link with Rules of Engagement 2, which he said "redeems the game somewhat for the wargamer or SF fan who must have everything."[^ref-19] The magazine's verdict box listed "Nice scenario/campaign builder. Integrates with Rules of Engagement 2" as pros and "Outdated graphics and woefully insignificant gameplay compared to the turn-based X-COM" among the cons.[^ref-19]

*PC Gamer US* awarded the game 77%, with reviewer T. Liam McDonald calling it "an entertaining return to the days when this was groundbreaking gameplay," a review more positive than most.[^ref-7]

*Computer Game Review* gave the game scores of 84/80/77 from reviewers Frank Snyder, Ted Chapman, and Tasos Kaiafas respectively.[^ref-7] The editors praised the "Excellent game engine" but criticized the "Amazingly few scenarios for a game that thrives on scenarios."[^ref-9] They notably commented that the game "would've been awesome if it had been released on schedule," suggesting development delays may have hurt its competitive position.[^ref-9]

*PC Games* (January 1996; listed by Wikipedia as *PC Entertainment*, with a C− grade) was considerably more critical.[^ref-7] Reviewer Christopher Lindquist delivered a harsh assessment: "Breach 3's big problem is that the merc-gaming world has become much richer in the past couple of years, but Breach has not."[^ref-12] He elaborated that "Games like X-COM and Jagged Alliance have set a new standard for what to expect in merc-style games: compelling storylines, sophisticated artificial intelligence, good graphics, and deep game play. Breach 3 contains none of the above."[^ref-12] Lindquist concluded with the dismissive recommendation to "Let this one rest in peace."[^ref-12]

The earlier games had fared better: in a 1992 survey *Computer Gaming World* called Breach 2 "Easy to learn and fun to play,"[^ref-8] and the Home of the Underdogs database later described the original Breach and Breach 2 as "definitely ahead of its time."[^ref-6]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective analysis has been sparse, with the game largely remembered as a curiosity from the transitional period of tactical gaming. MobyGames aggregates a critics average of 46%, reflecting the largely negative contemporary reception.[^ref-1]

One particularly negative assessment from MobyGames user Isdaron described it as "one of the worst games ever," criticizing the game as "a curiosity. Not only is it the third part in a series that did only showcase games from bad to worse, it is also one of the most uninterestingly designed games I was ever allowed to play."[^ref-13]

## Development

### Origins

The Breach series originated with Omnitrend Software, a company that developed an ambitious interconnected universe of games spanning different genres and gameplay styles.[^ref-8] The original Breach released in 1987 as a turn-based tactical squad combat game, introducing team-oriented missions, RPG-style squad leader development, and a scenario builder.[^ref-11] Breach 2 followed in 1990, expanding on the formula with enhanced graphics and additional features.[^ref-8]

In 1992 *Computer Gaming World* reported that Omnitrend, which had designed Breach 2 and Rules of Engagement to interface but saw the "Interlocking Game System" go largely unmarketed because the two games had different distributors, had signed an agreement with Impressions to release Breach 3 and Rules of Engagement 2 "with particular emphasis on the interlocking nature of the games."[^ref-21] Home of the Underdogs later wrote that Impressions "picked up the engine" after what it called Omnitrend's demise,[^ref-6] though the company did not disappear: Omnitrend Software still publishes its 1983 game Universe on GOG.com.[^ref-23] The game was advertised as early as 1994 but did not ship until late 1995, and reviewers singled out the delay as the reason it felt dated on release.[^ref-19][^ref-9]

Thomas Carbone, who served as designer on the project, co-created Omnitrend's first game, Universe (1983), with William G. M. Leslie;[^ref-22] a GOG community commenter describes him as Omnitrend's CEO.[^ref-16]

### Production

The development involved collaboration between the original Omnitrend team and Impressions Games. Impressions, founded in 1989 and focusing primarily on strategy and business games including sport simulations,[^ref-2] was acquired by Sierra On-Line in June 1995[^ref-20]; Breach 3 shipped later that year, with *Computer Game Review* listing Sierra On-Line as distributor and Impressions as developer.[^ref-9]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-1]
- **Designers:** Thomas R. Carbone, Christopher Bamford, Scott Woodrick
- **Composers:** Jeremy A. Bell, Jay Rinaldi
- **Publisher:** Impressions Games[^ref-1][^ref-19]; distributed by Sierra On-Line[^ref-9]

The game featured 256-color VGA graphics,[^ref-9] and it dropped the "fake isometric" diagonal tile view used in Breach 2.[^ref-8]

### Technical Achievements

Breach 3 utilized the AIL / Miles Sound System for its audio engine, a widely adopted middleware solution for DOS games of the era.[^ref-1] The game supported 256-color VGA graphics with digitized sound and speech, and *Computer Gaming World* noted that most major sound cards were supported.[^ref-9][^ref-19]

The interlocking game system represented an ambitious technical achievement, allowing character data to transfer between Breach 3 and Rules of Engagement 2.[^ref-4] Users reported that getting this integration to work required obtaining the absolute latest patches of both games.[^ref-16] When functional, players could command capital ships in ROE2 and then deploy their marines in ground operations through Breach 3, creating a comprehensive warfare simulation spanning multiple scales of combat.

### Technical Issues

*PC Games* reported several bugs in the shipped 1995 game:[^ref-12]

- Medical kit usage could reportedly cause the system to reboot
- Marines occasionally got lost and wandered through doors without orders
- Selected marines inconsistently acknowledged orders

Modern players running the game in DOSBox have reported further problems in user comments on the Internet Archive and MyAbandonware. These concern today's downloadable copies rather than the original release:

- Installation difficulties in DOSBox environments[^ref-5]
- A sound error when running the CD version, even after setup (MyAbandonware user report)[^ref-14]
- The install folder must be named in capitals, "B3" (MyAbandonware user report)[^ref-14]
- Files must be copied by hand from the CD's subfolders into the B3 folder (MyAbandonware user reports)[^ref-14]

The PC Games Magazine review noted that the box showed marines with color photos, but the actual game used grainy black-and-white character portraits.[^ref-12]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The game included several command-line parameters that functioned as level access cheats:[^ref-17]

- **malkavian** – Parameter accessing Dole campaign level
- **ender** – Parameter accessing Fox Hunt campaign level
- **pinback** – Parameter accessing Owls campaign level
- **cragmorton** – Parameter accessing X campaign level
- **lordplugs** – Parameter accessing ZFW level
- **pugsicus** – Parameter accessing ZUD level

The last five items listed in the campaign selection menu were actually component sets for building custom campaigns rather than playable content, a detail that confused some players.[^ref-12]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No specific sales figures have been documented for Breach 3. The game's release followed Sierra On-Line's June 1995 acquisition of Impressions Games, becoming part of Sierra's broader strategy game portfolio.[^ref-20]

The game largely faded from commercial availability following its initial release, eventually appearing on various abandonware sites as the years passed. GOG.com sells the earlier Breach and Breach 2 (published there by Throwback Entertainment),[^ref-24][^ref-25] but neither GOG.com nor Steam carries Breach 3, though community wishlists indicate ongoing interest in seeing the game (and its companion Rules of Engagement 2) made available through legitimate digital storefronts.[^ref-16]

### Collections

Breach 3 does not appear to have been included in any significant game compilations following its initial release. The game remained tied to its original DOS platform without receiving ports or updates for later operating systems.

### Fan Projects

The game's scenario builder and campaign creator features encouraged user-generated content, though documentation of specific community projects is limited. The GOG.com community wishlist page discusses the continued interest in preserving and making accessible both Breach 3 and Rules of Engagement 2, with one commenter noting that getting the two games to interlock is possible but needs the latest patches of both.[^ref-16]

### Related Publications

- **Game Reference Manual:** Included with the game, providing comprehensive documentation of gameplay systems, unit statistics, and campaign information.[^ref-5]
- **Quick Reference Card:** A summary card for quick access to controls and key information.[^ref-5]
- **Tutorial and Technical Support Documentation:** Separate materials included to assist new players.[^ref-5]

### Critical Perspective

Breach 3 occupies an unfortunate position in tactical gaming history, arriving at precisely the wrong moment. Released in 1995, it competed directly against landmark titles like X-COM: UFO Defense (1994) and Jagged Alliance (1994), both of which had dramatically raised expectations for the genre.[^ref-7] Where those games featured isometric graphics, sophisticated AI, compelling narratives, and deep strategic systems, Breach 3 offered a competent but aging engine that critics felt had failed to evolve with the times.

The game represents both the ambition and the tragedy of Omnitrend Software's vision. Their concept of interlocking games that could share data across titles drew attention as early as 1992.[^ref-21] However, Breach 3 arrived years behind schedule, and reviewers judged that the delay had cost it its competitive prospects.[^ref-9][^ref-19]

Many of its signature features were not new to the series: the 1987 Breach had already introduced RPG-style squad leader development and a scenario builder.[^ref-11] *Computer Gaming World* judged that its link with Rules of Engagement 2 and its scenario and campaign builder redeemed it only "somewhat," while its graphics and gameplay fell well short of X-COM.[^ref-19]
