# Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress is a fantasy role-playing game created by Richard Garriott, with programming credits in the manual also going to Kenneth Arnold and Keith Zabalaoui,[^ref-2][^ref-13] released for the Apple II in 1982.[^ref-1][^ref-10][^ref-23] The exact release date is uncertain: Wikipedia and IGDB give August 24, 1982, but Softline's May 1982 dealer price list already shows the game as "NEW," while Jimmy Maher places its debut at that December's AppleFest.[^ref-1][^ref-10][^ref-23][^ref-22] It is the only new numbered Ultima first published by Sierra On-Line, though Sierra also re-released the original Ultima and published the spin-off [[1983 - Ultima - Escape from Mt. Drash]].[^ref-12] Taking place across multiple time periods on Earth rather than the fantasy land of Sosaria from the first game, the game features an ambitious time-travel premise inspired by the 1981 Terry Gilliam film Time Bandits.[^ref-1][^ref-11]

The game marked several significant firsts for the Ultima franchise: it was the first game in the series to include a cloth map as part of its packaging,[^ref-1][^ref-13] the first Ultima game Garriott wrote completely in assembly language,[^ref-1] and the first game in the series officially ported to platforms other than the Apple II.[^ref-1][^ref-3] Sources differ on how long development took: the manual's author's note says "more than fourteen months," Maher says "a full eighteen months," and the Official Book says almost two years, longer than it should have been because Garriott was attending the University of Texas.[^ref-13][^ref-22][^ref-11] Before starting it he learned assembly language in an intensive, roughly month-long "boot camp" with programmer Tom Luhrs.[^ref-22][^ref-24]

Ultima II sold well, and Garriott soon began to fall out with Sierra; in 1983 he, his brother Robert, their father and Chuck Bueche founded Origin Systems. Accounts of the cause differ (see Legacy).[^ref-11] Contemporary reviews praised the game's ambitious scope and technical achievements, with Softline writing that "Lord British's sequel to the legendary Ultima continues the interplanetary fantasy saga with a creative programming flair far beyond the scope of most fantasy or adventure games."[^ref-16]

## Story Summary

The game takes place after the events of Ultima I, in which the hero defeated the evil wizard Mondain. However, Mondain had an apprentice and lover named Minax, a powerful enchantress who seeks revenge for her master's death.[^ref-1] Minax has manipulated time itself, creating temporal distortions that threaten to unravel reality across all of Earth's history.[^ref-1]

Unlike the first Ultima, set in the fantasy land of Sosaria, Ultima II unfolds on Earth across different time periods; later games in the series retconned its events as having taken place in Sosaria.[^ref-1][^ref-3] The player must navigate through various eras of human history, utilizing time doors that allow passage between different epochs.[^ref-3] The map and time-travel mechanics were directly inspired by the film Time Bandits; Garriott and his friends returned to $1.00 matinees of the film again and again in 1981 to sketch the time map shown on screen.[^ref-11][^ref-2]

The quest culminates in a confrontation with Minax, who can only be defeated using a special weapon called the Quicksword Enilno. When spelled backward, "Enilno" becomes "Online," a clever reference to the game's publisher, Sierra On-Line.[^ref-2] In the map of the solar system, Earth is notably located at coordinates (6,6,6), adding a subtle ominous touch to the world design.[^ref-2][^ref-14]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Ultima II utilizes a keyboard-driven interface with single-key commands for various actions, listed on the key-letter reference card packed with the game.[^ref-14][^ref-16] The game presents the world through a top-down tile-based perspective, with the player character navigating through overworld maps, dungeons, and eventually space.[^ref-2] Input is handled entirely through keyboard commands, following the conventions established in the original Ultima.[^ref-2]

### Structure and Progression

The game is structured around exploration across multiple time periods on Earth and eventually space travel to other planets in the solar system.[^ref-1] Players must gather equipment, increase their statistics, and accumulate resources to prepare for the final confrontation with Minax.[^ref-4]

- **Time Travel:** Navigation through various historical eras via time doors[^ref-3]
- **Earth Exploration:** Multiple continents and regions across different time periods[^ref-1]
- **Space Travel:** Interplanetary exploration of the solar system[^ref-2]
- **Dungeon Crawling:** First-person dungeon exploration segments[^ref-2]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The gameplay heavily emphasizes resource management and character development, requiring extensive gold accumulation to purchase necessary items and services.[^ref-4] Everything in the game requires gold, making it an extremely grindy experience by modern standards.[^ref-4] One reviewer described it as "essentially a non-stop grindfest of frustrating proportions."[^ref-4]

A bug in the original Apple II version prevents the Strength statistic from increasing after character creation; a fan patch fixes it.[^ref-3] The Ultima Codex also suggests that Lord British's reduced healing rate later in the game may have been meant to make it less likely that the HP counter rolls over, which would instantly kill the character.[^ref-3]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Ultima II received generally positive reviews upon release, though opinions varied on specific aspects of the gameplay experience. Reviewing the Apple II version in January 1983, Softline wrote that "Programming excellence and flawless execution are the trademarks" of the game and concluded: "Overall, Lord British has another, greater hit on his hands, a faster, drastically more advanced game that really is the ultimate in real-time D&D type fantasy games for the micro."[^ref-16]

Computer Gaming World's James A. McPherson reviewed the Apple II version in its March–April 1983 issue, finding the world "vastly improved over ULTIMA I", though he noted that "very little of this enormous work was really utilized as being required to complete the game."[^ref-17] K-Power reader David Langendoen, a 15-year-old reviewer, rated it 7 on the magazine's 1–10 scale in February 1984, writing that it "is more sophisticated and has a quicker pace" than Ultima I and that it "is unique and its storyline is original."[^ref-19] Wikipedia reports that Phil Wiswell's review in PC Magazine (August 21, 1984) scored the game 15.5 out of 18; the scan could not be checked for this page.[^ref-1]

Troy Christensen of Different Worlds magazine (issue 41, 1986) proclaimed that "Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress is a classic fantasy computer adventure game that is a must for anyone serious in computer gaming."[^ref-18] Softline's report on the 1983 Origins awards lists Ultima II among the nominees for "Best Adventure Game for Home Computer", which went to Sir-tech's Wizardry: Knight of Diamonds.[^ref-21]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been more mixed, with Ultima II widely considered the weakest entry in the series.[^ref-4][^ref-9] MobyGames user Salim Farhat titled his review "More like Time Bandit fanfiction than Ultima," writing that "the game wasn't so much Ultima as it was Time Bandits with a twist."[^ref-2] Daniel Saner wrote that "at most times, Ultima II more resembles work than play."[^ref-2]

As of November 2026, GOG.com's listing for Ultima II shows a 3.1 out of 5 user rating based on 18 reviews.[^ref-25] The former Ultima 1+2+3 bundle listing, as archived on October 27, 2026, showed 4 out of 5 based on 55 reviews,[^ref-4] and several of its reviewers single out Ultima II as the collection's weak link. One reviewer wrote that "Ultima 2's 4-color cyan and magenta are particularly hard to look at compared to alternatives."[^ref-4]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 7.5/10 Moby Score; critics 71% (6 ratings)[^ref-2]
- **GOG.com:** 3.1/5 (18 reviews, Ultima II listing, as of November 2026)[^ref-25]; the former Ultima 1+2+3 bundle listing showed 4/5 (55 reviews) on October 27, 2026[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

The development of Ultima II began while Richard Garriott was attending the University of Texas.[^ref-11] After California Pacific, publisher of Ultima I, went out of business in 1981, Garriott told the publishers courting him that he wanted a box, extensive documentation and, most of all, a cloth map; in his words, "every publisher but Sierra On-Line dropped out."[^ref-11][^ref-3] Maher likewise writes that the cloth map was "as un-negotiable as his royalty demands" and that Garriott found a taker in "none other than On-Line Systems";[^ref-22] Softline's March 1982 preview referred to "Lord British's new association with On-Line Systems";[^ref-24] and in 2026 Garriott said of the cloth-map request that "all of them disappeared, except Sierra, and that's why I went with Sierra."[^ref-27]

Garriott was heavily influenced by the 1981 film Time Bandits: he organized repeated trips to $1.00 matinees to copy down the film's time map, and said that "Ultima II was directly inspired by Time Bandits", from the time doors to the cloth map showing their connections.[^ref-11][^ref-2] This inspiration manifested in the game's time-travel mechanics and the design of its time doors.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Production

The Official Book says Garriott spent almost two years writing the game, longer than it should have taken because he was attending college at the same time; the manual's author's note gives "more than fourteen months" and Maher "a full eighteen months."[^ref-11][^ref-13][^ref-22] Before starting, Garriott went through an assembly-language "boot camp" of about a month with Tom Luhrs, author of Apple-oids, who "held his hand" through machine language; Garriott recalled that "Literally the first lines of machine language I wrote were the first lines I wrote for the game."[^ref-22][^ref-11] Softline's March 1982 preview likewise reported that "Last summer, Lord British took a month off from lording to learn assembly language."[^ref-24] This was the first Ultima game Garriott wrote completely in assembly language rather than BASIC.[^ref-1]

Garriott had written his first games in BASIC on his high school's teletype and Akalabeth on his own Apple at home, before tackling machine language for this game.[^ref-11] The manual's credit block lists Kenneth Arnold, Richard Garriott and Keith Zahalaoui first (MobyGames credits all three for programming, spelling the last name Zabalaoui).[^ref-13][^ref-2] Chuck Bueche, later a co-founder of Origin, did the Atari 800 version for Sierra.[^ref-11]

### Technical Achievements

Ultima II was notable for being one of the first graphical games ported to the Atari ST platform.[^ref-3]

The IBM PC version was specifically designed to be played on an IBM CGA system with a composite color monitor, which produced different color output than the standard RGB display.[^ref-2] The game was programmed entirely in assembly language, allowing for improved performance compared to interpreted BASIC programs.[^ref-1]

### Technical Issues

The IBM PC version of Ultima II is notorious for being prone to divide by zero errors and crashing.[^ref-1] The game runs too fast on modern computers since it was designed for the original IBM PC's 4.77 MHz CPU clock speed, making it unplayable without slowdown measures.[^ref-1][^ref-5]

A known issue affects the later CD-ROM re-releases: when files from all the game's disks were copied to one folder, several files with identical file names were overwritten, removing the maps of most planets other than Earth.[^ref-2][^ref-1][^ref-12] A MobyGames reviewer says this made the game unwinnable without a fan patch, but Wikipedia notes that the map for "Planet X" is intact and the game can still be won; fan patches restore the missing maps.[^ref-2][^ref-1]

Additional documented bugs include:[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-5]
- Bug preventing Strength stat increase after character creation (original Apple II version)[^ref-3]
- HP counter rollover that could instantly kill the character[^ref-3]
- Blank disk error when creating new character in GOG version (fixed in 2025)[^ref-4]
- PLAYER save cannot be overwritten in-game[^ref-5]
- Game crashes when saving with the GOG version and the fan upgrade patch unless a PLAYER.U2 directory is created[^ref-5]
- Planetary maps appearing as Earth maps in Ultima I-VI Series compilations[^ref-3]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Enilno Sword:** The weapon needed to defeat the villain is a sword called Enilno. Spelled backward, it becomes "Online"—a reference to Sierra On-Line, the game's publisher.[^ref-2]
- **Earth Coordinates:** In the map of the solar system, Earth is located at coordinates (6,6,6).[^ref-2][^ref-14]
- **Time Bandits Inspiration:** The time doors and cloth map were directly inspired by the 1981 film Time Bandits, with Garriott returning to the theater repeatedly to copy down the film's map.[^ref-11][^ref-2]
- **Cloth Map First:** This was the first game in the Ultima series to include a cloth map, a feature that became a series trademark.[^ref-1][^ref-11][^ref-13]
- **Cover Art:** Ultima II is the only installment in the series not to feature a cover painting by Denis Loubet. The dragon in the title screen was taken from the Ultima I cover.[^ref-3]
- **Pop Culture References:** The game contains numerous pop-culture references throughout.[^ref-3]
- **Celebrity Influence:** South Park co-creator Trey Parker has cited Ultima II as an early RPG influence on his work.[^ref-3]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Ultima II sold over 50,000 copies, earning a Certified Silver award from the Software Publishers Association, and nearly 100,000 copies by the early 1990s (the Official Book's second edition says "By now nearly 100,000 have been sold").[^ref-11] Accounts of why Garriott left Sierra differ. In Shay Addams's Official Book, the Ultima II contract "hadn't even mentioned royalty rates for an IBM version" and assigned all publishing rights for Ultima II to Sierra; Garriott recalls that Sierra said "here's the deal, take it or leave it," and he accepted the lower royalty rate ("I could have turned it down"). Addams quotes him saying that around Ultima III he "was beginning to fall out with Sierra," and that his brother then suggested forming their own company; the Garriotts and Bueche founded Origin in 1983.[^ref-11] In a 1986 Computer Gaming World interview, as quoted in Matt Barton's Game Developer book extract, Garriott said Sierra "did not seem very author friendly" and "I never really knew if I was getting a fair shake."[^ref-28] In a 2026 Dragon Con panel interview he said that "they also quit paying me," after which he took his trademarks back and his brother proposed the business that became Origin.[^ref-27]

The relationship between Garriott and Sierra On-Line deteriorated after the game's release, leading to difficulties when Origin Systems wanted to re-release the title.[^ref-3][^ref-12] Sierra refused to sell the rights to Origin for many years and did not relinquish the original cover artwork until 1998 at the latest.[^ref-3] There are two DOS ports of Revenge of the Enchantress; the second, released on the various Ultima collections, removes all mention of Sierra On-Line from the title.[^ref-12]

### Collections

Ultima II has been included in several compilation releases:

- **Ultima Trilogy I-II-III** (1989) – Included an enhanced version with audiovisual upgrades, exclusively available in this box set[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- **Ultima Value Pak** (1990)[^ref-3]
- **Ultima I-VI Series** (1993 per the Ultima Codex; MobyGames dates it 1992)[^ref-3][^ref-2]
- **Ultima Collection** (1998)[^ref-3][^ref-2]
- **Ultima Complete** – Japan[^ref-3]
- **Ultima 1+2+3** – GOG.com digital release (2011 onward, per PCGamingWiki);[^ref-5] the bundle's GOG page, archived October 27, 2026, is the former bundle listing,[^ref-4] and Ultima II now has its own GOG listing[^ref-25]

The 1989 enhanced version for Apple II is extremely rare due to its exclusive anthology availability.[^ref-3]

### Fan Projects

Because the re-releases lost most of the planetary map files, fans produced patches that restore them and fix other problems, including an upgrade patch that adds EGA graphics and a frame limiter to the DOS version.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Related Publications

- **Cloth Map:** Included with original game release, inspired by Time Bandits film[^ref-11][^ref-13]
- **Game Manual:** Standard documentation included with retail release[^ref-13]
- **The Official Book of Ultima:** Shay Addams's history and guide to the Ultima series (this page cites the second edition, 1992; the first edition appeared c. 1990), with a chapter on Ultima II[^ref-11]

### Critical Perspective

Ultima II occupies a curious position in RPG history as both a commercial success and a somewhat divisive gameplay experience. The game represents the Ultima series at a transitional moment—ambitious in scope but rough in execution. Its time-travel premise across Earth's history rather than the fantasy land of Sosaria distinguishes it from other entries in the series, making it feel less like a true Ultima game to many players.[^ref-2]

The game's historical significance lies in several areas: Shay Addams's Official Book says it "immediately established the state of the art for computer role-playing games and was soon widely emulated by other designers";[^ref-11] it established the cloth map as a series trademark; and Garriott's falling-out with Sierra, which the Official Book links to the royalty terms for the IBM version, led him to establish Origin Systems, which went on to publish the later Ultima games.[^ref-11][^ref-28][^ref-27][^ref-3] However, its reputation as the weakest entry in the series has persisted,[^ref-4][^ref-9] with the extremely grindy gameplay and numerous bugs overshadowing its innovative elements.
