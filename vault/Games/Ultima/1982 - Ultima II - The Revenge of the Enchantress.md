# Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress is a fantasy role-playing game designed and programmed by Richard Garriott, released for the Apple II on August 24, 1982.[^ref-1][^ref-10] The game stands as the only official Ultima title ever published by Sierra On-Line, making it a unique entry in both the Ultima series and Sierra's catalog.[^ref-1] Taking place across multiple time periods on Earth rather than the fantasy land of Sosaria from the first game, the game features an ambitious time-travel premise inspired by the 1981 Terry Gilliam film Time Bandits.[^ref-1][^ref-11]

The game marked several significant firsts for the Ultima franchise: it was the first game in the series to include a cloth map as part of its packaging,[^ref-1][^ref-13] the first Ultima game Garriott wrote completely in assembly language,[^ref-1] and the first game in the series officially ported to platforms other than the Apple II.[^ref-1][^ref-3] Development took almost two years while Garriott attended the University of Texas,[^ref-11] and before starting it he learned assembly language in an intensive, roughly month-long "boot camp" with programmer Tom Luhrs.[^ref-22]

Ultima II sold well, but a dispute with Sierra over royalties for the IBM PC port led Garriott, his brother Robert, their father and Chuck Bueche to found Origin Systems in 1983.[^ref-11][^ref-1] Contemporary reviews praised the game's ambitious scope and technical achievements, with Softline writing that "Lord British's sequel to the legendary Ultima continues the interplanetary fantasy saga with a creative programming flair far beyond the scope of most fantasy or adventure games."[^ref-16]

## Story Summary

The game takes place after the events of Ultima I, in which the hero defeated the evil wizard Mondain. However, Mondain had an apprentice and lover named Minax, a powerful enchantress who seeks revenge for her master's death.[^ref-1] Minax has manipulated time itself, creating temporal distortions that threaten to unravel reality across all of Earth's history.[^ref-1]

Unlike the first Ultima, set in the fantasy land of Sosaria, Ultima II unfolds on Earth across different time periods; later games in the series retconned its events as having taken place in Sosaria.[^ref-1][^ref-3] The player must navigate through various eras of human history, utilizing time doors that allow passage between different epochs.[^ref-3] The map and time-travel mechanics were directly inspired by the film Time Bandits; Garriott and his friends returned to $1.00 matinees of the film again and again in 1981 to sketch the time map shown on screen.[^ref-11][^ref-2]

The quest culminates in a confrontation with Minax, who can only be defeated using a special weapon called the Quicksword Enilno. When spelled backward, "Enilno" becomes "Online," a clever reference to the game's publisher, Sierra On-Line.[^ref-2] In the map of the solar system, Earth is notably located at coordinates (6,6,6), adding a subtle ominous touch to the world design.[^ref-2][^ref-14]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Ultima II utilizes a keyboard-driven interface with single-key commands for various actions, listed on the key-letter reference card packed with the game.[^ref-14][^ref-16] The game presents the world through a top-down tile-based perspective, with the player character navigating through overworld maps, dungeons, and eventually space.[^ref-2] Input is handled entirely through keyboard commands, following the conventions established in the original Ultima.[^ref-2]

### Structure and Progression

The game is structured around exploration across multiple time periods on Earth and eventually space travel to other planets in the solar system.[^ref-1] Players must gather equipment, increase their statistics, and accumulate resources to prepare for the final confrontation with Minax.[^ref-4]

- **Time Travel:** Navigation through various historical eras via time doors[^ref-3]
- **Earth Exploration:** Multiple continents and regions across different time periods[^ref-1]
- **Space Travel:** Interplanetary exploration of the solar system[^ref-2]
- **Dungeon Crawling:** First-person dungeon exploration segments[^ref-2]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The gameplay heavily emphasizes resource management and character development, requiring extensive gold accumulation to purchase necessary items and services.[^ref-4] Everything in the game requires gold, making it an extremely grindy experience by modern standards.[^ref-4] One reviewer described it as "essentially a non-stop grindfest of frustrating proportions."[^ref-4]

A bug in the original Apple II version prevents the Strength statistic from increasing after character creation; a fan patch fixes it.[^ref-3] The Ultima Codex also suggests that Lord British's reduced healing rate later in the game may have been meant to make it less likely that the HP counter rolls over, which would instantly kill the character.[^ref-3]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Ultima II received generally positive reviews upon release, though opinions varied on specific aspects of the gameplay experience. Reviewing the Apple II version in January 1983, Softline wrote that "Programming excellence and flawless execution are the trademarks" of the game and concluded: "Overall, Lord British has another, greater hit on his hands, a faster, drastically more advanced game that really is the ultimate in real-time D&D type fantasy games for the micro."[^ref-16]

Computer Gaming World's James A. McPherson reviewed the Apple II version in its March–April 1983 issue, finding the world "vastly improved over ULTIMA I", though he noted that "very little of this enormous work was really utilized as being required to complete the game."[^ref-17] K-Power's David Langendoen rated it 7 on the magazine's 1–10 scale in February 1984, writing that it "is more sophisticated and has a quicker pace" than Ultima I and that it "is unique and its storyline is original."[^ref-19] Wikipedia reports that Phil Wiswell's review in PC Magazine (August 21, 1984) scored the game 15.5 out of 18; the scan could not be checked for this page.[^ref-1]

Troy Christensen of Different Worlds magazine (issue 41) proclaimed that "Ultima II: The Revenge of the Enchantress is a classic fantasy computer adventure game that is a must for anyone serious in computer gaming."[^ref-18] Softline's report on the 1983 Origins awards lists Ultima II among the nominees for "Best Adventure Game for Home Computer", which went to Sir-tech's Wizardry: Knight of Diamonds.[^ref-21]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been more mixed, with Ultima II widely considered the weakest entry in the series.[^ref-4][^ref-9] MobyGames user Salim Farhat titled his review "More like Time Bandit fanfiction than Ultima," writing that "the game wasn't so much Ultima as it was Time Bandits with a twist."[^ref-2] Daniel Saner wrote that "at most times, Ultima II more resembles work than play."[^ref-2]

The GOG.com release of the Ultima 1+2+3 compilation holds a 4 out of 5 user rating based on 55 reviews,[^ref-4] and several reviewers single out Ultima II as the collection's weak link. One reviewer wrote that "Ultima 2's 4-color cyan and magenta are particularly hard to look at compared to alternatives."[^ref-4]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 7.5/10 Moby Score; critics 71% (6 ratings)[^ref-2]
- **GOG.com:** 4/5 (55 reviews, for trilogy compilation)[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

The development of Ultima II began while Richard Garriott was attending the University of Texas.[^ref-11] After California Pacific, publisher of Ultima I, went out of business in 1981, Garriott told the publishers courting him that he wanted a box, extensive documentation and, most of all, a cloth map; in his words, "every publisher but Sierra On-Line dropped out."[^ref-11][^ref-3]

Garriott was heavily influenced by the 1981 film Time Bandits: he organized repeated trips to $1.00 matinees to copy down the film's time map, and said that "Ultima II was directly inspired by Time Bandits", from the time doors to the cloth map showing their connections.[^ref-11][^ref-2] This inspiration manifested in the game's time-travel mechanics and the design of its time doors.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Production

Development took almost two years, longer than it should have taken because Garriott was attending college at the same time.[^ref-11] Before starting, Garriott went through an assembly-language "boot camp" of about a month with Tom Luhrs, author of Apple-oids, who "held his hand" through machine language; Garriott recalled that "Literally the first lines of machine language I wrote were the first lines I wrote for the game."[^ref-22][^ref-11] This was the first Ultima game Garriott wrote completely in assembly language rather than BASIC.[^ref-1]

Garriott made Ultima II almost single-handedly. He had written his first games in BASIC on his high school's teletype and Akalabeth on his own Apple at home, before tackling machine language for this game.[^ref-11] Chuck Bueche, later a co-founder of Origin, did the Atari 800 version for Sierra.[^ref-11]

### Technical Achievements

Ultima II was notable for being one of the first graphical games ported to the Atari ST platform.[^ref-3] GOG's store description, speaking of Origin's first three Ultimas, says "Origin Systems was infamous for pushing computer systems to the limit."[^ref-4]

The IBM PC version was specifically designed to be played on an IBM CGA system with a composite color monitor, which produced different color output than the standard RGB display.[^ref-2] The game was programmed entirely in assembly language, allowing for improved performance compared to interpreted BASIC programs.[^ref-1]

### Cut Content

While no specific cut content has been documented, the game as released contained many areas that were "simply empty" and some planets that had "literally nothing on them."[^ref-3] This suggests that time or resource constraints may have prevented the full realization of Garriott's vision for the game's scope.

### Technical Issues

The IBM PC version of Ultima II is notorious for being prone to divide by zero errors and crashing.[^ref-1] The game runs too fast on modern computers since it was designed for the original IBM PC's 4.77 MHz CPU clock speed, making it unplayable without slowdown measures.[^ref-1][^ref-5]

A known issue affects the later CD-ROM re-releases: when files from all the game's disks were copied to one folder, several files with identical file names were overwritten, removing the maps of most planets other than Earth.[^ref-2][^ref-1] A MobyGames reviewer says this made the game unwinnable without a fan patch, but Wikipedia notes that the map for "Planet X" is intact and the game can still be won; fan patches restore the missing maps.[^ref-2][^ref-1]

Additional documented bugs include:[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-5]
- Bug preventing Strength stat increase after character creation (original Apple II version)[^ref-3]
- HP counter rollover that could instantly kill the character[^ref-3]
- Blank disk error when creating new character in GOG version (fixed in 2025)[^ref-4]
- PLAYER save cannot be overwritten in-game[^ref-5]
- Game crashes when saving with the GOG version and the fan upgrade patch unless a PLAYER.U2 directory is created[^ref-5]
- Planetary maps appearing as Earth maps in Ultima I-VI Series compilations[^ref-3]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Enilno Sword:** The weapon needed to defeat the villain is a sword called Enilno. Spelled backward, it becomes "Online"—a reference to Sierra On-Line, the game's publisher.[^ref-2]
- **Earth Coordinates:** In the map of the solar system, Earth is located at coordinates (6,6,6).[^ref-2][^ref-14]
- **Time Bandits Inspiration:** The time doors and cloth map were directly inspired by the 1981 film Time Bandits, with Garriott returning to the theater repeatedly to copy down the film's map.[^ref-11][^ref-2]
- **Cloth Map First:** This was the first game in the Ultima series to include a cloth map, a feature that became a series trademark.[^ref-1][^ref-11][^ref-13]
- **Cover Art:** Ultima II is the only installment in the series not to feature a cover painting by Denis Loubet. The dragon in the title screen was taken from the Ultima I cover.[^ref-3]
- **Pop Culture References:** The game contains numerous pop-culture references throughout.[^ref-3]
- **Celebrity Influence:** South Park co-creator Trey Parker has cited Ultima II as an early RPG influence on his work.[^ref-3]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Ultima II sold over 50,000 copies, earning a Certified Silver award from the Software Publishers Association, and nearly 100,000 copies by 1990.[^ref-11] The trigger for Origin Systems was a royalty dispute: Garriott's Sierra contract assigned all publishing rights for Ultima II to Sierra but did not set a royalty rate for an IBM version, and he accepted Sierra's "take it or leave it" terms. When the same friction carried into negotiations for Ultima III, the Garriotts founded Origin in 1983.[^ref-11][^ref-1]

The relationship between Garriott and Sierra On-Line deteriorated after the game's release, leading to difficulties when Origin Systems wanted to re-release the title.[^ref-3][^ref-12] Sierra refused to sell the rights to Origin for many years and did not relinquish the original cover artwork until 1998 at the latest.[^ref-3] There are two DOS ports of Revenge of the Enchantress; the second, released on the various Ultima collections, removes all mention of Sierra On-Line from the title.[^ref-12]

### Collections

Ultima II has been included in several compilation releases:

- **Ultima Trilogy I-II-III** (1989) – Included an enhanced version with audiovisual upgrades, exclusively available in this box set[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- **Ultima 1+2+3** – GOG.com digital release (2011-present)[^ref-4]

The 1989 enhanced version for Apple II is extremely rare due to its exclusive anthology availability.[^ref-3]

### Fan Projects

Because the re-releases lost most of the planetary map files, fans produced patches that restore them and fix other problems, including an upgrade patch that adds EGA graphics and a frame limiter to the DOS version.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Related Publications

- **Cloth Map:** Included with original game release, inspired by Time Bandits film[^ref-11][^ref-13]
- **Game Manual:** Standard documentation included with retail release[^ref-13]
- **The Official Book of Ultima:** Shay Addams's 1990 history and guide to the Ultima series, with a chapter on Ultima II[^ref-11]

### Critical Perspective

Ultima II occupies a curious position in RPG history as both a significant technical achievement and a somewhat divisive gameplay experience. The game represents the Ultima series at a transitional moment—ambitious in scope but rough in execution. Its time-travel premise across Earth's history rather than the fantasy land of Sosaria distinguishes it from other entries in the series, making it feel less like a true Ultima game to many players.[^ref-2]

The game's historical significance lies in several areas: it demonstrated that RPGs could feature complex temporal mechanics and multi-era storytelling; it established the cloth map as a series trademark; and the dispute over its IBM port led Richard Garriott to establish Origin Systems, which would go on to create some of the most influential RPGs ever made.[^ref-1][^ref-11] However, its reputation as the weakest entry in the series has persisted,[^ref-4][^ref-9] with the extremely grindy gameplay and numerous bugs overshadowing its innovative elements. The game is perhaps best understood as an essential stepping stone—both technically and commercially—that made the greater achievements of later Ultima titles possible.
