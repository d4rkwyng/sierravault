# Ultima: Escape from Mt. Drash

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Ultima: Escape from Mt. Drash* is an extremely rare dungeon-maze game released in 1983 exclusively for the Commodore VIC-20[^ref-1][^ref-2]. It was written by Keith Zabalaoui, a high-school friend of Richard Garriott's, and published by Sierra On-Line, which had published *Ultima II*[^ref-1][^ref-6]. Sierra suggested selling it under the Ultima name to help its chances, and Garriott agreed as a favour to his friend[^ref-6][^ref-16]. Sierra's only known advertisement for the game, in the July 1983 issue of *Compute!*, showed it under the SierraVenture label as "A real-time, fantastic adventure"[^ref-17][^ref-6].

For years a collectors' legend held that Sierra had stuck the Ultima name on the game without Garriott's knowledge or consent. Garriott contradicted that in 2012 when Ultima fan Pix asked him about it, and Jimmy Maher's 2013 history of the game laid the legend to rest[^ref-16][^ref-6]. The story is still part of how the game is remembered, and some fan sites repeat it[^ref-7][^ref-14].

The game is remembered for its rarity rather than its gameplay, which modern players have generally panned[^ref-2][^ref-11][^ref-12]. Maher wrote that among Ultima collectors it "has for years been rivaled only by the original hand-assembled Akalabeth as the Holy Grail for these folks"[^ref-6]. Only a few thousand copies were made, often estimated at about 3,000[^ref-1][^ref-6], and complete copies have sold for thousands of dollars on eBay[^ref-19][^ref-11]. Beyond its name, the game has very little connection to the rest of the series[^ref-2][^ref-3].

## Story Summary

The player has been imprisoned within Mt. Drash by creatures called "garrintrots" and must escape the dungeons[^ref-1][^ref-3]. The manual describes the mountain as "honeycombed with old mining tunnels" that the Garrintrots "now use … as gaming arenas where you are the main attraction"[^ref-12]. Mt. Drash takes its name from a dungeon in *Ultima I*[^ref-2][^ref-1].

Wikipedia calls the name "garrintrots" a pun on Garriott's surname, and the CRPG Addict calls it "an obvious and weird play on 'Garriott'"; the Ultima Codex is more cautious and says the derivation is "uncertain"[^ref-1][^ref-11][^ref-3]. Beyond these names, the game shares virtually no story or setting with the mainline Ultima series[^ref-2][^ref-11].

The box shows a castle on a mountain peak[^ref-17]. Sources agree it is reused Ultima-related artwork but disagree on where it came from. The Ultima Codex says it is a detail from the lesser-known half of the *Ultima II* box art[^ref-3]. The Computer Game Museum points to the art of Sierra's Atari release of *Ultima I*[^ref-14]. The CRPG Addict says it was taken from an ad for Sierra's re-release of *Ultima*[^ref-11].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

*Ultima: Escape from Mt. Drash* is played entirely from the keyboard[^ref-2]. The screen has three views at once: a first-person 3D maze, an overhead map, and a side-on combat view[^ref-18][^ref-12]. Play is real-time, with a timer on each level[^ref-17][^ref-1][^ref-12].

The RUN/STOP and RESTORE keys are disabled during play. The Ultima Codex calls this an unusual form of copy protection, meant to stop players from breaking into the game's BASIC code[^ref-3].

### Structure and Progression

The game has 15 randomly generated maze levels. Each starts in the bottom-right corner and exits in the upper-left, and the player has 99 seconds per level[^ref-11][^ref-12][^ref-18]. The game removes the player's aids as the levels go on[^ref-11][^ref-12]:

- **Level 5 onward:** gems must be found before the exit will open
- **Level 7 onward:** the corridors no longer appear on the overhead map
- **Level 9 onward:** monsters no longer appear on the map
- **Level 11:** the compass and the player's position on the map disappear
- **Level 15:** a final winding corridor of fights, with no gems or maze[^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The player starts with three lives and has three spells: Blast (breaks a wall), Sleep and Teleport[^ref-11][^ref-15]. Combat takes place in a side view and depends on timing a thrust or counterthrust as the monster approaches[^ref-11][^ref-13]. The game draws its scenes with the VIC-20's built-in graphical characters rather than custom graphics. Its background music uses the sound capabilities of the MOS Technology VIC chip[^ref-1].

MobyGames' only user review calls the 3D view "nearly useless since it doesn't show monsters or gems," and notes that "you don't even know if you have picked up a gem or not"[^ref-2].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The game got little press. *Computer Gaming World*'s July–August 1983 issue (Vol. 3 No. 4, p. 9) included it in a round-up of new products, listed under Sierra On-Line. That entry was a short descriptive blurb with no score. It called the game "an intriguing adventure game because of its unique graphics and marvelous musical score (The Well-Tempered Commodore?)", and it noted the 15 levels, the escalating difficulty and the need for an 8K expander[^ref-18]. The CRPG Addict questions whether the magazine's writers were "doing anything other than parroting the press materials"[^ref-11].

Sierra's only known advertisement for the game was a VIC-20 page in the July 1983 *Compute!*, which promoted it alongside *Creepy Corridors* and *Flip-N-Match*[^ref-17][^ref-6].

The game was listed in the Sierra On-Line Consumer Price List for the First Quarter of 1984 at a retail price of $19.95, with stock number 40245202[^ref-1].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been harsh. Hardcore Gaming 101 called it "absolutely, horribly unplayable"[^ref-12]. The CRPG Addict wrote that the gameplay "could have been exciting as an action game, but the lack of any other features makes it fall flat"[^ref-11]. As of October 2026, MobyGames players rate it 2.3 out of 5, based on 8 ratings and 1 review[^ref-2].

That one review, written by Pix in 2008, says: "This is one of the single worst games I've ever played," and concludes, "There is a reason this title is so rare though - no one wanted to buy it in the first place"[^ref-2]. The review also repeats the legend that the Ultima name was used "without Lord British's knowledge". Pix himself corrected this in 2012, after Garriott told him he had approved it[^ref-2][^ref-16].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 2.3/5 (8 ratings, 1 review, as of October 2026)[^ref-2]

## Development

### Origins

Keith Zabalaoui was one of Garriott's high-school friends from Houston and had helped with Garriott's earlier projects[^ref-6][^ref-11]. According to Maher, he visited Sierra in Garriott's company and was inspired to start a simple maze game. When he showed Sierra the finished product, the company was doubtful[^ref-6]. Sierra was then based in Coarsegold, California[^ref-17][^ref-18].

The VIC-20 software market had already passed its peak by early 1983. The game also needed a memory expansion and a cassette drive, which many VIC-20 owners didn't have[^ref-6][^ref-1]. Sierra suggested that marketing the game as an Ultima might help. Garriott agreed, according to Maher "likely more as a personal favor" to Zabalaoui than anything else[^ref-6]. Pix reported Garriott's own account in 2012: "Garriott was well aware of Escape From Mt. Drash and agreed to the Ultima branding to help out the author as Sierra wouldn't have published the game otherwise"[^ref-16]. The cassette and title screen credit Garriott as owner of the Ultima trademark[^ref-13].

Even with the Ultima name, Sierra remained skeptical. It manufactured only the minimum quantity required by its contract with Zabalaoui, "on the order of a few thousand units", and placed a single advertisement[^ref-6]. Approximately 3,000 units is the figure usually given, though exact numbers are not known[^ref-1][^ref-3].

### The "Unauthorized Ultima" Legend

Because the game was so obscure, a story grew up around it. In that story Sierra had used the Ultima name without Garriott's knowledge or consent and then buried the game, in some versions literally[^ref-6]. Maher traced the tale to Ultima collectors in the late 1990s, when no working copy was known. It was first told with qualifiers like "apparently", and over time those qualifiers dropped away and it "became accepted fan canon"[^ref-6]. Sierra's John Williams had occasionally tried to correct it in comments on blogs that repeated it[^ref-6].

The legend is false. Garriott confirmed to Pix in 2012 that he had known about the game and approved the branding[^ref-16]. Maher's 2013 article reported that Garriott had "known about the game and duly authorized its release"[^ref-6]. The Ultima Codex, Giant Bomb, Hardcore Gaming 101 and the CRPG Addict now all say Garriott consented[^ref-3][^ref-9][^ref-12][^ref-11]. Some sources still repeat the old story, including the Ultima Fandom wiki and the Computer Game Museum's display page[^ref-7][^ref-14].

### Production

The game was written in Commodore BASIC, with assembly language routines for faster graphics animation and for the background music[^ref-1]. It was sold on cassette rather than cartridge, and it required an 8K or 16K RAM expansion to run[^ref-1][^ref-18]. Nerdly Pleasures says Zabalaoui approached Sierra with a game he had written in BASIC that also contains "some machine code in binary"[^ref-13].

**Development Credits:**[^ref-2][^ref-1]
- **Designer/Programmer:** Keith Zabalaoui
- **Publisher:** Sierra On-Line, Inc.

### Technical Achievements

The screen splits into first-person, overhead-map and combat views at the same time, an unusual layout for the limited VIC-20[^ref-18][^ref-12]. *Computer Gaming World* singled out its "unique graphics and marvelous musical score"[^ref-18]. The CRPG Addict identifies its looping music as excerpts from classical pieces, among them Saint-Saëns's "Danse Macabre", a Bach "March in D Major" and, during combat, Schumann's "Knecht Ruprecht"[^ref-11].

### Technical Issues

The first-person 3D view shows neither monsters nor gems, and the game gives no visible feedback when a gem is collected[^ref-2][^ref-11]. Because RUN/STOP and RESTORE are disabled, players cannot break out of the program during play[^ref-3].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- Mt. Drash shares its name with a dungeon in *Ultima I*; the CRPG Addict notes the Ultima dungeon is technically the "Mines of Mt. Drash"[^ref-2][^ref-11]
- The name "garrintrots" is widely read as a play on Garriott's name[^ref-1][^ref-11]
- One rumor claimed that Sierra buried its remaining stock at the foot of a mountain, echoing the Atari video game burial[^ref-1][^ref-6]
- The Computer Game Museum's copy, which it calls the first "re-discovered" complete copy, was found at the bottom of a cliff in British Columbia, where unsold software had been dumped[^ref-14][^ref-1]
- Retellings commonly add that Sierra for years denied the game had ever existed, until Zabalaoui confirmed it had been finished and shipped[^ref-1][^ref-11]

## Voice Cast

*Ultima: Escape from Mt. Drash* has no voice acting, as was standard for games of this era and platform.

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sales were very poor[^ref-6]. Maher writes that most of the small production run was likely written off and thrown away, whether by Sierra or by its distributors[^ref-6].

The first working copy surfaced online only in 2000. It came from an Indiana teenager whose parents had bought it at a garage sale several years earlier[^ref-6]. The first complete rediscovered copy sold on eBay in March 2004 for $3,605 to collector Peter Olafson. When Olafson resold it in 2014, it went for $3,050[^ref-19][^ref-1]. Counterfeit copies have also appeared on the market[^ref-1][^ref-3].

### Collections

*Ultima: Escape from Mt. Drash* has never been included in any official compilation or re-release[^ref-2][^ref-3].

### Fan Projects

Kasper Fauerby's 2003 Windows port, released under the GPL, is the version most people have played[^ref-15][^ref-12][^ref-3]. In January 2025, Aleksi Eeben released a Commodore 64 conversion[^ref-8]. Tape images of the original VIC-20 cassette also circulate for use with emulators such as VICE[^ref-3][^ref-13].

### Related Publications

- **Compute!, July 1983 (p. 79):** a VIC-20 advertisement, the game's only known ad: "ULTIMA · Escape from Mt. Drash. A real-time, fantastic adventure."[^ref-17]
- **Computer Gaming World, Vol. 3 No. 4 (July–August 1983), p. 9:** a new-product blurb[^ref-18]
- **Sierra On-Line Consumer Price List (Q1 1984):** lists the game at $19.95, stock number 40245202[^ref-1]

No official hint books, strategy guides or novelizations have been documented.

### Critical Perspective

The game's real significance is historiographical. Maher used it as a lesson for anyone writing gaming history: "be a bit more rigorous, and not to substitute easy assumptions for research"[^ref-6]. The legend, a "glib story" in Maher's words, was repeated for years without being seriously questioned. It also cast Sierra as a company that had stolen a trademark, a charge Maher calls hard to believe given Sierra's legal team and Ken Williams's own work securing the Ultima rights[^ref-6].

In Maher's view, the actual decision to attach the Ultima name to a slight VIC-20 game reflects how young the industry and Garriott both were in 1983. "Today," he writes, "one in Garriott's position would never risk tarnishing his trademark in such a way"[^ref-6]. As the MobyGames reviewer put it, "Without the Ultima brand on it, this game would be justly long forgotten"[^ref-2].
