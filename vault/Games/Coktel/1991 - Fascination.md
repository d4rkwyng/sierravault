# Fascination

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Fascination* is an erotic thriller graphic adventure game developed by Coktel Vision and published under Tomahawk, the label Coktel used for its simulation and erotic games, in 1991 for the Amiga, Atari ST, and MS-DOS platforms.[^ref-10][^ref-24][^ref-32] The game places players in the role of Doralice May, described in the game's manual as "the sexiest captain on the Paris-Miami flight,"[^ref-21][^ref-29] who becomes entangled in a dangerous conspiracy involving a revolutionary chemical substance after a dying passenger entrusts her with a mysterious vial.[^ref-2] As a first-person point-and-click adventure, *Fascination* was the third and final entry in Coktel Vision's short-lived line of mature-themed adventure games that began with *Emmanuelle* (1989) and continued with *Geisha* (1990).[^ref-10][^ref-18][^ref-6]

The game was designed by [[Muriel Tramis]], a Martinique-born designer whose Coktel Vision games frequently featured female protagonists and mildly erotic scenes.[^ref-4] Despite its adult themes, *Fascination* was noted by *The Adventurers' Guild* for having "sensible problems with sensible solutions, not based on minigames or external sources of information, but on the logical use of items you'll find."[^ref-5] The protagonist Doralice would later return in Coktel Vision's *Lost in Time* (1993), establishing a loose continuity between the games.[^ref-6]

Contemporary reception was mixed, with French publications rating the PC version highly (90-91%) while Amiga reviews ranged more widely from 47% to 80%.[^ref-7] The *I Heart Old Games* blog described it as "a less grotesque version of Leisure Suit Larry but with a more pronounced sensuality,"[^ref-8] while a Lemon Amiga user comment praised its "clever mix between eroticism and thriller."[^ref-9]

## Story Summary

The narrative opens with Jeffrey Miller, CEO of Quantum Unlimited Laboratory (Q.U.L.), who has discovered a revolutionary cerebro-chemical substance capable of neutralizing behavioral inhibitors—essentially a drug that increases the sex drive.[^ref-11][^ref-24] Miller's right-hand man,[^ref-6] Fayard Nichols, is transporting a prototype vial of this substance when he suffers a fatal heart attack aboard a Paris-to-Miami flight. In his dying moments, Nichols places the vial into the hands of Captain Doralice May, the flight's commanding officer, imploring her to deliver it into safe hands.[^ref-2]

Upon landing in Miami, Doralice finds herself thrust into a dangerous conspiracy. The chemical's potential has attracted numerous interested parties, and Miller himself has been assassinated.[^ref-13] Doralice must navigate Miami's various locales—including a lingerie shop in Coconut Grove, a jazz club in Little Havana, and the historic Villa Vizcaya—to uncover the truth.[^ref-6] A recorded message from Miller tasks her with recovering a stolen vial and delivering both samples to his son Kenneth.[^ref-14] The French Wikipedia summarises her task as often having to lie, use her charms and above all her wits to carry out her investigation and solve the mystery.[^ref-6]

Doralice must also avoid being made out as the murderer.[^ref-24] Her fellow pilot and boyfriend Rob, having consumed the drug while she was away, is driven by the drug to commit a series of rapes, which Doralice follows through the newspapers she collects.[^ref-1][^ref-14][^ref-29] The cast includes Inspector Pedro di Helgos, a wheelchair-using policeman who is eventually revealed to be the villain Doc in disguise.[^ref-1][^ref-14] In the ending, Doralice frees the real Kenneth and finds the whole cast waiting for her: Rob explains that the adventure was a trial run of a role-playing game business he has set up.[^ref-1] PC Gamer's Richard Cobbett called it "perhaps the stupidest ending of any game in history,"[^ref-29] while Polish scholar Filip Jankowski (*Games and Culture*, 2020) disputed Cobbett's view and argued that the ending anticipated the finale of David Fincher's film *The Game* (1997)[^ref-1], a comparison Abandonware France also drew.[^ref-3]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

*Fascination* employs a first-person perspective with a point-and-click interface, typical of adventure games from this era.[^ref-1] Players interact with the environment using a mouse-driven interface, clicking on objects and characters to examine or manipulate them.[^ref-16] Items discovered during exploration can be placed in an inventory for later use, and the plot advances through dialogue with characters and puzzle-solving.[^ref-17] The game can be saved at any time using the F1 key.[^ref-3]

### Structure and Progression

The game follows a linear progression through various Miami locations as Doralice investigates the conspiracy. The structure requires players to gather information, collect items, and solve puzzles to advance.[^ref-17] Notably, the game does not allow backtracking—if players miss an important item in one location, they cannot return for it and must restore a previous save.[^ref-18]

- **Airport/Airplane:** The opening sequence where Doralice receives the vial from the dying Nichols
- **Pelican Hotel:** Doralice's base of operations in Miami[^ref-14]
- **Coconut Grove:** Features a lingerie shop important to the investigation[^ref-6]
- **Little Havana:** Contains a jazz club with vital clues[^ref-6]
- **Quantum Unlimited Laboratory:** Miller's research facility[^ref-14]
- **Villa Mollière/Villa Vizcaya:** Climactic location for the game's finale[^ref-6]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzles in *Fascination* were praised for their logical nature, relying on sensible application of inventory items rather than minigames or external knowledge requirements.[^ref-5] However, the game does feature several design challenges that frustrated players:

- **Pixel hunting:** Some items are difficult to locate on screen without careful searching[^ref-18]
- **Random elements:** Phone numbers and access codes change with each playthrough, requiring players to find them in-game rather than using guides directly[^ref-19]
- **Death traps:** Players can die unexpectedly through dialogue choices or timing mistakes, such as ringing a studio doorbell three times resulting in game over[^ref-20]
- **Newspaper collection:** Six newspapers are scattered throughout the game, and players must collect at least five to achieve victory[^ref-20]

One notable puzzle involves an aquarium containing piranhas—players who attempt to retrieve an item without following the correct procedure will be bitten.[^ref-20]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

French gaming publications gave *Fascination* high marks upon its initial release on PC. *Génération 4* (Issue 38) awarded the game 91%, with reviewer Christian Roux praising the title in November 1991.[^ref-7] Similarly, *Joystick* (Issue 21) gave the PC version 90%, reviewed by Moulinex in the same month.[^ref-7]

The Amiga version received more varied scores across European publications. *CU Amiga* gave the game 80%, with reviewer Tony Dillon noting its strengths in September 1992.[^ref-7] *Amiga Games* awarded 79% from Lutz Mahle in December 1992, while *Amiga Mania* gave 78% from Jeff Davy in November 1992.[^ref-7] Lower scores came from *Amiga Power* at 67% (Jonathan Davies, September 1992), *Amiga Format* at 65% (James Leach, October 1992), and *The One Amiga* at 62% (Khalid Howlader, September 1992).[^ref-7]

The most critical Amiga reviews came from *Amiga Action* (51%, Peter Lee), *Amiga Joker* (48%, Oskar Dzierzynski), and *Amiga Computing* (47%, Ben Styles).[^ref-7] Ben Styles of *Amiga Computing* wrote that "The French just aren't funny any more."[^ref-21]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been mixed. *Hardcore Gaming 101* observed that "like most purportedly 'adult' games, *Fascination* is largely fixated on cheap thrills and ridiculously juvenile humor."[^ref-2] The *Adventurers' Guild* blog gave the game a score of 42, noting that its sexuality was "mostly just there to spice things up... it's all very much like a light version of Leisure Suit Larry." Reviewer Ilmari scored its Environment and Atmosphere just 2 out of 10, objecting to the plot's attempt to draw humour from rape, and deducted a further point overall for "the careless use of rape in the plot."[^ref-5]

The *Atari Legend* retrospective was more nostalgic, with the reviewer stating "I seriously don't know what to do with this one. To me *Fascination* is a classic, mostly because of nostalgic reasons."[^ref-23] The site awarded scores of 9 for graphics, 8 for sound, 6 for gameplay, and 7 overall.[^ref-23]

**Aggregate Scores:**

- **Abandonware DOS:** 3.88/5.00 (120 votes)[^ref-25]
- **MyAbandonware:** 4.4/5 (86 votes)[^ref-18]

## Development

### Origins

*Fascination* emerged from Coktel Vision's experimentation with mature-themed adventure games in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The company, known mainly for educational titles, published its simulation and erotic games under the Tomahawk label to keep them apart from its edutainment line.[^ref-32] It had previously released *Emmanuelle* (1989) and *Geisha* (1990) before developing *Fascination* as the third and final entry in this unofficial trilogy.[^ref-10][^ref-18] The game was designed by Muriel Tramis, whose work consistently featured female protagonists and explored themes of sensuality within adventure game frameworks.[^ref-4]

### Production

MobyGames, ScummVM, PCGamingWiki and StrategyWiki credit Coktel Vision (with programming by MDO) as developer and Tomahawk as publisher.[^ref-24][^ref-10][^ref-28][^ref-15] The English Wikipedia reverses the two roles,[^ref-1] but Tomahawk was not a separate studio: it was the label Coktel Vision used to publish its simulation and erotic games.[^ref-32] The game was built using the Gob engine, which would later power other Coktel Vision titles including the popular *Gobliiins* series.[^ref-10] The team included concept designer Muriel Tramis and manager Philippe Lamarque, with graphics created by Joseph Kluytmans, Rasheed (Rachid Chebli), and Yannick Chosse.[^ref-9][^ref-24]

According to composer Frédéric Motte, the programmers could not play ".MOD" music files on the PC at the time, so they sampled the Amiga music and played short loops of it.[^ref-35] Separately, the DOS version supports the Intersound MDO, a sound device distributed by Coktel Vision itself.[^ref-35]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-9][^ref-24]
- **Concept Designer:** Muriel Tramis
- **Manager:** Philippe Lamarque
- **Graphics:** Joseph Kluytmans, Rasheed (Rachid Chebli), Yannick Chosse
- **Music:** Frédéric Motte (floppy release; also sound effects); Bibapelula Productions / Alain Sery (credited for music on the CD-ROM version)[^ref-24]
- **Voice Actors (CD version):** Phil Mac Alpine, Marina Albert, Catherine Lypszyc

### Technical Achievements

The game's visual presentation was notable for its creative approach to imagery. The French Wikipedia article notes that the images do not always fill the screen, being cut into silhouette shapes such as a woman's face, an outstretched body or a dripping dagger.[^ref-6] The Atari ST version utilized 16 colors effectively, earning praise for its graphics in reviews.[^ref-23]

The music is usually credited to Frédéric Motte (credited as "Moby" in some versions), who later described his Coktel Vision tunes as his "first gigs in the video game industry"; his Bandcamp collection of them includes a "Fascination" track.[^ref-27] The composer, who came from the Amiga demo scene, was not the famous musician Moby but shared the nickname.[^ref-23] The credit is not consistent across sources: the English Wikipedia, Lemon Amiga and Atari Legend credit Motte,[^ref-1][^ref-9][^ref-22] MobyGames credits Motte for the floppy release but Bibapelula Productions / Alain Sery for the CD-ROM version's music (with Motte on sound effects),[^ref-24] and the French Wikipedia credits Alain Sery alone.[^ref-6]

### Cut Content

The CD-ROM version released in 1992 added multiple languages and speech, but censored several scenes from the floppy release: the hotel shower scene was left out, the underwear photos in the lobby magazine were replaced by car pictures, Doralice's rear view in the lingerie-shop mirror was left out, and nudity on the storeroom pin-up posters and Kenneth's buttocks was covered.[^ref-35][^ref-29]

**Gob Engine Notes:**[^ref-10]
The game uses the Gob engine, which was later employed for the *Gobliiins* series and other Coktel Vision titles. The CD version stores STK files hidden on the disc, and the game is supported by ScummVM for modern playback.[^ref-10]

### Technical Issues

Several design decisions created frustration for players:

- **No backtracking:** Missing items in previous areas requires restoring saves[^ref-18]
- **Pixel hunting:** Small interactive objects can be easily missed[^ref-18]
- **Random codes:** Phone numbers and access codes change each playthrough, preventing direct walkthrough use[^ref-19]
- **Instant deaths:** Certain dialogue options or actions result in immediate game over[^ref-20]
- **Miller's phone number:** Must be dialed correctly to avoid softlocking the game[^ref-20]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The game contains several optional interactions and hidden content:[^ref-20]
- **Bathroom scene:** Players can enter the bathroom to see Doralice nude (optional interaction)
- **Parking lot jokes:** Clicking on cars in the parking lot triggers humorous animations
- **Lingerie shop mirror:** Clicking the mirror shows Doralice trying on different outfits
- **Poster interactions:** Clicking posters in the lingerie backroom produces entertaining responses
- **Microscope secret:** Using the signet ring on a microscope reveals a randomly generated birth date

Additional trivia:
- A MobyGames user review compared the game's aesthetic to "a Bruckheimer movie from the 80's, complete with blond dudes with full tans, gigantic shades, gigantic hair, fast cars and fast women"[^ref-24]
- The *Atari Legend* reviewer opened his discussion of the moody sampled soundtrack with "This game sounds like a porn movie!"[^ref-23]
- Solution guides were published across three issues of *Amiga Power*[^ref-7]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

While specific sales figures are not available, *Fascination* represented the conclusion of Coktel Vision's mature-themed adventure game line.[^ref-18] The game was released across multiple European markets including France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and the United Kingdom, with a United States release following in 1992.[^ref-18] Digital Integration distributed the 1992 US release, which carried the Coktel Vision name,[^ref-33] and was named as the Amiga publisher in the *Amiga Action*, *The One* and *Amiga Computing* reviews.[^ref-7]

### Collections

The protagonist Doralice May returned in *Lost in Time* (1993), creating an informal continuity between the games.[^ref-6] *Fascination* itself remained a standalone title. It appeared in period compilations such as the Dutch Flyer collection but has not been re-released in modern times;[^ref-8] it remains playable through ScummVM.[^ref-10]

### Fan Projects

The game is supported by ScummVM, the open-source adventure game engine, allowing it to be played on modern systems without requiring DOSBox.[^ref-10] Various abandonware sites have preserved both the floppy and CD-ROM versions for historical purposes.[^ref-18]

### Related Publications

- **Walkthrough Guides:** Multiple fan-created walkthroughs exist, including comprehensive guides on GameFAQs (Version 1.3, July 2022)[^ref-19] and StrategyWiki[^ref-14]
- **Magazine Solutions:** *Amiga Power* published a complete solution across three issues (Issues 19, 20, and 21, November 1992 – January 1993)[^ref-7]

### Critical Perspective

*Fascination* is a mature-themed adventure designed by a woman, [[Muriel Tramis]].[^ref-4] Filip Jankowski counts it among the first video games to feature a female protagonist and adopt her viewpoint, and reads its ending, in which the characters turn out to be taking part in a murder party, most of them belonging to the white male establishment responsible for the game world's pornographic content, as Doralice questioning patriarchal norms.[^ref-1]

Reviewers have been less generous about how the game handles sex. *The Adventurers' Guild* found its sexuality "mostly just there to spice things up", with silly puns, sex jokes and "perhaps an occasional corny nude shot", but criticised the use of a drug-induced rape spree as a source of humour, deducting a point for "the careless use of rape in the plot."[^ref-5] The floppy release includes nude scenes, such as the optional shower cutscene, which were cut or covered in the 1992 CD-ROM version.[^ref-20][^ref-35]

The game was released shortly before Coktel Vision was acquired by Sierra On-Line, a deal finalised on 29 October 1993.[^ref-32] Among modern retrospectives, *I Heart Old Games* placed it among adventure games that are neither masterpieces nor awful, "probably in the lowest tier of it but is not an horrible game."[^ref-8]
