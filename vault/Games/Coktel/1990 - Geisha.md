# Geisha

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

Geisha is an erotic adventure game developed by French company Coktel Vision and MDO and published by Tomahawk in 1990 for Amiga, Atari ST and MS-DOS[^ref-9][^ref-1]. Tomahawk was not an independent publisher but a label Coktel Vision used to publish its simulation and erotic games, keeping them apart from its educational line[^ref-29]. The game predates Sierra On-Line's involvement with Coktel: Sierra agreed to acquire the company in 1992, and the deal was finalised on 29 October 1993[^ref-29]. It was designed by Muriel Tramis, whose work explores themes of race, colonialism and sexuality drawn from her Créolité heritage[^ref-4][^ref-3].

The game was part of what PC Gamer's Richard Cobbett called Coktel Vision's "short-lived quest to sex up adventure gaming"[^ref-27]. One modern reviewer introduced it as "another 'erotic adventure' from the makers of Emmanuelle"[^ref-12], and GameFAQs groups Emmanuelle (1989), Geisha (1990) and Fascination (1991) as "a sort-of loose spiritual trilogy"[^ref-4].

Critical reception varied widely, from French magazine Joystick's 84/100[^ref-17] to Amiga Power's 5%[^ref-6], whose reviewer Gary Penn concluded that "Geisha's about as user-friendly and sexy as a rotten anchovy"[^ref-28].

## Story Summary

The game's narrative begins with a desperate search: "Eva was missing.... She had been kidnapped from under my own eyes!"[^ref-8]. A mad scientist has kidnapped the unnamed male protagonist's girlfriend Eva and wants to transform her into "some sort of futuristic geisha (half machine, half human)"[^ref-19]. The villain is known as the "Lustful Dragon"[^ref-10].

The hero must travel to Tokyo, "a city torn between modernism and tradition", to rescue Eva from the Lustful Dragon's clutches[^ref-10]. OldGames' description sets the game "In the phantasmatic depth of an unwoonted and disconcerting Tokyo City, torn between modernism and tradition"[^ref-11].

Along the way, the player meets characters including[^ref-8]:
- **Sensei Isuzu** - who gives the hero a reading about his future
- **Mr. O (The Elegant Locust)** - a self-described "Yakusa chief" whose interests are "in the world of business and politics"

The story is hard to follow. One modern reviewer summarized their confusion: "something something something girl and robot body, something about an escape …. dragons?"[^ref-12]. OldGames describes the game as "a series of puzzle and arcade minigames connected with a story in an indeed adventure-like manner"[^ref-19].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Geisha uses a point-and-click interface with mouse support[^ref-13], and MobyGames classifies it as a first-person adventure[^ref-1].

The interface drew criticism at the time. Amiga Power called it "a dull, dreary, and very limited mouse-driven adventure with a user-interface so poor that it's more embarassing (or offensive even) than the subject matter"[^ref-28].

### Structure and Progression

Rather than a continuous adventure, Geisha is built around a series of puzzle and arcade minigames connected by its story[^ref-19]. MobyGames lists sub-games including "a simple card game, a jigsaw puzzle with a time limit, a short arcade sequence, and others"[^ref-1]. Amiga Power counted five arcade sequences, among them adult-themed variants of Mastermind, a shoot-'em-up, a card game, and Scissors-Paper-Stone[^ref-28].

The game is short: Alex Bevilacqua, citing a YouTube longplay, notes that it "only takes about 30 minutes to complete"[^ref-12].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Bevilacqua found the minigames "boring or stupid" and wrote that he "gave up after going through the maze and sliding piece puzzle like a dozen times"[^ref-12]. PC Gamer called Geisha "the most unfair erotic game ever", noting that one minigame requires winning seven times out of 15[^ref-27].

## Development

### Origins

Geisha was designed by Muriel Tramis at Coktel Vision[^ref-21]. PC Gamer notes that Tramis, credited as "Authoress", switched "from programming missiles to designing games that aimed to be erotic rather than pornographic"[^ref-27]. Her work draws on themes of race, colonialism and sexuality from her Créolité heritage[^ref-21].

The game followed Coktel's earlier erotic adventure Emmanuelle[^ref-12], and was released under the Tomahawk label, which Coktel used to publish simulation and erotic games separately from its educational titles[^ref-29]. In a 2016 retrospective, blogger Ilmari Jauhiainen describes it as "an erotic game from Tomahawk, the subsidiary of Coktel Vision"[^ref-2]. In the UK, Amiga Power listed the publisher as "Tomahawk (Electronic Zoo)" at £25.99[^ref-28].

### Music and Sound

The game's music was composed by Frédéric "Elmobo" Motte, with sound effects by Robin Aziosmanoff[^ref-1]. Motte later described the collection of tunes he made for Coktel Vision at the start of the 1990s as "My first gigs in the video game industry... Oh boy..."[^ref-22].

### Technical Implementation

Geisha runs on the Gob engine, Coktel Vision's proprietary adventure game technology[^ref-7]. The game supports multiple graphics modes to accommodate the varied hardware of the era[^ref-7]:

- CGA (4 colors)
- EGA (16 colors)
- VGA (256 colors)
- Hercules (monochrome)
- Tandy/PCjr

The minimum system requirements included an Intel 286 processor, 512 KB RAM (640 KB for MCGA and Tandy modes), and DOS 2.11[^ref-23]. Sound support included PC Speaker, AdLib, and the Intersound MDO[^ref-24]. The game was distributed on 3.5" floppy disks[^ref-1].

## Legacy

### Coktel's Adult Line and the Sierra Acquisition

Geisha belongs to Coktel Vision's short run of erotic adventures published under Tomahawk[^ref-27][^ref-29]. Sierra On-Line acquired Coktel in a deal finalised on 29 October 1993[^ref-29]. As PC Gamer put it, "it wouldn't be long afterwards that the company was absorbed into the Sierra family, told to put its pants back on, and got a chance to make some actually popular games"[^ref-27].

### Muriel Tramis's Career

Tramis went on to design further games built around Caribbean history and slavery: in Freedom: Rebels in the Darkness (1988) the main character is a rebellious black slave, and in Lost in Time (1993) the heroine discovers an ancestor who was a slave[^ref-3].

### Preservation and Accessibility

The game is considered no longer commercially available[^ref-25]. It remains playable through emulation, with ScummVM providing modern compatibility[^ref-26].
