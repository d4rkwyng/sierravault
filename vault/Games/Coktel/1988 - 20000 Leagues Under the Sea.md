# 20,000 Leagues Under the Sea

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

20,000 Leagues Under the Sea is a first-person adventure game developed and published by [[Coktel Vision]] in 1988, based on Jules Verne's classic 1870 science fiction novel of the same name[^ref-1][^ref-4]. Coktel released it independently, five years before Sierra On-Line's acquisition of Coktel Vision was finalised on 29 October 1993; MobyGames lists Coktel Vision as its publisher, with Bomico and SYSTEM 4 de España as regional distributors[^ref-4][^ref-25]. The player takes the role of Professor Arronax, who finds himself aboard the submarine Nautilus, navigated by Captain Nemo[^ref-2][^ref-4]. Common Sense Media notes that "next to Agatha Christie, Jules Verne is the most translated author of all time"[^ref-3].

The game is played in real time, so progress depends on being in the right location at the right time; this also means the game has waiting periods and can be rendered unwinnable[^ref-4]. Most of the adventure takes place within the Nautilus itself, across three rooms: the living room, the control room, and the library[^ref-4].

The source material, regarded as "one of the premiere adventure novels and one of Verne's greatest works," has inspired numerous adaptations across all media[^ref-5]. Verne's prescient descriptions of the Nautilus "accurately describes features on submarines, which at the time were very primitive vessels," lending the story a scientific credibility that continues to resonate with audiences[^ref-5]. Coktel Vision's 1988 adaptation predates the later educational, hidden object and interactive-novel adaptations described below[^ref-12][^ref-16][^ref-22].

## Story Summary

The game opens on July 5th, 1867, when the government launches the famous ship *Abraham Lincoln* on the trail of a sea monster, with the French professor Arronax taking part in the expedition[^ref-4]. After weeks without any sign of the monster, 300 miles from the shores of Queen Charlotte's Island, three members of the expedition find themselves aboard the Nautilus, a submarine navigated by Captain Nemo[^ref-4][^ref-2][^ref-6]. The player takes Arronax's role to find out more[^ref-4].

Aboard the Nautilus, the player manipulates objects and talks to people to receive hints about what to do next and the state of the submarine, and can influence its route using the map[^ref-4]. Some sequences take place outside the submarine, with puzzles to solve and action sequences such as a fight against a shark[^ref-4]. Throughout, the player must watch Nemo's mood: angering him, for example by manipulating the ship's instruments, gets the player locked in their room while valuable time passes[^ref-4].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

20,000 Leagues Under the Sea is played completely in point and click[^ref-4]. The game presents a first-person perspective as players explore the Nautilus[^ref-1][^ref-4]. MobyGames lists keyboard input for the DOS version, mouse for the Amiga and Atari ST versions, and keyboard or digital joystick for the Amstrad CPC version[^ref-4].

### Structure and Progression

The game's structure is defined by its real-time mechanics, meaning being in the correct location at the precise moment is essential for success[^ref-4].

Most of the adventure takes place aboard the Nautilus, which consists of three rooms[^ref-4]:

- **The Living Room**
- **The Control Room:** manipulating the ship's instruments angers Nemo
- **The Library**

In these rooms the player can manipulate objects and talk to people to receive hints[^ref-4].

The real-time nature means the game features mandatory waiting periods, and there are various possibilities to render the game completely unwinnable[^ref-4]. If players anger Nemo—for example, by manipulating the ship's instruments—he locks them in their room while valuable time passes[^ref-4].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Puzzles inside and outside the submarine yield hints or lead to action sequences, and the player must also watch Nemo's mood[^ref-4].

A critical gameplay element is the complete absence of a save function, meaning players must complete the entire adventure in a single session or restart from the beginning[^ref-4].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

MobyGames records eight critic ratings for the game, averaging 54%[^ref-4]. It lists 1988 releases in the United Kingdom (DOS), France and Germany, with Bomico distributing in Germany, and a 1989 Spanish DOS release distributed by SYSTEM 4 de España[^ref-4]. The game was sold under regional titles including "20000 Lieues sous les Mers" (French), "20000 Meilen unter dem Meer" (German) and "20000 Leguas de Viaje Submarino" (Spanish)[^ref-4][^ref-2].

### Modern Assessment

On MobyGames, the critics average is 54% (8 ratings) and the players average is 2.4 out of 5 (18 ratings)[^ref-4]. MobyGames classifies the game as an Adventure with 1st-person perspective, puzzle elements, a steampunk setting and a licensed property[^ref-4].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 54% critics (8 ratings); 2.4/5 players (18 ratings)[^ref-4]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sales figures for the 1988 release have not been documented in available sources. MobyGames lists releases on four platforms, in the United Kingdom, France, Germany and Spain[^ref-4].

### Subsequent Adaptations

The Jules Verne source material has inspired numerous video game adaptations beyond the 1988 Coktel Vision release:

**1995 - Educational Adaptation:**
Orange Cherry Software developed an educational title published by SoftKey International for Windows 16-bit systems[^ref-12]. This version was "a digitized version of the novel of the same name by Jules Verne" that added "multimedia features such as narrated text, word definitions and interactive fishing which leads to an encyclopedia entry"[^ref-12]. The game portion consisted of multiple-choice quizzes testing knowledge of each chapter[^ref-12].

**1997 - Crayola 3D Coloring:**
IBM and Crayola released an educational coloring program based on the novel for Windows 3.1, Windows 95, and later systems[^ref-13]. The software allowed children to "color 20 images and watch them come to life as 3D pictures" using "63 Crayola crayon colors and 18 fun textures"[^ref-14]. The program could print scenes "plus a title page in color or black & white to create your own storybook"[^ref-14].

**Technical Requirements (1997 Version):**[^ref-14]
- **Minimum CPU:** 486DX 66MHz (Pentium 75MHz recommended)
- **RAM:** 8MB minimum (16MB recommended)
- **Resolution:** 640x480 SVGA, 256 colors minimum
- **Storage:** 10MB hard disk space
- **Media:** CD-ROM (2x drive or better)

**2002 - The Secret of the Nautilus:**
Cryo Interactive developed a more ambitious adventure game adaptation, released in Europe and by DreamCatcher Interactive/The Adventure Company in North America[^ref-15]. The game sold approximately 117,000 copies globally by 2004[^ref-15]. Reviews were mixed, with critics noting "small hotspots, and timed sections let the game down" according to Four Fat Chicks, while Süddeutsche Zeitung observed that "while the game would attract fans of Jules Verne, they would become dismayed by the game's combination puzzles"[^ref-15]. The game's characters were "based on original 19th century engravings from Jules Verne's novel" despite being set in the 21st century[^ref-15].

**Review Scores (The Secret of the Nautilus):**[^ref-15]
- **Metzomagic:** 3/5
- **Spazio Games:** 7.5/10
- **Bonusweb.cz:** 67%
- **4Players:** 62%
- **GameOver:** 69%
- **Adventure Archiv:** 60%
- **Just Adventure:** C (Ray Ivey), B (Robert Freese)

**2009 - Captain Nemo Hidden Object Game:**
Mzone Studio and Solilab developed a hidden object adventure game published by Anuman Interactive and various regional publishers including S.A.D. Software (Germany), Game Factory Interactive, and Russobit-M (Russia)[^ref-16]. The game was released for Windows (November 18, 2009), Macintosh (2009), iPhone (2010), and iPad (2010)[^ref-16]. Designer Christophe Leclerc helmed the project, with music composed by Dan Foster[^ref-16]. The game used "original illustrations by Alphonse De Neuville and Edouard Riou," the artists who illustrated the original 1870 novel[^ref-16].

Kinthdom's review stated "I cannot recommend this game due to how poor the game" and noted issues with "translation errors throughout game," "poor screen compatibility with modern monitors," and only supporting 4:3 aspect ratio[^ref-19].

**2018 - Steam Release:**
The Captain Nemo hidden object game received a Steam release on March 15, 2018, published by M.INDIE[^ref-20]. Steam user reviews were Mixed, with 67% of 43 reviews being positive[^ref-20].

**Minimum Requirements (Steam Version):**[^ref-20]
- **Processor:** 1 GHz
- **Memory:** 512 MB RAM
- **Graphics:** 512 MB

**2011/2013 - Extended Edition:**
An Extended Edition was released featuring "New puzzles, New scenes, New objects, New mini-games, New characters, An improved interface, brought to life with the collection of key objects, An improved gaming experience, A longer life span"[^ref-21]. The iOS version was released February 4, 2013 by Microids[^ref-21].

**2016-2017 - Living a Book Interactive Novel:**
Living a Book, Inc. released a mobile interactive book adaptation for iPhone (December 23, 2016), iPad (2016), and Android (2017)[^ref-22]. The developers described their approach: "At Living a Book we believe that books are to be lived, to generate intense emotions and unforgettable memories in the reader. We created a new concept of reading, they're not just books or Chat stories, nor games, we call them PATHBOOKS"[^ref-22]. The application featured "the complete original Book by JULES VERNE with great special features" including "Interactive book with choices and multiple endings. A masterpiece of literature even more intense"[^ref-22].

### Collections

The 1988 Coktel Vision game is not sold on GOG or Steam. The later *Captain Nemo* hidden object game is sold on Steam[^ref-20].

### Related Publications

The game's source material has generated extensive related publications over more than 150 years:

- **Original Novel:** "Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas: An Underwater Tour of the World" by Jules Verne, originally serialized March 1869-June 1870[^ref-5]
- **Illustrated Edition:** Deluxe edition published November 1871 featuring 111 illustrations by Alphonse de Neuville and Édouard Riou[^ref-5]
- **Educational Adaptations:** Various annotated editions for language learning, including "Russian Reader: Elementary. 20000 leagues under the sea by J. Verne, annotated" by Kristina Malidovskaya (CreateSpace Independent Publishing Platform, 2016)[^ref-23]

### Critical Perspective

20,000 Leagues Under the Sea is an early Coktel Vision literary adaptation whose real-time structure, absence of a save function and ways to become unwinnable define how it plays[^ref-4]. Its MobyGames averages (54% from critics, 2.4/5 from players) suggest a modest reputation[^ref-4]. Later adaptations of Verne's novel took it in other directions, from educational software to hidden object games to interactive books[^ref-12][^ref-16][^ref-22].
