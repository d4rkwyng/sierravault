# Lords of the Realm II

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Lords of the Realm II is a medieval strategy game that masterfully blends turn-based resource management with real-time combat, set against a rich historical backdrop of 13th-century England.[^ref-1] Released in North America on November 20, 1996 (Steam's store page lists October 31),[^ref-6][^ref-55] the game was developed by [[Impressions Games]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]], which had bought Impressions in 1995.[^ref-54] The game places players in the role of a feudal lord competing against four other nobles for the vacant English throne following the death of the king in 1268 A.D.[^ref-3]

What distinguished Lords of the Realm II from many medieval strategy games of its era was its commitment to historical authenticity over fantasy elements—the game features no magic, no supernatural forces, and no technology tree.[^ref-4] Instead, players must carefully manage food, population, and happiness levels to build their power base while avoiding Malthusian population crashes.[^ref-4] PC Gamer praised the game as offering "the best of two worlds, adding the real time combat model of a WarCraft II to the turn-based strategic elements of a Civilization."[^ref-5]

The game sold well. Sierra reported more than 350,000 copies sold worldwide by November 1997,[^ref-53] it peaked at number 9 on PC Data's chart in January 1997,[^ref-7] and it was among the best-selling computer games of 1997 and 1998.[^ref-6] [[David Lester]] later said it sold 2.5 million copies worldwide.[^ref-6] Steam user reviews remain overwhelmingly positive decades later: 96% of 1,718 reviews (all languages) as of October 2026.[^ref-5]

## Story Summary

The year is 1268 A.D., and the King of England has died without an heir to succeed him.[^ref-3] While the news spreads sadness across the land among the common folk, for the nobility it signals an opportunity—the crown is now within reach for any lord bold enough to seize it.[^ref-10] The lands of England and Wales are ruled by powerful feudal nobles who cruelly oppress the people, and all have their eyes on the English throne, willing to do anything to claim it.[^ref-11]

Five nobles, the player included, compete for the crown. MobyGames describes the four computer rivals as "the noble Baron, the headstrong Knight, the ice princess The Countess, and the backstabbing Bishop".[^ref-1] AllGame noted that any of them can be the player's ally for a while, but will attack once they feel more powerful.[^ref-12]

The path to the throne is inevitably a bloody one. Those who would dare deny the player's right to rule must face their wrath on the battlefield.[^ref-14] Victory requires not just military might but also economic prowess—a well-managed realm provides the resources necessary for conquest. Players must balance the needs of their subjects against the demands of war, for unhappy populations will revolt if happiness drops below 25 and stays there for more than four seasons.[^ref-14]

The game's narrative is straightforward yet compelling in its simplicity: "Because in the end, you're either King, or you're dead."[^ref-15] This stark premise drives all gameplay decisions, from the allocation of peasants between farming and military service to the design of castle fortifications.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Lords of the Realm II features a simple interface that is easy to pick up, making it an excellent starting point for those new to the strategy genre.[^ref-16] Slider bars were added to automate parts of the economy so that players interested in battles could focus on real-time combat.[^ref-6] The game combines mouse-driven county management screens with keyboard controls for real-time battles.[^ref-1]

The interface allows players to be as hands-on or hands-off as they prefer—a steward AI can help manage the kingdom for those who want to focus on military conquest rather than economic micromanagement.[^ref-11] However, experienced players recommend micromanaging everything rather than trusting the work sliders and auto-calculating battles.[^ref-18]

### Structure and Progression

The game operates on a seasonal turn-based system for county and economic management, switching to real-time control during battles and sieges.[^ref-9] Each turn represents one season, during which players allocate their population between various tasks: farming, cattle herding, weapons manufacturing, and castle construction.[^ref-1]

- **County Management:** Players control individual counties, managing population distribution, food production, and resource gathering on a turn-by-turn basis.[^ref-4]
- **Army Management:** Troops must be recruited, equipped, and organized into armies that can be moved across the strategic map.[^ref-3]
- **Castle Building:** Defensive fortifications can be constructed in any county, with multiple castle designs available from simple wooden stockades to massive royal castles.[^ref-11]
- **Diplomacy:** Players can negotiate with rival lords, though contemporary reviewers noted that diplomacy was largely meaningless in practice.[^ref-19]
- **Real-Time Battles:** When armies clash, control switches to a Warcraft-style isometric battlefield where players command their troops directly.[^ref-20]

Single-player games use one of 24 maps.[^ref-6] AllGame listed real maps such as England, Scotland, Ireland and Australia alongside imaginary ones such as Yin-Yang, Rose, Pretzelland and Rubix World.[^ref-12]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The fundamental theorem of Lords of the Realm II is that the player's power is directly proportional to the skill with which they run their economy.[^ref-22] Players grow crops, accumulate resources, manufacture weapons, manage armies, build and lay siege to castles, capture provinces, and ultimately attempt to conquer their enemies.[^ref-3]

Armies are drawn from seven troop types: peasants, archers, macemen, swordsmen, crossbowmen, pikemen and knights. Home of the Underdogs notes that each has its own strengths and weaknesses, "although an army of knights usually is practically invincible."[^ref-51] If players shield their archers with a wall of pikemen, they can defeat enemy forces relatively cheaply.[^ref-23]

Perhaps the game's most remarkable feature is the need to carefully manage food, population, and happiness levels in order to build population levels while avoiding Malthusian meltdowns.[^ref-4]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Lords of the Realm II received strong reviews upon release, with critics praising its blend of economic simulation and tactical combat. PC Gamer awarded the game an exceptional 92% score, calling it "immense fun for both the empire-builder and the medieval combatant with all the intrigue and bloodletting you could want."[^ref-19]

GameSpot's Tim Soete gave the game 7.6 out of 10, acknowledging that "The Middle Ages were tough times and similarly, Lords of the Realm II is a tough game."[^ref-25] Other contemporary scores included 4/5 from *Computer Gaming World*, 85% from *Hyper*, 80% from *PC Zone* and 4/5 from *Next Generation*.[^ref-6] TotalGames.net awarded a 90%, noting that "Lords of the Realm was one of the best selling historical simulations ever but there was room for improvement. Lords 2 takes all the elements of the original and embellishes them, with added graphics, sound and gameplay."[^ref-19]

The Adrenaline Vault gave a score of 70%, noting that "The AI in this game failed to impress me. It was not bad, but I thought it would have presented more of a challenge."[^ref-19] Computer Games Magazine also scored it 70%, calling it "A solid game, but it isn't terribly original."[^ref-19]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been increasingly favorable, with many considering the game ahead of its time. One user review on Metacritic declared it "Simply way ahead of its time, a defining piece of the Grand Strategy Genre, featuring more polished and complex gameplay than titles from the Total War series offer 15 years later."[^ref-19] Steam user reviews show an overwhelming 96% positive rating from about 1,700 reviews.[^ref-5]

Home of the Underdogs called it "one of the most underrated strategy games of all time" and "the best game designed by David Lester, prolific designer and founder of Impressions."[^ref-51] Modern players appreciate that despite dated mechanics, they remain "rock solid, and the art style is timeless."[^ref-27]

## Development

### Origins

Lords of the Realm II was developed by Impressions Games following the success of the original Lords of the Realm in 1994.[^ref-17] The first game had won PC Gamer US's 1994 Best Historical Simulation award and was nominated for Computer Gaming World's Strategy Game of the Year.[^ref-17] While considered by some not so much a sequel as a remake featuring enhanced graphics and improved gameplay mechanics, Lords II expanded significantly on the original formula.[^ref-16]

Impressions general manager David Lester explained the decision to add real-time combat to *Next Generation* in 1996: "We wanted the game to be more multiplayer friendly, and one way to do that was by adding realtime combat. Besides, when you can bring a castle down by aiming a battering ram or a catapult at it in realtime, it's a lot more satisfying."[^ref-52] This shift from the original's turn-based battles to real-time tactical combat proved to be one of the game's most celebrated features.

### Production

David Lester sold Impressions Games to Sierra in 1995,[^ref-54] and Sierra announced Lords of the Realm II in 1996.[^ref-6] Lester, the studio's founder, was lead designer;[^ref-6][^ref-51] Impressions was also the studio behind the Caesar games, Pharaoh and the Civil War General series.[^ref-27]

Composer Keith Zizza worked on the game's soundtrack during the spring and summer of 1996.[^ref-28] It was his second game with Sierra, following Rise & Rule of Ancient Empires.[^ref-28] The audio production faced significant technical constraints due to CD-ROM space limitations.

### Technical Achievements

The game utilized the DOS4GW extender to overcome conventional memory limitations on DOS systems.[^ref-9] It combined a turn-based strategic layer with real-time tactical battles.[^ref-6]

The visual presentation featured 256-color square-pixel SVGA graphics at 640x480 resolution.[^ref-50] The real-time battles provided a Warcraft-style isometric view of the battlefield.[^ref-20]

### Cut Content

The game's soundtrack was originally intended to be released on the CD-ROM as 16-bit 44100 stereo WAV files, but there wasn't enough room on the CD to accommodate them.[^ref-28] Because the music files wouldn't stream directly off the CD, they had to be loaded into memory and were therefore released as 8-bit 11025 Hz files instead—a significant quality downgrade.[^ref-28] Composer Keith Zizza later created a remastered version of the soundtrack in 2020 to restore the intended audio quality.[^ref-30]

A file called "Pumkin.wav" appeared on the game disc as a composite file used only as filler to maximize disc data capacity; it was never meant to be played by users.[^ref-28]

### Technical Issues

The game suffered from various compatibility issues on systems newer than its original target platforms. Setup would lock up when run on faster PCs.[^ref-31] Users needed to play with display settings, using GPU scaling, 256-bit color, and 640x480 resolution for acceptable visuals on modern systems.[^ref-34]

Known issues include:[^ref-33]
- Distorted colors on newer Windows versions
- Multiplayer requires DirectPlay to be enabled
- Sierra Internet Gaming System has been shut down

A tax rate glitch exists where large numbers of counties at 50% tax rate causes high tax rates to increase happiness instead of decreasing it.[^ref-4] A castle building glitch also allows Royal Castle construction for reduced resources.[^ref-4]

Controlling units when attacking castles has been criticized as "nothing short of impossible" due to poor pathfinding—described as worse than controlling Dragoons in StarCraft.[^ref-35]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game features distinctive voice acting with iconic lines including "Slaughter THESE villagers, m'lord?"—a memorable quote that players recall fondly decades later.[^ref-35]
- Advanced farming mode is always better than default farming, contrary to what the interface might suggest.[^ref-18]
- The maximum population in a county appears to be capped at 2000 people.[^ref-18]
- Neutral counties can rebel on their own, and forum players report that rival nobles' counties can be pushed into rebellion by pillaging their food supply, though the Bishop's resist it.[^ref-23]
- The AI tends to "turtle" in a corner of the map during battles unless they significantly outnumber the player.[^ref-23]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sierra's November 1997 press release for the Lords Royal Collection said Lords of the Realm II had "sold more than 350,000 units worldwide".[^ref-53] PC Data ranked it the 19th best-selling computer game of 1998,[^ref-6] and David Lester later stated that it sold 2.5 million copies worldwide.[^ref-6] It appeared regularly on PC Data's monthly charts:

- December 1996: #14 on GameCenter's games-only version of the PC Data list[^ref-41]
- January 1997: #9 (bounced back from #16)[^ref-7]
- February 1997: #11[^ref-36]
- March 1997: #16[^ref-37]
- May 1997: #19[^ref-38]
- June 1997: #16[^ref-39]
- First Half 1997: #14 overall[^ref-40]

### Expansion Pack

The Lords of the Realm II: Siege Pack was released on June 10, 1997,[^ref-6] earning critical acclaim as "an excellent expansion to one of the best medieval strategy games ever made."[^ref-42] Home of the Underdogs praised it enthusiastically: "If only more expansion packs today gave as much bang for your buck. Two thumbs up, way up!"[^ref-42]

The expansion featured:[^ref-42]
- 20 new maps
- Skirmish Mode, with open-field battles or castle sieges
- Sieges with ten new castle designs
- A more challenging AI
- Map editor
- Enhanced diplomacy
- More random events
- New terrain features including flammable pitch

### Collections

The game has been released in several compilation formats:[^ref-44]
- **Royal Edition (1997):** Included Lords of the Realm, Lords of the Realm II, and the Siege Pack expansion
- **Lords of the Realm II + Siege Pack (1998):** Bundled main game with expansion
- **Lords of the Realm: Royal Edition (GOG, 2009):** Digital re-release including both original games and expansion[^ref-32]

### Fan Projects

A speedrunning community has formed around the game, with 32 total runs recorded by 4 unique players.[^ref-45] Notable speedrun records include:
- Original Campaign: 54m 38s by TheObserver429
- The New Campaign: 45m 47s by TheObserver429
- Quaintville Any% (Siege Pack): 34.767s by Admiral_Iddy[^ref-45]

The game inspired long-running community engagement, with detailed strategy guides created by players with over 28 years of experience.[^ref-18]

### Related Publications

- **Lords of the Realm II User's Manual:** Published by Sierra, praised by users for its quality ("Good manual")[^ref-46]
- **Strategy Guides:** Multiple fan-created guides available on GameFAQs and Steam Community, including comprehensive economic and military strategy documentation[^ref-18][^ref-22]

### Sequels and Series Impact

The game was followed by Lords of the Realm III in 2004, which received mixed reviews. GameSpot gave Lords III 84%, but most critics were disappointed—PC Gamer scored it only 66%, noting "Given the series' heritage and the long buildup to this entry, it's hard not to feel let down."[^ref-47] Computer and Video Games was harshest, giving it 19%: "God knows what happened to LOTR3, but the usually dependable Impressions Games has lost the plot on this one."[^ref-47]

Lords III shifted to a primarily real-time format with a full 3D engine, abandoning much of what made Lords II beloved.[^ref-48] Critics noted the gameplay became "horrendously simple" compared to its predecessor.[^ref-47]

### Critical Perspective

*PC Gamer* listed Lords of the Realm II among the best games of 1997 and ranked it the 38th best computer game of all time, praising its "hectic real-time battles" and its mix of real-time and turn-based strategy.[^ref-6]

Modern players often compare it to the Total War series: one retrospective says "mechanically it is similar to the Total War series" and describes it as "comfort food", a strategy game that "isn't overly demanding or stressful".[^ref-27]
