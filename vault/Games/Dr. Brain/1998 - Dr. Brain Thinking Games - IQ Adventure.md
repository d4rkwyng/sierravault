# Dr. Brain Thinking Games: IQ Adventure

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Dr. Brain Thinking Games: IQ Adventure is an educational action game with puzzle elements released in 1998 by Knowledge Adventure, Inc.[^ref-1] It was one of the first two games Knowledge Adventure produced from the earlier Sierra On-Line Dr. Brain series, under the codename "Dime".[^ref-7] A contemporary reviewer found the series had "changed substantially": Dr. Brain was now younger, and there was "a little more emphasis on fighting and combat."[^ref-2] Pitched at "tweenagers",[^ref-2] its puzzles try to teach creative problem solving, pattern and sequence recognition, deductive reasoning, gathering facts and drawing conclusions, and logical analysis.[^ref-1]

The game is a third-person isometric puzzle/adventure game.[^ref-7] The player is a test subject of Dr. Brain who is stranded in another universe after the Interdimensional Transporter Machine (IDTM) breaks apart, with robots eager to dissect the player's brain.[^ref-9][^ref-1] The test subject must explore a world of Plant People, Molemen and angry robots, finding the parts needed to repair the transporter and return home.[^ref-5][^ref-1] The game has also been released as "Dr. Brain: Mind Venture", "Dr. Brain: The Ultimate Games for Your Brain - MindVenture" and, in France, "L'aventure cérébrale".[^ref-1]

## Story Summary

The game opens with Dr. Brain having just completed his latest invention: the Interdimensional Transporter Machine (IDTM).[^ref-9] The player character, serving as Dr. Brain's test subject, wins a coin toss to determine who gets to try out the experimental device first.[^ref-9] However, the IDTM breaks apart, and the player crash-lands in an alternative universe.[^ref-9]

In this alien world, the player meets Plant People, Molemen and many angry robots.[^ref-5] The robots pursue the player with the intent to dissect their brain.[^ref-1] According to the game's store description, the robots who rule this world have polluted the planet and mutated its peaceful creatures into monsters.[^ref-24]

To escape this predicament and return to Earth, the player must find eleven missing pieces of the IDTM.[^ref-9] The store description promises "15 mind-bending missions through 4 bizarre environments".[^ref-24] Along the way, the player encounters ten otherworldly species and hundreds of puzzles.[^ref-5] The story ends on a cliffhanger: back in Dr. Brain's laboratory, the camera pans to the smouldering IDTM and three Eye Bots pop up. The walkthrough author TSC wrote: "As the game leaves you on such a cliffhanger, who knows? Maybe there'll be I.Q Adventure 2."[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Dr. Brain Thinking Games: IQ Adventure uses a third-person isometric perspective.[^ref-7] The player navigates through various environments, interacting with characters and objects while solving puzzles and avoiding hostile enemies.[^ref-1] Conversations with non-player characters use an HTML-inspired text dialog system, and Dr. Brain's lip-synced instructions were made with a tool Knowledge Adventure built for its whole line of titles.[^ref-7]

### Structure and Progression

The game is structured as fifteen missions through a variety of isometric terrains.[^ref-1] The box copy counts four environments.[^ref-24] The GameFAQs walkthrough by TSC counts five:[^ref-9]

- **Jungle:** Levels 1, 2, 5, 6, 7, 10 and 13
- **Cavern:** Levels 3, 4, 8, 11 and 12
- **Pollution:** Levels 5, 6, 7 and 13 (found only within the Jungle)
- **Outpost:** Levels 4, 7, 8 and 13 (robot outposts found within other environments)
- **Metropolis:** Levels 9, 14 and 15, which together form the Robot Fortress, with the final IDTM piece at its centre

Players progress by solving puzzles, collecting IDTM components, and evading robots and other hazards.[^ref-9] GameFAQs users most often rate the game's length at 10 hours (11 votes).[^ref-14]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

According to the game's description, puzzles teach players creative problem solving, pattern and sequence recognition, deductive reasoning, gathering facts and drawing conclusions, and logical analysis.[^ref-1] One example from a 1998 review asks the player to pick which of four designs is an unfolded cube in 20 seconds, while an alarm sounds and a Quiz Bot threatens to drain the player's brainpower.[^ref-2]

All puzzles and in-game quizzes were rule-based, so they would be different on every playthrough.[^ref-7] Levels were not laid out only by hand: a map specification language was used to generate variations of them.[^ref-7]

The game includes multiplayer modes for both cooperative and competitive play.[^ref-1] It could be played online, a feature the companion title Puzzle Madness lacked.[^ref-2] Players could chat with each other online.[^ref-7]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Period coverage of the game is thin. The main contemporary review found is Paul Stern's for the Hartford Courant, reprinted in The Seattle Times on December 20, 1998. It covered IQ Adventure together with Puzzle Madness.[^ref-2] Stern wrote that, despite "a little cartoonish warring", "the emphasis is clearly on thinking in all its many splendid forms." He added that "the programming has not lost its high standards for artwork and originality."[^ref-2] His 13-year-old preferred IQ Adventure over Puzzle Madness, citing its online capability and its "more varied and challenging series of adventures".[^ref-2]

The review noted that Dr. Brain had changed substantially from the Sierra era, becoming "a lot younger, more cool and more handsome, and there is a little more emphasis on fighting and combat."[^ref-2] Stern regretted that the social-awareness elements of the old Dr. Brain, such as cultural and racial diversity, were "relegated to history."[^ref-2]

### Modern Assessment

Few scores exist. MobyGames lists a critic average of 40%, based on a single rating, and a player average of 3.4 out of 5 from two ratings.[^ref-1] GameFAQs users rate it "Great" (13 ratings).[^ref-14] One Reddit user recalled: "I remember playing this game in our Windows 98 PC. I don't remember myself, nor my dad, nor my brother, ever beating this game."[^ref-13]

## Development

### Origins

The Dr. Brain series was created by [[Corey Cole]], who wanted to bring science and technology into adventure games.[^ref-15] The four original titles, from Castle of Dr. Brain (1991) to The Time Warp of Dr. Brain (1996), were Sierra On-Line games.[^ref-4]

After Sierra was acquired by CUC International in 1996, the series was continued by CUC's Knowledge Adventure division, which turned it into a more action-oriented game.[^ref-8] As lead programmer Jim Winstead Jr. put it, "KA and Sierra had wound up under the same corporate umbrella." The "games group" at Knowledge Adventure developed the new titles.[^ref-7] The group handled three projects at the time: IQ Adventure (codename "Dime"), Puzzle Madness (codename "Nickel") and the corporate website (codename "Penny").[^ref-7]

### Production

Jim Winstead Jr. was the lead programmer.[^ref-7] The game was written in C++ and used the networking and graphics library from Blizzard Entertainment, "another corporate sibling".[^ref-7] According to Winstead, "The whole game was very data-driven."[^ref-7] Before the art team had created the main character, he prototyped with "a little whirling tornado" so he could work on things like path-finding.[^ref-7]

Because the game was aimed at younger users, its multiplayer chat had a basic bad-word filter. Winstead believes all chat was logged and reviewed by someone from customer service.[^ref-7] The lip-synced animations of Dr. Brain were driven by audio files and frame timings feeding an eight-frame animation set.[^ref-7]

### Technical Achievements

Levels were generated from a map specification language rather than only being laid out by hand.[^ref-7] All puzzles and quizzes were rule-based, so each playthrough was different; Winstead added, "Sorry to our QA team!"[^ref-7] The released game's art was rendered down to a 256-color palette.[^ref-7]

Winstead no longer has the source code. He reflected that "it would be fairly straightforward to bring the game up on current platforms. You could probably even do it on WebAssembly or something else cross-platform."[^ref-7]

### Technical Issues

The game was designed for Windows 95 and 98.[^ref-16] Winstead suggests it "may still just work on a more current version of Windows that can run 32-bit apps."[^ref-7] Lutris hosts a community Wine install script for the CD version.[^ref-21]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- IQ Adventure was one of the first two Dr. Brain titles produced by Knowledge Adventure, which was under the same corporate umbrella as Sierra.[^ref-7]
- Its development codename was "Dime"; Puzzle Madness was "Nickel".[^ref-7]
- The main character was originally prototyped as a whirling tornado before the art team created the real character.[^ref-7]
- The game's networking and graphics libraries came from Blizzard Entertainment.[^ref-7]
- The story ends on a cliffhanger involving Eye Bots. The walkthrough author noted: "As the game leaves you on such a cliffhanger, who knows? Maybe there'll be I.Q Adventure 2."[^ref-9]
- The French release was titled "L'aventure cérébrale."[^ref-1]
- Dr. Brain himself was redesigned to be younger and more handsome than the original Sierra portrayal.[^ref-2]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for IQ Adventure have been found. For context, Sierra reported combined sales of the earlier Dr. Brain series above 350,000 units by the end of March 1996, before the Knowledge Adventure releases.[^ref-4]

The game retailed at $30, the same price as its companion title Puzzle Madness.[^ref-2]

### Collections

Dr. Brain Thinking Games: IQ Adventure has been distributed through various channels since its original release:

- Original US retail release (1998) and the "Mindventure" re-release (1999)[^ref-25]
- French release by Sierra On-Line (1999)[^ref-25]
- ZOOM Platform digital release as "Dr. Brain: Mind Venture" (2023), from Jordan Freeman Group and Jumpstart Games[^ref-25][^ref-24]

### Fan Projects

TSC wrote a walkthrough in July 2001 (version 0.4). It covers the game's fifteen levels, puzzles and quizzes, but only on the easy setting. It is hosted on GameFAQs.[^ref-9]

### Critical Perspective

Knowledge Adventure turned the series into a more action-oriented game.[^ref-8] Even so, Stern found that the emphasis remained "clearly on thinking in all its many splendid forms."[^ref-2] He also noted that, despite the shift toward fighting and combat, "the programming has not lost its high standards for artwork and originality."[^ref-2] Beyond that one review and a few user ratings, little critical writing on the game has been found.
