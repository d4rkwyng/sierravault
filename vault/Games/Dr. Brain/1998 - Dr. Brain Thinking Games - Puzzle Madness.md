# Dr. Brain Thinking Games: Puzzle Madness

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

Dr. Brain Thinking Games: Puzzle Madness, later known as Dr. Brain: Puzzleopolis, is the first of three games in Knowledge Adventure's revived Dr. Brain trilogy and is billed as an "interactive puzzle board game"[^ref-20]. It was released by Knowledge Adventure in October 1998 for Windows and Macintosh[^ref-30][^ref-34]. After CUC International acquired Sierra (1996) and Knowledge Adventure (early 1997), the series was continued by CUC's Knowledge Adventure division, which turned it into a more action-oriented game[^ref-14][^ref-42][^ref-43]; Jim Winstead, a programmer in Knowledge Adventure's games group, recalls that "KA and Sierra had wound up under the same corporate umbrella"[^ref-39]. Set in the year 2326, the game casts players as Pro, the good clone of Dr. Brain, against the evil clone Conn, who threatens to destroy the city[^ref-9][^ref-19].

Reviewing it alongside its sibling IQ Adventure, Paul Stern of the Hartford Courant wrote that although there is "a little cartoonish warring," in the Dr. Brain Thinking Games "the emphasis is clearly on thinking in all its many splendid forms"[^ref-8]. The game also appeared under the French title Family Fun: Logique Mania[^ref-19][^ref-30], and was sold on CD-ROM for both Windows and Macintosh[^ref-4][^ref-30].

## Story Summary

The game's narrative unfolds in the year 2326, where Dr. Brain's evil clone, Conn, is wreaking havoc on the city with strange schemes that threaten to destroy everything[^ref-9][^ref-14]. Players assume the role of Pro, Dr. Brain's good clone, who must stop him[^ref-9][^ref-19]. Wikipedia's series summary notes that this first Knowledge Adventure game "turns Dr. Brain into a brain sitting in a jar" while casting the player as his clone[^ref-21].

The retail listing promises over 20 unique locations and 15 characters[^ref-6]. As MobyGames' description, reproduced on LaunchBox, states: "Complete puzzles to get items and friends, which you will use to defeat Conn and his evil companions"[^ref-30][^ref-19].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Puzzle Madness is played from a top-down perspective with keyboard and mouse[^ref-30], and PCGamingWiki classes it as a single-player board, educational and puzzle game[^ref-34]. The manufacturer's recommended age was 9 and up[^ref-6].

IGN's cheat listing describes an in-game cheats console: entering a code preceded by a pound sign unlocks items such as the Android, Cab Token, Ear Muffs, Flash Gun and Gas Mask[^ref-22].

### Structure and Progression

The retail listing advertises "hundreds of variations of puzzles" and three difficulty levels[^ref-6]. To succeed, players must use "logic, strategy, planning, experimentation, and skill"[^ref-7]. Puzzle Madness is single-player: LaunchBox lists a maximum of one player[^ref-19], and Stern noted that IQ Adventure "has an online capability the other lacks"[^ref-8].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzles take the form of logic-oriented mini-games, which the player completes to earn devices for dueling the evil Conn and his flunkies[^ref-21]. Stern highlighted the variety: "How many kids' games let their operators fly a blimp or help stamp out computer viruses in the quest to defeat Dr. Brain's evil clone?"[^ref-8]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Stern's December 1998 review covered Puzzle Madness and IQ Adventure together, each priced at $30[^ref-8]. He pitched them to parents worried about "idiotic and violent computer games," but noted that the new Dr. Brain had "a little more emphasis on fighting and combat" and that the old games' social-awareness elements were "relegated to history"[^ref-8]. His 13-year-old preferred IQ Adventure, though Stern added, "Not that 'Puzzle Madness' isn't fun, too"[^ref-8].

### Later Assessment

TV Tropes calls the Dr. Brain series "one of the few examples of Edutainment Games which are actually good games in their own right," and notes that the Knowledge Adventure games recast Dr. Brain "as a twenty-something genius," with the Thinking Games sub-series placing "less of an emphasis on educational content"[^ref-40].

## Development

### Origins

Knowledge Adventure took over the Dr. Brain series after CUC International acquired Sierra (1996) and Knowledge Adventure (early 1997)[^ref-14][^ref-42][^ref-43]. According to Winstead, the Knowledge Adventure games group handled three projects at the time: IQ Adventure (codename "Dime"), Puzzle Madness (codename "Nickel"), and the corporate website (codename "Penny")[^ref-39].

### Production

Winstead writes, "I believe that Puzzle Madness was developed in Acomplish, the in-house proprietary multimedia scripting language"[^ref-39]; he was lead programmer on IQ Adventure, not Puzzle Madness, so this is a recollection rather than a confirmed detail.

MobyGames credits 89 people[^ref-30]. Ari Schindler was executive producer and Albert H. M. Reinhardt producer; Marcus Smith was associate producer and writer; Thomas M. Fiola was lead programmer; Charles Zembillas designed the Dr. Brain character; and Giorgio Bertuccelli composed the music for Sublime Music[^ref-30]. Character voices were provided by Andy Milder, Victor Raider Wexler, Neil Ross and Carol Bach-y-Rita[^ref-30].

### Technical Details

For Windows, the listed requirements were Windows 95 or 98, a Pentium 90 MHz, 16 MB RAM, a quad-speed CD-ROM drive, 15 MB (or 50 MB) of free hard-drive space and SVGA 256-color graphics[^ref-6]. Mac users needed a Power PC 60 MHz, 8 MB RAM, a quad-speed CD-ROM, a 13-inch or larger color monitor and System 7.5 or higher[^ref-6].

## Legacy

Puzzle Madness was the first of Knowledge Adventure's Dr. Brain games and marks the point where the series moved from Sierra's puzzle games to Knowledge Adventure's more action-oriented approach[^ref-14][^ref-21].

The game is preserved at the Internet Archive[^ref-7] and the Macintosh Repository[^ref-28]. The Internet Archive notes suggest a PPC Mac OS emulator such as SheepShaver or a virtual machine to run it on modern computers[^ref-7], and Lutris offers a Wine install script for the CD version[^ref-41].

For context, Sierra reported that combined sales of its earlier Dr. Brain games had passed 350,000 units by the end of March 1996, before Knowledge Adventure took the series over[^ref-14].
