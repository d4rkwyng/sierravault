# Lode Runner On-Line: The Mad Monks' Revenge

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Lode Runner On-Line: The Mad Monks' Revenge* is an enhanced version of *[[1994 - Lode Runner - The Legend Returns|Lode Runner: The Legend Returns]]*. Presage Software (now Human Code) developed it and Sierra On-Line published it in 1995 for Windows 95 and the Macintosh.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-4] Its main addition is two-player play over a modem or local network. Despite the "On-Line" title, it keeps the full single-player game and can be played entirely offline.[^ref-1][^ref-4]

Sierra's *InterAction* magazine promised "two new worlds and 180 new levels".[^ref-17] Its feature list broke this down as 30 new single-player levels and 150 new two-player levels. It also listed a new "liquid tiles" hazard that kills any character touching it, and items that only one of the two players can use.[^ref-5] The *Albuquerque Journal* described the same tiles differently, as ones "which like to adhere on contact", alongside "phaze befuddlement gas, which disorients monks for a limited time" and slime and glue buckets "which slow down the adventurer".[^ref-20] The game's lead programmer later wrote that the original 16-bit code "was updated to 32-bit for Windows 95".[^ref-7]

Reviews were sharply divided. Germany's *PC Action* gave it 75%.[^ref-8] The *Albuquerque Journal* reviewed the Windows 95 version as "a superb update the entire family will enjoy" and rated it 5, on a scale the clipping does not state.[^ref-20] *MacFormat* gave the Mac version 76%.[^ref-18] *Computer Games Magazine* gave it 1/5 per Wikipedia and called it "an absolute waste for anyone but a puzzle freak".[^ref-1][^ref-9] The UK's *PC Zone* gave it 20%.[^ref-10] Home of the Underdogs later called it "the new and improved version" that fans of *The Legend Returns* would love "at least twice as much".[^ref-4]

## Story Summary

The premise carries over from *The Legend Returns*. *PC Action* described the subtitle story as following on seamlessly from the earlier game, with the player and a friend getting the chance to win back the treasure the monks had stolen.[^ref-8] Sierra's *InterAction* described the core game as unchanged: the player is still Jake Peril, a treasure-hunter raiding the lairs of the evil monks for their gold and gems while the red-cloaked monks give chase.[^ref-5] A second player controls Jake's partner, Wes Reckless. Some items can be picked up only by the player of the matching colour.[^ref-1]

*Computer Games Magazine*'s reviewer framed it more cynically: "You are a man on a hostile alien world trying to steal all of their treasure and escape with your life."[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Control is unchanged from the 1994 game. Jake runs, climbs ladders, crosses bars hand over hand and digs temporary holes with a laser blaster to trap the monks.[^ref-11][^ref-9] *PC Player* noted that the screens still do not scroll, but now run at 640×480 instead of the earlier 640×400. Levels can be chosen directly from the menu.[^ref-11] Two players can share one PC or connect by modem or network. Even in network mode, the game supports only two runners.[^ref-11][^ref-12]

### Structure and Progression

*PC Player* counted a dozen underground worlds with 187 levels in single-player mode, 30 of them new. Sierra designed 150 entirely new stages for two players.[^ref-11] *PC Action* likewise described all the original levels plus 150 new ones built for two-player mode.[^ref-8] Period sources do not agree on the level counts, so they are set out side by side:

| Source | What it says about levels |
|---|---|
| *InterAction*, Fall 1995 | "two new worlds and 180 new levels"[^ref-17] |
| *InterAction*, Holiday 1995 | "130 new levels added to the original game" in the intro text; the feature list gives thirty more single-player and 150 more two-player puzzles[^ref-5] |
| *PC Player* 5/96 | A dozen worlds with 187 single-player levels, 30 of them new; 150 new two-player levels[^ref-11] |
| *PC Player* 12/98 | 150 additional two-player and 30 new single-player levels[^ref-22] |
| *Philadelphia Daily News*, August 1996 | "more than 300 levels of play"; 30 more levels for solo play and 150 new levels for two players[^ref-21] |
| *Computer & Games* (Brazil) 27, October 1996 | 180 stages in total, of which 120 were created for two players[^ref-23] |
| *MacFormat* 38, June 1996 | "The 150 two-player network/modem levels" (photo caption)[^ref-18] |

Sierra's own feature list gave the additions as:[^ref-5]

- **Network options:** "challenge anyone, anywhere", either over a local area network or by modem
- **Player-specific items:** treasures and items usable by only one player at a time, to encourage cooperative or competitive puzzles
- **Two new worlds** with new animations
- **Liquid tiles:** a new trap that kills any character it touches
- **Extra puzzles:** 30 more single-player and 150 more two-player puzzles than the original

Two players can cooperate against the monks and split the gold, or "slug it out with each other". *PC Player* noted that some levels can only be completed as a team.[^ref-5][^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Wikipedia describes the new elements:[^ref-1]

- **Self-igniting bombs** that constantly go off and reappear on a timer
- **Phase blocks** that vanish and reappear on a timer, crushing any player or monk standing in the gap when they return
- **Player-coloured items:** blue-outlined bombs, for example, can be picked up only by Wes Reckless
- **A more complex level editor** that allows multiple exits, each of which can lead to a different level in a pack

Home of the Underdogs summed up the additions as "regenerating bombs, disappearing turf, and 'personalized' items that can only be used by one of the two players".[^ref-4] *PC Player* noted deadly new acid surfaces, rare hiding places, and tools such as bombs, traps and axes. The construction kit was unchanged and easy to use.[^ref-11]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Date | Platform | Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Computer Games Magazine* | December 20, 1995 | Windows 95 | 1/5 per Wikipedia[^ref-1][^ref-9] |
| *PC Action* | March 1996 | Windows 95 | 75%[^ref-8] |
| *PC Zone* No. 37 | April 1996 | Windows 95 | 20%[^ref-10] |
| *Joystick* No. 70 | April 1996 | Macintosh | Intérêt 20%, Technique 30%[^ref-12] |
| *MacFormat* No. 38 | June 1996 | Macintosh | 76% (Graphics 70%, Sound 59%, Gameplay 80%)[^ref-18] |
| *PC Player* | May 1996 | Windows 95 | 2/5 (Monika Stoschek); the editors' star ratings ran from 1 to 3 (3, 3, 2, 2, 1 in the July 1996 index)[^ref-11][^ref-19] |
| *Albuquerque Journal* | July 2, 1996 | Windows 95 | Rating: 5 (scale not stated)[^ref-20] |
| *Philadelphia Daily News* | August 1, 1996 | Not stated (the roundup covers Mac and Windows titles) | 2.5/4 per Wikipedia; the clipping text does not clearly show the rating[^ref-1][^ref-21] |

**PC Action.** Adrian Vogler wrote that the game was technically no different from *Lode Runner*, "aber das einfache Spielprinzip hat es in sich" ("but the simple game principle packs a punch"). He judged its graphics and sound "ohne Tadel" ("without fault"), but joked that Germany's raised telephone charges made modem play affordable only at night-time rates.[^ref-8]

**PC Player.** Reviewer Monika Stoschek questioned why Sierra had followed the cheaper first version with a Windows 95-only edition that left out many customers. She found some of the many levels "schlicht langweilig" ("simply boring") and some unfair, and suggested the content would have made more sense as a cheaper level disk for DOS and Windows 3.1.[^ref-11]

**Computer Games Magazine.** Daryll Henrich wrote that "The only extra in this version is the networking support". He concluded: "Overall, this game is an absolute waste for anyone but a puzzle freak. Why spend hours upon hours running around stealing someone's gold for no apparent reason? I don't know, I wouldn't do it."[^ref-9]

**PC Zone.** The review called the game "just as dull" as the original, with "Teeny weeny sprites", and mocked the modem option.[^ref-10]

**Joystick.** The French magazine reviewed the Mac CD-ROM. It complained that online play added nothing beyond the existing two-player mode on one keyboard. It added that the reviewer's old *Lode Runner* had run faster on an Apple II than this one did on a Power Mac.[^ref-12]

**MacFormat.** Garrick Webster reviewed the Mac version. He wrote that "although it is fun to dip into now and again for some light puzzling action, it's not really as compelling as the original was in its day." The review box lists it at £39.99, "Out: now", needing an LC II or better, 8 MB RAM, System 7.0 and a double-speed CD-ROM drive.[^ref-18]

**Albuquerque Journal.** The 2 July 1996 roundup, with Philip Griego as primary reviewer, called it "a superb update the entire family will enjoy". It listed a 486DX33, 8 MB RAM, a 2x CD-ROM drive and Windows 95, and noted that the game can be played by modem or over an IPX-compatible network.[^ref-20]

**Philadelphia Daily News.** Marianne Costantinou's August 1996 roundup of kids' games said some of the moves "were frustrating", that the laser gun seemed to shoot only left, and that "a joystick is recommended".[^ref-21]

**Awards.** Presage's product page listed honours alongside the game. These were *Games Magazine*'s "Best New Arcade Game", Top 100 Products finalist (*Home PC*), Best Macintosh Game of the Year finalist (*MacUser*), All Star Software (*Children's Software Revue*), and Best Action Arcade finalist at the SPA Codie Awards, the last marked "(1994)".[^ref-3] At least two of these belong to the 1994 game. In autumn 1995, before *On-Line* shipped, Sierra's *InterAction* reported that "The new Legend Returns has already taken Best New Arcade Game honors (Games Magazine) and was a finalist for Best Macintosh Game of the Year (Mac User Magazine)."[^ref-17] The lead programmer also dates the *Games Magazine* award to 1994.[^ref-7] Presage's page does not separate the two releases.

### Modern Assessment

Home of the Underdogs rates the game 8.6 from 282 votes. Its review regrets the name, which "has probably led many people to believe that this is an on-line-only game". It praises the extra tile sets for making "the level editor much more robust and fun to design new levels with."[^ref-4] In 2020 Apple II Bits' Ken Gagne saw boxed copies of both Presage games in a friend's collection, then played *The Mad Monks' Revenge* through the fan-made Definitive Edition. He noted its opening enemy-free levels and later mechanics such as keys, doors and bombs.[^ref-13]

## Development

### Origins

Wikipedia's article on *The Legend Returns* reports that Sierra released *Lode Runner Online* a year after the 1994 game, fixing many bugs and adding gameplay features.[^ref-14] *The Legend Returns*' lead programmer, credited as Todd Daggert, describes the sequel: "For this sequel multi-player gameplay was added, allowing two persons to play Lode Runner together over a network, modem, or on the same machine. At the same time many new levels and game elements were introduced, and the original 16-bit code was updated to 32-bit for Windows 95."[^ref-7] Presage said it "designed and developed both the Macintosh and Windows95 versions".[^ref-3]

### Production

According to the lead programmer, design on the Lode Runner projects was done by the programmers themselves: "Game elements were brainstormed, added and balanced exclusively by the programmers as needed, especially in the case of Lode Runner Online."[^ref-7] Wikipedia lists Jake Hoelter as director, with Todd Daggert, Mick Foley, Jake Hoelter and Craig A. Vinet as designers.[^ref-1]

### Technical Achievements

The lead programmer lists the technologies as Windows 95, "IPX fault-tolerant networking" and DirectX 1.0 modem support. His notes, which cover both Presage games together, list 1.44 MB floppy and CD-ROM media and say the CD "contained over a dozen full-length original redbook tracks".[^ref-7] Every 1995–96 listing and review found for *On-Line* shows it on CD-ROM only; no floppy edition has been documented.[^ref-5][^ref-6][^ref-8][^ref-10] *PC Player* confirmed that the CD's audio tracks play in an ordinary CD player.[^ref-11] *PC Action* noted that almost the whole game could be copied to the hard disk to avoid slow CD-ROM drives.[^ref-8]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The lead programmer's notes say that clicking a hidden hotspot in the upper-left corner of the 640×480 screen and typing "YOG" plays a clang and unlocks a cheat menu in *Lode Runner Online*.[^ref-7]
- Home of the Underdogs reports that the lead programmer made the game a free download in 2002.[^ref-4]

## Legacy

### Successors

Presage's next original Lode Runner game was *Lode Runner 2* (1998), published by GT Interactive and MacSoft rather than Sierra.[^ref-15]

### Collections

No compilation or digital-store re-release of *Lode Runner On-Line* was found during research for this page.

### Fan Projects

*Mad Monks' Revenge: Definitive Edition* by Quark Robot is a fan-made, cross-platform clone written from scratch. It keeps "same graphics, same gameplay, and even the same bugs", runs on Windows, macOS and Linux, and supports online play with up to four friends. The site notes that the Presage Lode Runner titles are owned by Activision/Sierra and that the project has no association with either company; it also credits the *Lode Runner* trademark to Tozai. The site lists version 24.12 as the current stable release.[^ref-16] Apple II Bits reported that it stays compatible with custom levels made for the 1995 original.[^ref-13]
