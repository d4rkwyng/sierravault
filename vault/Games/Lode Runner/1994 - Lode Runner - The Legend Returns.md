# Lode Runner: The Legend Returns

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Lode Runner: The Legend Returns* is a 1994 remake of Doug Smith's 1983 *Lode Runner*. Presage Software developed it and Sierra On-Line published it.[^ref-1][^ref-2] Presage designed and developed both the Windows and Macintosh versions.[^ref-3] The credits place the project under [[Jeff Tunnell Productions]], with [[Jeff Tunnell]] as executive producer and [[Randy Dersham]] as producer.[^ref-4] Some contemporary coverage credited the game to [[Dynamix]] instead: *Computer Gaming World* listed Dynamix as publisher, and *Next Generation* called it "Dynamix's retrogame".[^ref-5][^ref-6]

The rules are those of the original. The runner collects every piece of gold on a single-screen level while avoiding the robed "mad monks", and digs holes with his blaster to trap them. Collecting the gold opens the exit to the next level.[^ref-7][^ref-2] Presage added collectible tools such as bombs, jackhammers, slime buckets and monk snares. It also added head-to-head and cooperative play for two players on one computer, and a level editor called the Game Generator.[^ref-7][^ref-5] The single-player game holds 150 levels in ten themed worlds of fifteen levels each.[^ref-2][^ref-8]

Critics split over whether a faithful remake was enough. *Computer Gaming World* called it "a great game, but it arrives in a world which might have already passed it by".[^ref-5] *Next Generation* gave the PC version two stars out of five, and later gave the Mac version four.[^ref-6][^ref-9][^ref-2] *Games Magazine* named it Best New Arcade Game of 1994.[^ref-1][^ref-10] Sierra followed it in 1995 with an enhanced edition, *[[1995 - Lode Runner On-Line - The Mad Monks' Revenge|Lode Runner On-Line: The Mad Monks' Revenge]]*.[^ref-2]

## Story Summary

Sierra's own magazine *InterAction* set out the premise. The mad monks of the Evil Empire have stolen all the gold and gems in the world. The player is Jake Peril, "a notorious bounty hunter and all-around hero-type with indefatigable legs", whose job is to infiltrate their underground labyrinths and steal the riches back.[^ref-8] *Electronic Games* noted that this "wacky backstory" replaced the original game's Bungeling Empire.[^ref-11]

The descent runs through ten underground worlds. The Sierra Chest's walkthrough calls them Moss, Fungus, Ancient, Crystal, Ice, Fossil, Lava, Phosphorus, Dark and Industrial.[^ref-12] Wikipedia gives their in-game titles, such as Moss Caverns, the Lost City of Ur, Winter's Dungeon, Inferno's Playground, the Shadowlands and Meltdown Metropolis. In the Shadowlands the screen is dark except for a circle of light around the player.[^ref-2] A second player can join as Jake's partner Wes Reckless, who wears blue.[^ref-12][^ref-2] Short animations play between worlds. The manual warns that players who skip levels or play them out of order will miss these, including the final scene.[^ref-7]

> [!warning]- Ending Spoiler
> According to Wikipedia, the game ends in the industrial world with Jake calling an elevator to the surface. It arrives but malfunctions, leaving him to climb what is presumably a very tall staircase.[^ref-2]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The game can be played with the keyboard, mouse or joystick.[^ref-7][^ref-11] In Game Bytes' review, the Windows version shipped with its own joystick driver, but it defaulted to keyboard control and did not save configuration changes.[^ref-7] With the default settings, Jake uses the numeric keypad and Wes uses a second set of keys shown in the options menu.[^ref-12] *Electronic Games* thought the joystick was "the only control device any gamer should even consider using". It praised the icon interface, whose tooltips explain each button's function.[^ref-11]

The main menu offers:[^ref-12]

- Single-player Lode Runner play
- Head-to-head play
- Homemade levels
- Loading level sets from disk
- The Game Generator

### Structure and Progression

The 150 single-player levels are grouped into ten worlds of fifteen levels each. A separate set of two-player levels is included, and Wikipedia gives the count as 30.[^ref-8][^ref-2] The first level has no monks. Once all the gold is collected a door appears, and later levels add monks and new obstacles.[^ref-7] Levels can be played in any order, and the game can be saved. Game Bytes' reviewer noted that the player earns an extra life for every level completed.[^ref-7]

Jake is introduced to new tools as he progresses, so the arsenal grows through the game.[^ref-7][^ref-11] Two players can race each other for gold or cooperate against the monks. *InterAction* noted that some levels require the two to work together.[^ref-8]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The blaster digs temporary holes. Monks that fall in are delayed, can be walked over, and always drop any gold they are carrying.[^ref-7] *Computer Gaming World* found the monks "proximity sensitive": they try to get as close to the player as they can, without realising they sometimes need to take the long way round. Players can exploit this by luring a monk to pick up unreachable gold and then trapping him to release it.[^ref-5] *InterAction* reported that "some of Doug Smith's original artificial intelligence programming was incorporated into the game's engine."[^ref-8]

New tools, as listed by Game Bytes, *Electronic Games* and Wikipedia:[^ref-7][^ref-11][^ref-2]

- **Bombs:** small and large. Large bombs permanently destroy turf.
- **Jackhammer:** breaks through bedrock.
- **Slime bucket:** slows pursuers.
- **Mad Monk Snare** and **Befuddlement Gas:** hold or stun monks.
- **Pickaxe:** brings down rock from the ceiling to block enemies.
- **Hideouts:** spots where Jake can hide from the monks.
- **Spotlight:** for the dark levels.
- **Transporter:** moves the player across the level.

New terrain includes "gooey turf" that slows everyone down, as well as trapdoor turf and bedrock.[^ref-2] *Next Generation* singled out "ice and slime".[^ref-6] Keys and locked doors also appear.[^ref-5][^ref-12]

The Game Generator lets players build levels and whole level groups. Levels can be set by day or night, use any tile set and use any background music.[^ref-2][^ref-7] *Computer Gaming World* called its interface "solid, intuitive, and a 'lode' of fun".[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Date | Platform | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Computer Gaming World* | November 1994 | Windows | Review by Paul C. Schuytema; pros "A classic game is reborn, and it plays like a dream"[^ref-5] |
| *Electronic Games* | December 1994 | PC | B+, Bill Kunkel[^ref-11] |
| *Pyramid* No. 11 | January 1995 | PC | Pyramid Pick[^ref-2] |
| *Next Generation* No. 3 | March 1995 | PC | 2/5[^ref-6][^ref-2] |
| *Wired* | April 1995 | Mac/DOS/Windows | Positive, Zach Meston[^ref-13] |
| *Next Generation* No. 8 | August 1995 | Macintosh | 4/5[^ref-9][^ref-2] |

**Computer Gaming World.** Paul C. Schuytema loved the play but questioned the point of a straight remake. Judged as if seen for the first time, it was "a wonderful, wonderful product". Judged against late-1994 design, he wrote that it "falls a little short, being only a rehash of an earlier design". The magazine listed the cons as "More of a rehash than a reinvention. Doesn't push the envelope."[^ref-5]

**Electronic Games.** Bill Kunkel found the remake "remarkably faithful to the original, almost to the point of visual austerity". He argued that "A great game in 1983 should still be a great game today", but admitted the nagging feeling that it would have been more fun with state-of-the-art graphics.[^ref-11]

**Next Generation.** The PC review praised the 256-colour graphics, soundtrack and "gruesome" sound effects. It concluded that basic play "is about the same as the original, a fact that may not appeal to those looking for a unique challenge".[^ref-6] The Mac review a few months later was far warmer: "there isn't one aspect of it that isn't better than the original one." It added that "Internal arguments have prevented us from rating this game with five stars."[^ref-9]

**Wired.** Zach Meston called it "even better" than the original and said it "drags Lode Runner kicking and screaming into the era of SVGA graphics and MIDI sound".[^ref-13]

**Game Bytes.** Angel Lynn reported finishing all 150 levels and building 30 new ones after two weeks with the game. The review recommended the game "very highly" to fans of the original, while noting occasional Windows protection faults and video-driver complaints.[^ref-7]

**Pyramid.** According to Wikipedia, the magazine contrasted the remake with a classic "which was almost instantly addictive", and noted that the monks now tear the player apart "in a delightfully graphic manner".[^ref-2]

**Awards.** *Games Magazine* named the game Best New Arcade Game of 1994.[^ref-1][^ref-10] The game's lead programmer recalled that it won in the same year that *Myst* took *Games*' Game of the Year.[^ref-10] *PC Gamer US* nominated it for Best Puzzle Game of 1994, but the award went to Coktel Vision's *[[1993 - Goblins Quest 3|Goblins Quest 3]]*.[^ref-2]

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames lists a 75% critic average from 15 ratings and a 3.8/5 player average from 34 ratings.[^ref-1] On MyAbandonware the game holds 4.4/5 from 221 votes. Its Home of the Underdogs review calls it "not only worthy of the name, but ... also leaps and bounds better" than Brøderbund's original.[^ref-14] The GOG Dreamlist entry asking for a re-release had 411 votes when it was captured for this page's research. Several of its posts single out the level editor and couch co-op.[^ref-15]

Later console versions drew a cooler response. IGN's 1998 review of Natsume's PlayStation compilation scored it 6 ("okay"), saying it was "just what you remember", though the characters were "extremely tiny".[^ref-16] *Next Generation* said the PlayStation release "adds little that's new" but was "well worth a look" for newcomers.[^ref-17]

## Development

### Origins

Presage Software was founded in 1986 and was based in San Rafael, California. Its company page listed *The Prince of Persia*, *Lemmings*, *Lode Runner* and *Star Wars Screen Entertainment* among its entertainment successes.[^ref-18] In 1993, according to lead programmer Daggert's own project page, "Presage Software acquired the rights to the classic Lode Runner game by Doug Smith and set out to create a new, updated version for modern machines."[^ref-10] MobyGames credits Douglas E. Smith for the "Original Lode Runner Game".[^ref-4]

The project ran under [[Jeff Tunnell Productions]].[^ref-4] *InterAction* pointed out that these were "the same people who brought the world The Incredible Machine".[^ref-8] *Electronic Games* wrote that Presage re-created the game "under the guiding hand of Jeff Tunnell", and that Tunnell gave it its new backstory.[^ref-11] *InterAction* likewise credited "Jeff Tunnell and company" with adding the new features.[^ref-8]

### Production

MobyGames' Windows credits list:[^ref-4]

- **Director:** Jake Hoelter
- **Game engine:** Todd Daggert, Craig A. Vinet
- **Platform programmers:** Jake Hoelter (Macintosh), Craig A. Vinet (MS-DOS), Todd Daggert (Windows)
- **Art director:** Steve Snyder
- **Level director:** Dane Bigham (one of thirteen credited level designers)
- **Music director:** Matthew Berardo
- **Composition:** Matthew Berardo, Brian Salter
- **Executive producer:** Jeffrey Tunnell (Jeff Tunnell Productions)
- **Producer:** Randy Dersham
- **Quality assurance:** Sierra's team under David Steele

Daggert's page describes the lead programmer's job as dividing work among the team, leading the Windows 3.1 effort and keeping the code base in sync "between two different remote offices". It adds that the programmers also designed the game: "there was no significant paper design to begin with. Game elements were brainstormed, added and balanced exclusively by the programmers as needed."[^ref-10]

### Technical Achievements

*InterAction* described the art as "Repainted using SVGA resolution and a glowing 256 colors". It compared the percussion-heavy score to a "Raiders of the Lost Ark ambiance".[^ref-8] The Sierra Chest lists 22 music tracks, with an introduction and a theme for each world.[^ref-19] According to Daggert, the game was written in C and C++.[^ref-10] A MobyGames trivia entry says that level 77 is a tribute to C++.[^ref-1]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- After all the treasure on level 80 is collected, the letters "LR'94" appear, according to MobyGames.[^ref-1]
- Changing the Windows video resolution while the game is minimised produces the message "In R'yleth doth Cthulhu lay".[^ref-1]
- Daggert recalled a cheat menu that is unlocked by clicking hidden hotspots in the corners of the screen and typing "YOG" or "CTHULHU" ("I forget which"). With cheats on, the player can control the monks.[^ref-10]

## Legacy

### Successors

In 1995 Presage and Sierra released *[[1995 - Lode Runner On-Line - The Mad Monks' Revenge|Lode Runner On-Line: The Mad Monks' Revenge]]*. It added network and modem play, new levels and new hazards.[^ref-2][^ref-10] Presage went on to develop the isometric *Lode Runner 2* (1998), which was published by GT Interactive and MacSoft.[^ref-21]

### Collections

The PlayStation *Lode Runner* (1998) combined *The Legend Returns* with *Lode Runner Extra*. Wikipedia says it included a video introduction by Doug Smith.[^ref-2][^ref-16] MobyGames also lists the game among the titles in *Best of Sierra Nr. 4* (1998).[^ref-1]

### Fan Projects

*Mad Monks' Revenge: Definitive Edition* is a fan-made cross-platform re-creation of the 1995 follow-up. It reuses that game's graphics and gameplay and runs on Windows, macOS and Linux.[^ref-22]
