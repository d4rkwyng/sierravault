# EcoQuest: The Search for Cetus

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

**EcoQuest: The Search for Cetus** is an educational adventure game designed to teach about the importance of environmental ethics[^ref-1] and marine conservation[^ref-2]. Released by Sierra On-Line in 1991[^ref-3], the game casts the player as young Adam Greene, the only son of a biological researcher and ecologist[^ref-4]. Its intended audience is given differently by different sources: Wikipedia says it introduces "children ages 12 and up" to marine biology and marine conservation[^ref-2], while the box text reads "For ages 10 and up"[^ref-25].

Adventure Gamers' verdict called it "a rare example of well-done edutainment: it's a pleasure to play and does a great job in creating awareness of environmental issues"[^ref-4]. A 2023 MobyGames review by SAGA_ observed that "Sierra takes their years of adventure-game experience -- arguably some of the best in the business -- and applies its familiar formula to an educational game for the kids"[^ref-5]. Player reviewer Tomer Gabel called it "one of the best adventure games ever made"[^ref-5], while Mickey Gabel described "a fun, informative game, that manages to teach you without being didactic even a bit"[^ref-5].

EcoQuest launched Sierra's edutainment-oriented "Discovery Series" in 1991 as an adventure game aimed at a young audience[^ref-4]. The box carried the tagline "Adventure, Danger and Discovery Beneath the Sea"[^ref-25].

## Story Summary

The game follows Adam Greene, whose age is disputed between sources: MobyGames[^ref-23], Alex Bevilacqua[^ref-17], Collection Chamber[^ref-25] and a 1992 Game Bytes review[^ref-29] make him twelve, while Wikipedia[^ref-1] and The Sierra Chest[^ref-3] make him ten. A young dolphin named Delphineus set out to search for Cetus, the missing king of his land, and became entangled in an abandoned fishing net; Adam's father rescued him, and with his assistants and son nursed the dolphin back to health[^ref-11]. Adam's attempts to help the dolphin take a strange turn when it starts speaking[^ref-10].

As a qualified scuba diver, Adam is chosen to venture into Eluria, an underwater kingdom of animals, and help its inhabitants[^ref-12], who are threatened by human pollution. Cetus is the great sperm whale whom the undersea creatures look to for guidance, and he has gone missing[^ref-27]. Along the way the game confronts the player with the consequences of various human activities for marine life, in an attempt to reach a secret underwater kingdom[^ref-9].

The underwater realm of Eluria serves as both a fantastical setting and an allegory for real-world ocean ecosystems. Throughout his journey, Adam encounters marine creatures affected by human pollution, from swordfish and dolphins entangled in plastic six-pack rings to coral killed by tar and oil[^ref-27]. The game's villain, Flesh-Eater, is a manta ray that preys on Eluria's inhabitants[^ref-11], though the underlying message emphasizes human carelessness; co-designer Jane Jensen described the characters as "giving a voice to the faceless victims of our carelessness"[^ref-27].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

EcoQuest uses Sierra's point-and-click icon interface, with cursor actions for Walk/Swim, Look, Touch/Take, Talk, Item and a special "Recycle" action[^ref-14]. Once Adam picks up a recycling bag early in the game, the Recycle cursor lets him pick up trash and earn points[^ref-14]. The game runs on Sierra's Creative Interpreter (SCI)[^ref-20]; PCGamingWiki lists the engine as SCI1[^ref-8], while Alex Bevilacqua's 2024 review describes it as SCI1.1[^ref-17].

Compared to most other Sierra adventures, the difficulty is lower to suit younger players; it is impossible to "die" or get irrevocably stuck[^ref-23]. Jensen put it this way: "You can't die in the game, but other than that, it's a real Sierra adventure"[^ref-27]. HOTUD's MyAbandonware description says its educational elements are "ingeniously blended into gameplay"[^ref-13].

### Structure and Progression

The game has a maximum score of 725 points[^ref-19][^ref-20]. Points are awarded not only for solving puzzles but also for picking up trash with the Recycle cursor[^ref-14]. The README of the EQ1-CDR fan patch calls "the 256 color undersea kingdom of Eluria" "a sight to behold even by today's standards"[^ref-16].

The game opens with Adam helping his father clean an oil-coated seagull[^ref-11]. In the non-linear middle section, Adam solves a series of pollution problems for Eluria's inhabitants and marks a major chemical spill for cleanup[^ref-27]. Examples include freeing a sea turtle's throat from a swallowed balloon with tweezers[^ref-11] and devising a protective cage for a fishing boat's propeller so it stops injuring manatees and other ocean life[^ref-27].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

One IGN walkthrough sums it up as "an educational point and click adventure game by Sierra, with a large focus on saving the environment"[^ref-18]. Jensen described the puzzles as "traditional Sierra adventure-game puzzles, with an ecological and educational slant"[^ref-27], and the game includes a number of optional puzzles that score points but are not required to finish it[^ref-27].

Alex Bevilacqua found most of the game to be fetch quests and item-combination puzzles, with "no dead ends and no red herrings"; clicking the Help button on the sliding-tile puzzle makes the game solve it automatically[^ref-17].

The "Recycle" cursor puts the recycling symbol among Sierra's standard command icons, awarding bonus points for removing litter from the marine environment[^ref-2]. This folds the game's environmental lesson into play itself, rewarding players for cleaning up rather than lecturing them.

Game Bytes' Vic Witek wrote that "EcoQuest is very delightful in the way the plot unfolds" and that "The graphics (VGA) and animation are equal to or better than any release from Sierra", concluding "Recommended as part of your gaming library"[^ref-29]. PC Review was more guarded: "Like many adventure games, Eco Quest is a once-only program; when you have finished it you are unlikely to want to start over", adding that while the price "does not seem expensive for the variety and the quality of the graphics", it "does seem a lot for a game which you'll only want to play a limited number of times"[^ref-20].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective opinion is mixed. Alex Bevilacqua gave it 36% in 2024; he praised the artwork as excellent but concluded that "it's really not all that much fun to play", while recommending the CD version for its voice acting[^ref-17].

The Digital Antiquarian described EcoQuest as a "rather delightful educational adventure"[^ref-28], and praised the game's concessions to its young audience, "a lack of pointless deaths, a lack of unwinnable states", as simply good design[^ref-27].

Collection Chamber's retrospective review notes that "Adam does shuffle a little too slow" and that the need to pick up trash on almost every screen "gets a bit tedious too", yet adds that "there is a charm that exudes for each and every pixel"[^ref-25].

## Development

### Origins

According to the Digital Antiquarian, Gano Haine pitched EcoQuest when Sierra invited designers to propose ideas for its new Discovery line. The idea grew from a teenage memory of a beach she visited with children's summer camps, which she later found covered with litter and with sewage running into the ocean[^ref-27]. After the idea was accepted, Sierra assigned Jane Jensen to work with her as co-designer, making EcoQuest "the first computer game in history that was the product of an all-female design team"[^ref-27]. Bill Davis is credited as the game's Creative Director[^ref-30]; Wikipedia credits him with the original concept[^ref-1], but the Digital Antiquarian's more detailed account attributes the idea to Haine[^ref-27].

Jimmy Maher places the game alongside the wave of environmental media attention that followed the first international Earth Day on April 22, 1990[^ref-27].

### Production

Haine and Jensen worked with the Marine Mammal Center of Sausalito, California, to get the science right, and Sierra agreed to donate a portion of the profits to the organization[^ref-27]. The box promised that "extensive research and attention to detail create a realistic play environment"[^ref-25]. The game shipped with a booklet by Michael O'Brian, "I helped save the Earth – 55 fun ways kids can make a world of difference"[^ref-4].

Jane Jensen, who would later gain fame as the creator of the Gabriel Knight series, served as co-designer on EcoQuest alongside Gano Haine. It was an early Sierra project for her, which she had just finished when Sierra assigned her to King's Quest VI[^ref-27][^ref-28]. Game Bytes' May 1992 review listed the game as "Coming soon for Mac and Amiga"[^ref-29], but those ports were never published[^ref-1]; MobyGames lists only a DOS release[^ref-23].

One modern reviewer felt that a number of Eluria's characters seem inspired by Disney's "The Little Mermaid"[^ref-17].

### Technical Achievements

The game was built on Sierra's SCI engine[^ref-8], with 256-color graphics[^ref-16], and supports MCGA, EGA, Tandy/PCjr and VGA video modes[^ref-8]; the floppy release offered VGA and EGA versions separately[^ref-29]. A full-speech CD-ROM version followed in 1992[^ref-1]. Its credited voice cast includes Dylan Skirvin as Adam, Brittany Benov as Delphineus, Walt Danneman as Cetus and Josh Mandel as Hippocrates[^ref-30]; a MobyGames review notes that Adam was voiced by William Skirvin's son[^ref-5]. William D. Skirvin is credited as the game's director and art designer[^ref-30]. The music was composed by Chris Braymen[^ref-24][^ref-30], and Mark Seibert, Aubrey Hodges, Dan Kehler and Orpheus Hanley share the sound-effects credit with him[^ref-30].

The CD version removed the copy protection from the first scene[^ref-3]. Opinions differ on its graphics. One player review felt the CD release had "slightly better graphics in certain areas"[^ref-5]. The README of the EQ1-CDR fan patch disagrees, calling the CD artwork "objectivly worse compared to the original Floppy Disk version" because many graphics "were scaled-down or altered in undesirable ways"[^ref-16]. Running the CD version under DOSBox requires the game's RESOURCE.CFG to point to the correct path for speech to be heard[^ref-15].

### Copy Protection

The original floppy disk version asks for a four-digit security code[^ref-11], found in the included "Adam's Eco News" guide[^ref-10]. The later multimedia (CD) version removed this check[^ref-3]; the EQ1-CDR fan patch restores it[^ref-16].

## Trivia

- [[Jane Jensen]], who would later create the [[Gabriel Knight Series|Gabriel Knight series]], served as co-designer on EcoQuest alongside Gano Haine, just before co-designing King's Quest VI[^ref-27][^ref-28]
- The Digital Antiquarian calls it "the first computer game in history that was the product of an all-female design team"[^ref-27]
- The game includes Easter eggs referencing [[1990 - King's Quest V - Absence Makes the Heart Go Yonder|King's Quest V]] (on the office computer) and [[Space Quest Series|Space Quest]] (in a cave)[^ref-11], and even LeChuck from *Monkey Island*[^ref-5]
- Dylan Skirvin provided the voice of Adam Greene in the CD-ROM version[^ref-30]
- One modern reviewer felt that a number of the game's characters seem inspired by Disney's "The Little Mermaid"[^ref-17]
- The box advertised "a FREE book with 55 fun ways to help save our planet"[^ref-25]
- Planned Mac and Amiga versions were announced but never published[^ref-29][^ref-1]
- The game introduced the recycling symbol to Sierra's standard palette of command icons[^ref-2]
- EcoQuest has no player death and no way to get irrevocably stuck[^ref-23]
- The four-digit security code required players to reference the "Adam's Eco News" guide as a form of copy protection[^ref-11][^ref-10]
- A community restoration project (EQ1-CDR) released version 1.1 in February 2024 to fix graphics issues in the CD version[^ref-16]

## Legacy

Collection Chamber observes that "in the pantheon of Sierra classics, the EcoQuest series has been somewhat forgotten compared to King's Quest, Space Quest or Police Quest"[^ref-25]. The game inspired community restoration work, including a collaboration between Doomlazer and Threepwang to restore the CD version's graphics and fix its bugs[^ref-16].

EcoQuest spawned a sequel, Lost Secret of the Rainforest, released in 1993[^ref-1], in which Adam and his father head for the Amazon[^ref-27]. Jensen did not work on the sequel, and the series ended with it[^ref-27].

The game includes several Easter eggs, including references to King's Quest V[^ref-11], Space Quest[^ref-11], and even LeChuck from Monkey Island[^ref-5].

The EQ1-CDR restoration project released version 1.1 in February 2024[^ref-16], fixing bugs and restoring artwork from the floppy version in the CD version.

The Digital Antiquarian judges that "the science behind the two games still holds up", and records that Sierra received a letter from children in Finland who had played The Search for Cetus and been motivated to organize a cleanup at a polluted lake[^ref-27].
