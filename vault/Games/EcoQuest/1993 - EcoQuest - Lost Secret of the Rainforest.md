# EcoQuest 2: Lost Secret of the Rainforest

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

*EcoQuest 2: Lost Secret of the Rainforest* is an educational adventure game developed and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] in 1993 as the sequel to *EcoQuest: The Search for Cetus*.[^ref-1] While the official title screen displays simply "Lost Secret of the Rainforest" without the "EcoQuest 2" designation, the game is commonly referred to by both names.[^ref-2] Released as part of Sierra's Discovery Series—marketed with the promise "Your child will have fun AND your child will learn. GUARANTEED!"—the game shifts its environmental focus from the ocean depths of the original to the endangered rainforests of South America.[^ref-2]

The game stars Adam Greene, now slightly older and able to speak with animals as a matter of course,[^ref-1] as he journeys through lush jungle environments, mysterious caverns, and ancient ruins atop mist-covered peaks.[^ref-3] Players must navigate the real-life dangers threatening the rainforests while encountering endangered animal species, rare flora, and remote native cultures.[^ref-3] The educational content is woven throughout the gameplay via an innovative "Ecorder" device—a tricorder-like tool that Adam uses to learn about the things he encounters during his journey.[^ref-1]

Unlike its predecessor, which was co-designed by [[Jane Jensen]], *Lost Secret of the Rainforest* was designed solely by [[Gano Haine]], as Jensen had moved on to *King's Quest VI* and then to create the *Gabriel Knight* series.[^ref-33][^ref-1]

## Story Summary

Adam Greene and his father Noah, a doctor of ecology, travel to the town of Iquitos in Peru to meet with the Ecology Emergency Network.[^ref-6] Their mission is to establish contact with a native tribe known as the Grove People and help them set up a local economy using sustainably harvested natural resources, preventing the government from misusing their land.[^ref-2] Upon arrival at the airport, Adam must clear customs using his passport and immunization card—the latter having slipped inside his passport.[^ref-8]

At the docks, Adam encounters suspicious characters, including employees of a man named Maxim Slaughter who has bribed the Customs Official.[^ref-8] After some luggage goes missing and Adam's father becomes occupied with Nicanor from the Ecology Emergency Network, a pair of friendly river otters lead Adam on an unexpected journey deeper into the rainforest.[^ref-9] Adam is lured by a mysterious call from the heart of the jungle, where he discovers that the Forest Heart—a massive, magical tree that protects the Grove People—is dying.[^ref-10]

The Forest Heart reveals to Adam that she once had a sprout to replace her, but it did not survive, meaning another must be found somewhere.[^ref-10] Adam's quest takes him through the indigenous village, where he must help numerous villagers with their problems before gaining access to the shaman.[^ref-11] He assists a potter whose baby was stung by a bee, helps patch up a quarrel between a young man, Llusti, and the weaver Churana,[^ref-10] and gathers ingredients for a healing poultice.[^ref-11] The village contains a great hut where a storyteller tells tales to children, while weavers, hunters, and gardeners go about their daily lives.[^ref-10]

Adam's journey eventually leads him to be kidnapped by a goon working for Maxim Slaughter, who runs a camp filled with disturbing trophies including a jaguar-skin rug, stuffed gorilla hand, zebra-skin covered box, elephant foot stool, and a mounted rare dolphin.[^ref-12] With the help of Paquita, a very rare golden bat, he must escape from Slaughter's prison while avoiding a patrolling guard,[^ref-18] and continue his quest to find the lost City of Gold in pursuit of a remedy for the Grove People.[^ref-6] According to *Pelit*, the game places more emphasis on the dangers of selfishness and greed than its predecessor did.[^ref-1]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Like its predecessor, *Lost Secret of the Rainforest* uses a fully mouse-driven version of Sierra's SCI engine, in the manner of *Space Quest IV* and *King's Quest VI*.[^ref-13] The game features Sierra's classic icon interface with some modifications, providing intuitive point-and-click controls that allow players to examine, interact with, and talk to objects and characters.[^ref-14] The interface includes standard adventure game cursors for walking, looking, touching, and talking.

Two extra icons sit in the icon bar: the recycling bag carried over from the first game, and a bat-shaped icon that gives random trivia about the rainforest.[^ref-14] The Ecorder itself is not an icon but an inventory item, carried from the start, that Adam uses to record information about the flora and fauna of the areas he explores.[^ref-30][^ref-6] Players score points for using it, and the information it collects is what they are tested on near the end of the game.[^ref-30]

### Structure and Progression

The game progresses through several distinct areas, each presenting unique challenges and environments:

- **Iquitos:** The opening town where Adam clears customs, explores the docks, and witnesses suspicious activities before being separated from his father.[^ref-9]
- **Rainforest:** The lush jungle environment where Adam first encounters talking animals and begins following the call of the Forest Heart.[^ref-9]
- **Village:** The Grove People's settlement where Adam must help numerous villagers with their problems to gain the trust needed to meet with the shaman.[^ref-10]
- **Camp:** Maxim Slaughter's camp where Adam is held captive and must escape with Paquita's help.[^ref-12]
- **Cave:** Underground areas that Adam must navigate during his journey.[^ref-9]
- **Flooded Forest:** A waterlogged section of the rainforest presenting unique traversal challenges.[^ref-9]
- **City of Gold:** The ancient ruins where Adam must ultimately find the solution to save the Forest Heart and the Grove People.[^ref-9]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

According to *Compute!*, the game gives no instructions for solving its problems, so the player must explore and use logic and imagination; its examples include saving an egg from a nest fire and getting past a swarm of insects.[^ref-29] Many puzzles involve helping villagers—gathering ingredients for a poultice, catching butterflies with sweet drinks, and resolving interpersonal conflicts.[^ref-11] The game includes a copy protection puzzle near the Grove People section requiring players to reference the game manual to identify face paint patterns.[^ref-16]

Inventory management plays a significant role, with Adam collecting items like a clay cup, bark cup filled with sap, seed pods, and a special branch from the Forest Heart.[^ref-10][^ref-11] The camp escape sequence features timed elements where the goon will catch Adam if he's in the wrong place too long, requiring careful planning of movements.[^ref-17] Unlike the first game, these tightly timed sequences can be jarring and require repeated attempts.[^ref-17]

Like the first game, it does not let the player get irrevocably stuck or let Adam die.[^ref-6] According to *Pelit*, the puzzles are somewhat harder than the predecessor's.[^ref-1] The Adventurers' Guild reviewer also struggled with unclear and invisible exits in several places.[^ref-17]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Lisa Young's review in *Compute!* (November 1993) was positive overall. She wrote that "vibrant graphics and sounds" make the game "a highly entertaining product", and that children "should find this latest addition to the Sierra Discovery Series highly educational as well."[^ref-29] Her one caution concerned difficulty: "Although the game targets children age 10 and older, young users may find some of the problems frustrating; however, the thrill of solving a difficult problem is rewarding and may encourage them to continue."[^ref-29]

According to *Pelit* magazine, the game is somewhat harder than its predecessor and places more emphasis on the dangers of selfishness and greed, as opposed to the blight of man.[^ref-1]

### Modern Assessment

Andrea Morstabilini's review for Adventure Gamers (January 2010) gave the game 3 out of 5 stars, a "Decent" rating that the site's scale defines as "a qualified success; the positive aspects still outnumber the negative, but the weaknesses noticeably hinder the experience."[^ref-18] The review praised the "luxuriant, colorful graphics" and "varied and captivating musical score" with "some good, intelligent puzzles," while criticizing the "often uninspired" writing, "predictable plot without any real twist," and gameplay that "feels a bit stretched from time to time."[^ref-18] The verdict concluded: "Less enjoyable than its predecessor, EcoQuest 2 is still a good edutainment title for children and a fair choice for adventurers in search of a less demanding adventure."[^ref-18]

The Adventurers' Guild blog provided an extensive retrospective, noting ambivalence about the game despite wanting to like it: "A sequel nearly always struggles to live up to the bar set by its original. What carries over tends to constrain the sequel rather than letting it be its own thing."[^ref-17] The reviewer struggled with several arbitrary puzzles and found the timed camp sequence tedious, requiring multiple restarts.[^ref-17]

MobyGames user reviews are generally positive, with one calling it "a truly magnificent game which comes close to perfection" and "one of the best adventure games I have ever played."[^ref-19] Another reviewer praised the game's emotional approach to education, noting how the music contributes to atmosphere—shifting from disquieting when Adam first lands at the empty village to happy ethnic music when people return.[^ref-14]

## Development

### Origins

*Lost Secret of the Rainforest* belongs to the Discovery Series, Sierra's brief-lived educational line of the early 1990s, which also included two *Dr. Brain* puzzle games and the first *EcoQuest*.[^ref-34] *EcoQuest: The Search for Cetus*, released in early 1992, "did well enough" that Sierra funded a CD-ROM version and a further adventure for Adam, released in early 1993.[^ref-34] Sierra sought to continue the franchise with a new environmental focus, selecting the endangered rainforests of South America as the setting for the sequel.[^ref-2]

The game designer [[Gano Haine]] took sole creative control for the sequel, as co-designer Jane Jensen had moved on to *King's Quest VI* and then to create the *Gabriel Knight* series.[^ref-33][^ref-1] Haine was simultaneously working on the project while navigating Sierra's busy development environment, which included high-profile visitors like Daryl F. Gates, to whom she reluctantly provided orientation on Sierra's development tools.[^ref-21]

### Production

Development took place at Sierra On-Line's headquarters in Oakhurst, California, where Haine was working on the game in late 1992.[^ref-21] Peter Fisher is credited as producer, with Cheryl Sweeney credited under production.[^ref-31] Robert (Bob) Fischbach and [[Doug Oldfield]] led the programming, and Andy Hoyos and Arturo Sinclair were the art directors.[^ref-31] The game was built on Sierra's SCI1.1 engine, the same technology powering other contemporary Sierra adventures.[^ref-5]

The game's description on GameFAQs says it "is based on scientifically accurate information."[^ref-3] The box also held an "EcoNews" booklet with reading material, a coloring activity and a word search; it explains how to use the Ecorder, and a question asked when Adam first meets the Grove People can only be answered from it.[^ref-30]

### Technical Achievements

The game featured VGA graphics with beautiful, mostly realistic scenery depicting varied terrain from South American towns through rainforest environments, native villages with magical trees, remote camps, and ancient ruins.[^ref-14] The variety of locations made the game more visually diverse than its underwater predecessor.[^ref-14]

The music, composed by Dan Kehler, contributed significantly to the game's atmosphere.[^ref-22] The soundtrack dynamically shifted to reflect story moments—slightly disquieting when Adam senses he's being watched, sad when reaching the dying Forest Heart, and energetic ethnic music when the village comes alive.[^ref-14] The audio supported multiple sound configurations including General MIDI for enhanced quality.[^ref-19]

### Technical Issues

The Windows 3.x release does not function on 64-bit versions of Windows.[^ref-5] The DOS version requires DOSBox or similar emulator to run on modern Windows systems.[^ref-4] A "somewhat but not quite" copy protection system exists where players must create a facemask using patterns documented in the manual; this can theoretically be brute-forced, and the game directory contains all alternative solutions.[^ref-20]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Slaughter's Initials:** The pillow in Slaughter's camp is monogrammed "MAS"; his first name later turns out to be Maxim, but the middle initial is never explained ("No idea what the A stands for," the Adventurers' Guild reviewer notes).[^ref-12]
- **Trophy Room:** Slaughter's bedroom contains numerous grotesque animal trophies including bunny slippers made from real bunnies, reflecting the game's anti-poaching message through dark humor.[^ref-12]
- **Forest Heart Comparison:** The magical Forest Heart tree bears resemblance to another giant tree encountered in *Quest for Glory 3*, suggesting some idea-sharing between Sierra development teams.[^ref-14]
- **Point System:** The game awards extra points for thorough exploration and talking to everyone, though the game can advance without collecting all points.[^ref-8]

## Voice Cast

The game has no full voice acting. The floppy release contains only a couple of spoken lines for a few characters; Adam, Noah and the animals are not voiced.[^ref-30] The DOS credits list four people under Acting / Voiceovers: Richard Aronson, Neal Grandstaff, Trisha Korte and Greg Tomko-Pavia.[^ref-31]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

While specific sales figures are not documented in available sources, *Lost Secret of the Rainforest* has maintained a dedicated following among adventure game enthusiasts and those who played it during childhood.[^ref-24]

### Collections

The game has appeared on various community wishlists for digital re-release, with GOG.com's Dreamlist showing 403 votes requesting the title.[^ref-24] As of the research date, the game has not received an official digital re-release through major platforms like GOG or Steam.

### Fan Projects

Multiple fan-created walkthroughs and guides have been produced over the years, including comprehensive FAQ/Walkthroughs hosted on GameFAQs by authors Tom Hayes and odino.[^ref-9][^ref-25] The Let's Play Archive features a complete playthrough with commentary.[^ref-16] The game is supported by ScummVM, which enables cloud save functionality on third-party storage services.[^ref-5]

### Related Publications

- **Game Manual:** Included with retail release, contains copy protection answers for face paint puzzle[^ref-16]
- **EcoNews Booklet:** Supplementary educational material with a coloring activity and a word search, included in the box; also needed to answer an in-game question[^ref-30]

### Critical Perspective

*EcoQuest 2: Lost Secret of the Rainforest* continued Sierra's environmental education series in a new setting, but reviewers have found it short of its predecessor: Adventure Gamers called it "less enjoyable than its predecessor",[^ref-18] and Jimmy Maher wrote that "it just doesn't all come together as well as it did the first time around."[^ref-34]

It was the last *EcoQuest* game. The Discovery Series itself ended in 1993, when Sierra acquired Bright Star Technology as its educational division and retired the line's other series and characters apart from Dr. Brain.[^ref-34]
