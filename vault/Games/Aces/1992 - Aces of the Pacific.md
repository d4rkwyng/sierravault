# Aces of the Pacific

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

Aces of the Pacific is a World War II combat flight simulator developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line in 1992[^ref-1]. Directed and designed by Damon Slye[^ref-5], the game transports players to the Pacific Theater of World War II, where they can experience aerial combat from December 7, 1941, through the end of the war[^ref-2]. The game allows players to fly for either the Allied or Japanese forces, featuring over 25 meticulously researched vintage aircraft including the A6M Zero, F4U Corsair, F6F Hellcat, and P-47 Thunderbolt[^ref-2].

Set against the backdrop of Pearl Harbor's aftermath, Aces of the Pacific spans the entire Pacific theater with "mighty 'floating airfields' carrying the battle out across the seas"[^ref-1]. Players can engage with legendary aces such as Greg "Pappy" Boyington, Hiroyoshi Nishizawa, and America's highest-ranking ace Richard Bong[^ref-2][^ref-12]. The game was a commercial hit, selling 350,000 units according to Retro Gamer[^ref-12], and its success led Dynamix to create the very similar follow-up Aces Over Europe in 1993[^ref-3].

## Story Summary

Aces of the Pacific places players in the cockpit during one of history's most pivotal aerial conflicts. Beginning with Pearl Harbor in smoking ruin on December 7, 1941, "aerial history would continue and new legends would be born as American pilots launched into battle against the Japanese Empire"[^ref-1]. The game covers the complete Pacific War timeline from 1941-1945, allowing players to participate in both historical battles and create their own character to survive the brutal Pacific Theater of Operations[^ref-3].

The narrative encompasses major Pacific engagements, with one of the most memorable missions being the shoot-down of Admiral Yamamoto[^ref-3]. Players can take a career path in the U.S. Army Air Forces, Navy or Marines, or with the Imperial Japanese Army or Navy[^ref-12], launching ground attacks, intercepting bombers and dogfighting enemy aces[^ref-2]. The WWII: 1946 expansion disk extends the timeline into alternate history scenarios based on extensive research of historical and never-realized Allied war plans and documents[^ref-2].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Aces of the Pacific offers comprehensive control options supporting keyboard, mouse, joystick, and rudder pedals[^ref-8]. The game features multiple control schemes with general commands including F10 for the Preference Panel, ALT-R for the Realism Panel, and ALT-J for joystick toggle[^ref-8]. Flight controls utilize keys 1-9 or +/- for throttle management, with joystick and rudder pedal support for authentic flight simulation[^ref-8].

The viewpoint system provides F1-F5 cockpit viewing angles and F1-F7 external aircraft views, with right mouse button support for view panning[^ref-8]. Weapons systems are controlled via SPACEBAR or Joystick Button 1 for guns, and BACKSPACE for bomb deployment[^ref-8]. The interface accommodates both arcade and realistic difficulty settings, with the realistic mode described as "outright brutal"[^ref-3].

### Structure and Progression

The game offers both historical reenactments and career mode progression where players create their own pilot character[^ref-3]. Mission types include ground attacks, bomber interceptions, and dogfighting scenarios across the vast Pacific theater[^ref-2]. Players can also choose a single or instant mission instead of a full career[^ref-3].

The expansion pack WWII: 1946 introduces Operation Coronet, the planned U.S. invasion of Japan, featuring experimental jets and prototype aircraft that might have been used in extended Pacific operations[^ref-9]. This expansion is "premised on what might have happened in the pacific theater if WWII had not ended in 1945" and is "based on research of the allied war plans and documents"[^ref-10].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The Pacific setting is built around aircraft carriers, the "mighty 'floating airfields' carrying the battle out across the seas"[^ref-1]. Historical missions include the attack on Pearl Harbor, the Battle of Midway, the Battle of the Coral Sea, the Battle of Leyte Gulf and the interception of Admiral Yamamoto[^ref-12].

Realism is adjustable through the Realism Panel (ALT-R)[^ref-8]; one player recalls that "Arcade was a blast, and realistic could be outright brutal"[^ref-3]. The game runs on Dynamix's 3-Space engine[^ref-23][^ref-5], requiring minimum specifications of an Intel i386 SX processor, 2MB RAM, and VGA graphics[^ref-7].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Aces of the Pacific received widespread critical acclaim from gaming publications. Dragon magazine awarded the game a perfect 5 out of 5 stars in their December 1992 review, with reviewers Hartley, Patricia and Kirk Lesser praising the simulation[^ref-9]. Computer Gaming World initially found the game "simultaneously awesome and disappointing" in their September 1992 review, but after updates, Doug Fick declared that "as updated, Aces of the Pacific lives up to its tremendous advanced billing and is now superior to Red Baron"[^ref-12].

### Modern Assessment

The game's lasting impact is evidenced by its inclusion in multiple "best of" lists over the decades. PC Gamer US ranked Aces of the Pacific as the 12th best computer game ever in 1994, stating "With its unsurpassed variety of aircraft and mission types, Aces of the Pacific may well be the most gratifying air-combat simulation ever made"[^ref-12]. Computer Gaming World later ranked it as the 47th best game of all time in 1996[^ref-12].

IGN's 2003 retrospective ranked the game 92nd among top games, noting "Realism, aircraft, technology, multiplayer and many ways to kill many a folk made Aces of the Pacific an immediate hit. (...) Better flight sims have come and gone, but this was one of the first truly glorious ones and its brand is still burnt in our minds"[^ref-12]. Modern gaming communities continue to request the title for digital distribution, with active wishlists on platforms like GOG demonstrating ongoing interest[^ref-3].

## Development

### Origins

Aces of the Pacific was developed by Dynamix, with Damon Slye credited as both Director and Lead Designer[^ref-5]. The credits list Christopher Shen as Assistant to the Director, John Bruning Sr. on historical research, David McClurg on the 3 Space modules, and Jan Paul Moorhead for the original music score[^ref-5]. Sierra's own promotional text described the game's more than 25 vintage aircraft as "meticulously researched"[^ref-2].

### Production

After the initial 1992 release, Dynamix significantly improved the game's performance, opponent AI, sound and aircraft realism, as Computer Gaming World reported in December 1992[^ref-12]. The game shipped on 5.25" and 3.5" floppy disks[^ref-15], followed by a 1994 CD-ROM release[^ref-29]. An Amiga version was previewed but ultimately not released[^ref-12].

Technical specifications required an Intel i386 SX processor minimum, PC/MS-DOS 5.0, and 2MB RAM, with support for various sound devices including Sound Blaster, Roland MT-32, and Adlib[^ref-7]. The game supported multiple input devices including keyboard, mouse, analog and digital joysticks, and specialized flight controllers like the Thrustmaster FCS[^ref-7].

### Technical Achievements

The game was built on Dynamix's 3-Space engine[^ref-23][^ref-5]. Technical features included VGA graphics support and compatibility with multiple sound devices[^ref-7]. In 1996 Computer Gaming World credited it with setting "new standards for graphics and performance"[^ref-12].

Version 1.2 included significant upgrades and all-new features, while the WWII: 1946 expansion pack introduced experimental aircraft and extended campaign scenarios[^ref-16]. Manuals for both the game and the WWII: 1946 expansion are archived at Sierra Help[^ref-17].

## Legacy

The game's success caused Dynamix to create the very similar follow-up Aces Over Europe in 1993[^ref-3].

Players' memories on its GOG Dreamlist page recall reenacting historical battles and the mission to shoot down Yamamoto[^ref-3]. Community-created content continues to be preserved and distributed through archive sites, demonstrating the game's lasting appeal among flight simulation enthusiasts[^ref-18]. The game also faced some technical challenges with DOSBox compatibility, experiencing input control issues similar to other period flight simulators when run on modern systems[^ref-19]. Sierra Help offers a DOSBox installer that works with both the CD and floppy versions[^ref-26].
