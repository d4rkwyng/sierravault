# PGA Championship Golf 1999 Edition

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

PGA Championship Golf 1999 Edition is a golf simulation for Windows, developed by [[Headgate Studios]] and published by Sierra Sports, the sports label of [[Sierra On-Line]].[^ref-1][^ref-6] Sierra presented it as the sequel to [[1997 - Front Page Sports - Golf|Front Page Sports: Golf]].[^ref-2][^ref-3] Sierra Sports announced the game in July 1998, together with an agreement with PGA Interactive, for an October 1998 release at a suggested price of $29.95.[^ref-4] It slipped into the following year: Sierra announced on May 20, 1999 that it had gone gold, and said it would reach North American shelves by June 1, 1999.[^ref-2][^ref-3]

The game's main selling point was TrueSwing, a real-time mouse swing in which the on-screen golfer's club moves with the player's mouse. The 1999 Edition enhanced it and added a horizontal mode alongside the vertical one, while also offering a traditional "TriClick" swing.[^ref-2][^ref-9][^ref-13] It shipped with eight courses, seven of them real championship courses, plus Jocassee Shoals, a fantasy course built with the game's own Course Architect editor.[^ref-1][^ref-9] It also introduced ReadyPlay, which let every golfer in a group play at their own pace instead of waiting for turns.[^ref-7][^ref-8]

Critics were generally positive, with an aggregate of about 80% on GameRankings.[^ref-16] Reviewers praised TrueSwing and the Course Architect, and criticized the commentary, the graphics and a weak offline tournament mode.[^ref-5][^ref-7][^ref-14] MobyGames also lists a 1998 UK release under the title "PGA Championship Golf" (also known as "PGA Championship Golf '99"), which it treats as the previous entry in the series.[^ref-22]

## Story Summary

PGA Championship Golf 1999 Edition is a sports simulation and has no story. Players create and customize a golfer and play rounds, matches and tournaments on the bundled courses or on courses made with the Course Architect.[^ref-1][^ref-13] Tom Lehman is the only real PGA player in the game.[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

TrueSwing treats the mouse as the club head: the player pulls the mouse back to start the backswing and pushes it forward to hit the ball. The speed and angle of that push decide the shot.[^ref-13] The 1999 Edition lets players swing vertically or horizontally, and it keeps a traditional three-click ("TriClick") power-bar swing as an option.[^ref-2][^ref-9][^ref-13] The game has no aiming marker. Players rotate the golfer to aim, and a shot menu offers fades, draws, punch shots and flop shots. There are also two custom shot slots, which change the club face, the ball position and the swing plane.[^ref-9] A caddie can select clubs for the player, and the camera views can be adjusted extensively.[^ref-13][^ref-15]

### Structure and Progression

The game offers twelve types of play, including medal (stroke), match, best ball, skins, scramble and Stableford.[^ref-1][^ref-9] IGN also listed Ryder Cup, Greensome and Bloodsome formats, and Tournament, Recreation and Practice modes. Mulligans, gimmes and pickups are optional.[^ref-13] Offline tournaments are available, but GameSpot noted there is "no built-in support for a tour," and IGN that "there's no season mode."[^ref-9][^ref-13]

The eight courses are:[^ref-1]
- Sahalee Country Club (Washington)
- Royal Birkdale Golf Club (England)
- Black Diamond Ranch (Florida)
- Coeur d'Alene (Idaho)
- Pasatiempo Golf Club (California)
- Pete Dye Golf Club (West Virginia)
- the Prince Course at Princeville Resort (Hawaii)
- Jocassee Shoals, a fantasy course

GameSpot noted that five of the real courses were carried over from Sierra's earlier golf game, and that Pasatiempo and Royal Birkdale were new.[^ref-9] Each real course opens with a short narrated introductory film.[^ref-8][^ref-13]

Multiplayer supports hot seat, LAN and modem play. Internet play goes only through WON.net, Sierra's online gaming service, with no direct TCP/IP support.[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-5][^ref-9] On WON.net, the Sierra Sports Golf Association (SGA) ran organized online tours and tournaments.[^ref-17][^ref-18]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

ReadyPlay lets each golfer play through a hole without waiting for the others. It works offline against computer players as well as online.[^ref-9][^ref-10] Computer opponents can be tuned for distance, accuracy and other abilities, and can be set to "learn" as they play.[^ref-13] Course conditions such as green firmness, wind and rough length can be set before each round.[^ref-13][^ref-15]

The Course Architect lets players build their own courses. A course wizard guides beginners through the process.[^ref-9] Unlike the Jack Nicklaus designer, it lays out a whole plot of land instead of designing each hole separately.[^ref-10] GameSpot found one limit that contradicted the manual: holes cannot cross water at all.[^ref-9]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score |
|---|---|
| PC Gamer (US) | 90%[^ref-10] |
| GameSpot | 8.5/10[^ref-9] |
| Hartford Courant | 8.2/10[^ref-14] |
| Eurogamer | 8/10[^ref-15] |
| IGN | 7.6/10[^ref-13] |
| CNET Gamecenter | 7/10[^ref-8] |
| PC Gamer (UK) | 73%[^ref-11] |
| Computer Games Strategy Plus | 3.5/5[^ref-5] |
| GameRankings (aggregate) | 79.81% (16 reviews)[^ref-16] |
| MobyGames critics (aggregate) | 78% (12 ratings)[^ref-1] |

Most of the praise went to TrueSwing. William Abner of *Computer Games Strategy Plus* wrote that "PGA Championship Golf is an enigma. It has the best swing method in the industry, a powerful course designer, addictive gameplay, and yet it feels unfinished."[^ref-5] Paul Rosano of the *Hartford Courant* wrote that "the feel of the mouse swing is truly incredible, way ahead of the competition."[^ref-14] AllGame's Michael L. House said the game "achieves what I had begun to think of as the unattainable Holy Grail of golf simulation."[^ref-7] Stephen Poole of GameSpot called it "the best implementation of this swing method I've tried."[^ref-9]

ReadyPlay was also well received. House called it an "enormous innovation."[^ref-7] Gordon Goble of CNET Gamecenter said "it's the latter innovation that really gets things cooking."[^ref-8] Abner was more mixed, calling ReadyPlay "both a blessing and a curse" because opponents sometimes stood in the line of a shot.[^ref-5]

The commentary by Mark Lye and Grant Boone drew the most criticism. Several reviewers complained that the announcers revealed where a ball would land as soon as it was hit. Abner wrote that this "kills any tension," and Poole noted that "these guys know almost immediately where the ball will wind up."[^ref-5][^ref-9] House called the commentary "the weakest and most annoying part of the game."[^ref-7] Eurogamer disagreed, calling it "commendable."[^ref-15]

Several critics found the graphics behind *Links* and *Jack Nicklaus*.[^ref-5][^ref-9][^ref-14] Goble described "a dull, almost drab look."[^ref-8] PC Gamer UK's Chris Buxton criticized "the awful-looking spectators" and stuttering golfer animation.[^ref-11] IGN, by contrast, felt that the 2D spectators "look very, very real."[^ref-13] Abner called offline tournament play "flat out broken": opponents finished rounds out of sequence, and the tournament ended without a report.[^ref-5] Both IGN and the *Hartford Courant* reported crashes in the Course Architect. Rosano noted that after a recent patch "the architect has repeatedly crashed at the beginning of a new session."[^ref-13][^ref-14]

Other reviewers were warmer. Rob Smolka of *PC Gamer* (US) wrote that the game "may have the best physics model of any golf game."[^ref-10] Eurogamer reviewed it in August 2000 as a budget re-release and preferred it to the *Links LS* series.[^ref-15]

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames gives the game a Moby Score of 7.5 and a critics average of 78% from 12 ratings.[^ref-1]

## Development

### Origins

Sierra Sports announced the game on July 13, 1998, as part of an agreement with PGA Interactive, which *Computer Games Strategy Plus* described as "a long-term alliance between PGA of America and IBM." The announcement promised seven courses, including Sahalee Country Club, site of the 80th PGA Championship the following month. It also promised 3D polygon golfers of up to 20,000 polygons, a new mouse-based swing, and Internet play on the World Opponent Network.[^ref-4]

### Production

AllGame's credits list Vance Cook as designer, director and software engineer, Mike Jacob as producer, and Kari St. John as art director. Chuck Barth is credited with music and sound effects.[^ref-6] The game was developed by Headgate Studios.[^ref-6][^ref-1] Brian Silvernail appears among nine Course Architect design consultants, not as a lead designer.[^ref-6] The manual was written by Nathan Hall. Mike Jacob and Mark Lye wrote the commentary script, and golfer motions were motion-captured.[^ref-6]

The game was scheduled for October 1998 and slipped to 1999.[^ref-4] Sierra announced that it had gone gold on May 20, 1999.[^ref-2] On May 28, Sierra said it would reach North American retail by June 1.[^ref-3] Sources give slightly different release dates:
- MobyGames: June 1, 1999[^ref-1]
- A June 1999 *Computer Games Strategy Plus* news item: May 27, 1999[^ref-18]
- GameRankings: May 31, 1999[^ref-16]

### Voice Cast

The game's tournament commentary was provided by The Golf Channel's Mark Lye and PGA Tour Radio's Grant Boone.[^ref-1][^ref-9] GameSpot counted "over 1400 speech clips."[^ref-9]

### Technical Achievements

The 1999 Edition's TrueSwing was a reworked version of the system Sierra introduced in its earlier golf game, now with vertical and horizontal modes.[^ref-9][^ref-10] The golfers were built from more than 20,000 polygons, according to GamePro.[^ref-12] *Computer Games Strategy Plus* listed these requirements:[^ref-5]
- Windows 95/98
- 200 MHz processor
- 32 MB RAM
- 8x CD-ROM

Eurogamer's listing for the budget re-release gives a lower minimum: a Pentium 133, 32 MB RAM, 80 MB of disk space and a 4x CD-ROM.[^ref-15] The Internet Archive copy of the game has two CD images, one for installation and one for courses.[^ref-23]

## Legacy

Players kept adding to the game after release. By late July 1999, Sierra reported that more than 35 user-made Course Architect courses had been posted on WON.net, adding to the eight that shipped with the game. A new Library Creator tool let course designers import their own graphics, textures and sounds.[^ref-19] In late June 1999, WON.net opened SGA 1999 Season II, with official tournaments starting July 20. It had three tiers of competition: an Amateur Tour, the PGA Championship Golf Tour and the SGA Pro Tour.[^ref-18]

The game was followed in 2000 by [[2000 - PGA Championship Golf 2000|PGA Championship Golf 2000]].[^ref-1][^ref-21] It was later re-released on Sierra's Sierra Originals budget label. Eurogamer, reviewing that re-release, described it as "one of the top selling sports games of its day across the Atlantic."[^ref-15]
