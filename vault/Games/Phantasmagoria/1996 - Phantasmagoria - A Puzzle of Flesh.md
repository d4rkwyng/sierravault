# Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh is a psychological horror adventure game from Sierra On-Line, on sale in North American stores from November 26, 1996[^ref-1][^ref-66]. It carries the name of Roberta Williams' 1995 game Phantasmagoria, but the Sierra Chest notes that it "has no connection whatsoever" with that game[^ref-13]; the Los Angeles Times called it "the second installment in Sierra On-Line's horror anthology"[^ref-4]. Its hero is Curtis Craig, a young technical writer[^ref-39] who starts to fear for his own sanity as terrifying, unexplained events and then murders gather around him[^ref-3].

Developed on a budget of $4.5 million with a cast of more than 30 professional actors[^ref-4], A Puzzle of Flesh was one of Sierra's full-motion video productions. The game was filmed over about six months, with actors performing on movie sets built in and around Seattle, Washington, specifically for the production rather than on blue screen[^ref-4]; the alien world, however, was computer-rendered, with the live video overlaid by chromakey[^ref-63]. It sold less well than the first game: the Sierra Chest cites the sequel's "lower sales"[^ref-73], and Wikipedia calls it a "commercial disappointment"[^ref-74]. Its graphic violence and sex drew censorship abroad, and designer Lorelei Shannon later recalled: "Some governments banned the game altogether, believe it or not."[^ref-8]

## Story Summary

Sierra's 1996 synopsis introduces Curtis Craig as a reserved young man who works for the Wyntech Corporation, has a girlfriend named Jocilyn, and left a mental hospital exactly a year earlier[^ref-10]. His ordinary life comes apart as inexplicable things happen around him; in Sierra's words, "Photographs bleed. Rats speak. His computer develops a vicious mind of its own."[^ref-11] Curtis comes to question whether he is sane at all, and then the killings start[^ref-12].

In the Sierra Chest's summary, Curtis follows "a trail of hallucinations, blood, sex and murder victims" and learns that "he is not who he always thought he was"[^ref-13]. The title "Puzzle of Flesh" refers to the puzzle of who Curtis really is, and the fact that his flesh is not his own[^ref-14]. Throughout his journey, Curtis encounters various characters including his therapist Dr. Harburg, his co-worker Trevor Barnes, and the mysterious entity known as the Hecatomb[^ref-15]. The game explores themes of identity, mental illness, sexuality, and supernatural horror as Curtis unravels the truth about his past and confronts the forces seeking to control him[^ref-16].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh uses Sierra's traditional point-and-click adventure interface with a cursor that changes over hot spots[^ref-17]. Anything Curtis can interact with lights up under the cursor, and a click on it plays a live-action clip that advances the story[^ref-18]. The game alternates between two modes: interactive sequences with video sprites on filmed backgrounds, and non-interactive full-motion video sequences that advance the narrative[^ref-19].

The game follows Sierra's traditional chapter-by-chapter structure, divided into five chapters[^ref-20]. Estimates of its length vary widely: Adventure Gamers said a seasoned player could finish it in "less than six or seven hours"[^ref-26], Game Revolution put it at 10-15 hours[^ref-39], and Adventure Classic Gaming at 15-20 hours[^ref-20]. Locations Curtis visits include his apartment, the WynTech offices, Dr. Harburg's office, the Dreaming Tree Restaurant and the Borderline[^ref-21]. The interface emphasizes cinematic storytelling over complex puzzle-solving, with the game functioning more like an interactive movie than a traditional adventure game[^ref-22].

### Structure and Progression

The game spans five chapters[^ref-23]. Players progress through the story by triggering specific plot events and completing mostly simple puzzles[^ref-33], though Adventure Classic Gaming found some of them, such as the alien circuit board puzzle, "simply illogical"[^ref-24]. The therapy sessions with Dr. Harburg serve as a unique gameplay element where inventory objects are used to bring up discussion topics, providing backstory and character development[^ref-25].

Much of the game's running time is spent watching video rather than playing; Adventure Gamers noted that "a good share" of its length "will be spent watching the videos rather than actually playing"[^ref-26]. The game includes multiple possible endings based on player choices, though the overall story arc remains largely fixed[^ref-27]. Easter eggs are scattered throughout the game, with a total of 480 possible Easter Egg points that can be discovered through exploration[^ref-28].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzle design in A Puzzle of Flesh received significant criticism from reviewers, with many describing the challenges as illogical or frustrating[^ref-29]. One reviewer specifically cited "one of the most irrational puzzles I've ever seen" involving Curtis's wallet being found under his sofa[^ref-30]. The game prioritizes cinematic presentation over traditional adventure game mechanics, leading to complaints that it felt more like watching a movie than playing a game[^ref-31].

The inventory is kept along the bottom of the screen, and Adventure Gamers found its inventory puzzles "just as simple and painless" as the rest of the game, with the answer "always the most obvious"[^ref-32]. Game Revolution called most puzzles "straight-forward password-key-inventory puzzles", the exception being a final mechanical puzzle "whose elements aren't all highlighted by cursor sweeping"[^ref-33]. Adventure Gamers summed the game up as "very easy and never really challenging"[^ref-34].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh received largely negative reviews from critics. In a review published in 2000, GameSpot gave the game a harsh 3.7/10, with reviewer Ron Dulin stating, "As the credits rolled, all I felt was regret—regret that I had spent a good chunk of my life in this ugly world, with annoying, unappealing characters and their silly problems"[^ref-35]. The review particularly criticized the story quality, noting that "technical achievements don't amount to much when it seems like the story was written for the Halloween issue of a junior high school newspaper"[^ref-36].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been more appreciative of the game's progressive elements and historical significance. GLAAD commemorated the game's 25th anniversary in 2021, noting that "Curtis is canonically and identifiably bisexual in the game, making him arguably the first playable LGBTQ protagonist in gaming history (among commercial games, at least)"[^ref-42]. The organization praised the game's "remarkably progressive" LGBTQ representation for its time period[^ref-43].

Adventure Gamers gave a more mixed retrospective assessment in 2008, giving the game 2.5 stars; reviewer Andrea Morstabilini concluded, "If you can overcome the substantial lack of actual gameplay and you're a fan of raunchy horror movies, this game will be a lot a fun. If not, this isn't the game for you"[^ref-44]. Bloody Disgusting published a 20-year retrospective calling the game "wildly ahead of its time"; in the same piece, star Paul Morgan Stetler said the game had explored BDSM and bisexuality "without judging them"[^ref-45]. User reviews on platforms like MobyGames range from highly positive to extremely negative, with some players praising the acting quality and mature themes while others criticize the limited gameplay and questionable story choices[^ref-46].

### Regional Censorship and Controversy

The game's explicit content led to bans and censorship in several countries. It was banned in Singapore, and Wikipedia describes it as heavily censored in Australia[^ref-77]. The Sierra Chest lists it as banned in both Singapore and Australia and says it was released in "a whole slew of different censored versions"[^ref-53]. The Australian classification record says it was never banned there: classification took three months, and in February 1997 a censored version was passed with an MA rating[^ref-79].

In Germany, German Wikipedia records that the German and American versions were released uncut with a USK rating of "not released to persons under 18", yet both were indexed by the Bundesprüfstelle für jugendgefährdende Schriften; it gives the indexing as running from March 1998 to February 2023, and says the British version, from which some video scenes had been cut, was spared[^ref-83].

## Development

### Origins

The sequel was not a continuation of the first game's story[^ref-13]. Roberta Williams stepped out of the project and passed development responsibilities to Lorelei Shannon, who had previously co-designed King's Quest VII: The Princeless Bride[^ref-48]. In Shannon's own account, Williams "wasn't particularly interested in spearheading the franchise," and "a number of different designers wrote proposals for a potential Phantasmagoria II"; hers "was more urban horror, as a counterpoint to the original," and she was given the project[^ref-80]. In a 1997 interview, asked whether she was the first person asked to design the game or whether someone else had been considered before her, she answered: "I was the first."[^ref-84] Shannon drew inspiration from authors Edgar Allan Poe, Charles Beaumont, and Shirley Jackson, as well as visual influences from photographer Joel-Peter Witkin and the film Seven[^ref-49]. She also cited fondness for the psychological horror film Jacob's Ladder and admiration for Clive Barker's work[^ref-50].

The project was produced as the second installment of Sierra's horror anthology, at a time when game producers were increasingly hiring professional screen actors for CD-ROM titles[^ref-4]. The Sierra Chest describes the result as "Sierra's most explicit game", with blood and gore, nudity, sex and strong language[^ref-53]; its subject matter includes child abuse, mental illness, sadomasochism and bisexuality[^ref-71].

### Production

The game's own map is a map of Bellevue, Washington; according to the Sierra Chest, a hidden Easter egg sits on the spot "where Sierra On-Line had its headquarters and where the Phantasmagoria development team was located"[^ref-28]. Filming took place in and around Seattle[^ref-4]. Wikipedia dates it from February to September 1996[^ref-54], while the Los Angeles Times reported "about six months in filming"[^ref-4] and Shannon recalled that "the shoot was going to last six months"[^ref-8]. The production was directed by [[Andy Hoyos]], working with a crew of young USC film school graduates[^ref-55]; [[Tammy Dargan]] is credited as project manager[^ref-81]. The game was shot on Digital Betacam, producing nearly four and a half hours of video; Wikipedia describes the script as over 200 pages long[^ref-56]. Other accounts differ: Paul Morgan Stetler recalled being sent the entire script, "something like 350 pages"[^ref-58], and Shannon said in 1997 that the script was originally five hundred pages and that "What we shot was a little over two hundred pages."[^ref-84] Unlike the first Phantasmagoria, A Puzzle of Flesh was filmed with actors on high-quality movie sets built for the production rather than against blue screen[^ref-4], although the alien-world scenes were still computer-rendered with video overlaid by chromakey[^ref-63].

Sources date the release differently. CNET Gamecenter reported the game just released on November 26, 1996, and Wikipedia and PCGamingWiki also give November 26, 1996 for North America[^ref-66][^ref-1][^ref-62]; GameSpot and the Sierra Chest give November 30, 1996[^ref-35][^ref-13]; and GOG's store page gives December 3, 1996[^ref-82].

The casting process proved challenging, with the main actor Paul Morgan Stetler being cast very late after the original Los Angeles-based actor dropped out over a contract dispute[^ref-58]. The production employed Screen Actors Guild talent, with actors receiving a minimum of $522 per day for interactive media projects[^ref-4]. The game featured a cast of more than 30 professional actors[^ref-4], including Ragna Sigrunardottir as Therese, Monique Parent as Jocilyn, and Paul Mitri as Trevor[^ref-60]. The music was composed by Gary Spinrad, whose "haunting score" GameSpot's reviewer felt was wasted on the story[^ref-61].

The Los Angeles Times called Tammy Dargan the game's producer[^ref-4], while MobyGames credits her as Project Manager[^ref-81]. ### Technical Achievements

The sources label the engine differently. The Sierra Chest says the game uses "a rewritten version of Sierra's SCI32 game engine" to support the Duck movie format[^ref-65], while PCGamingWiki lists the engine as SCI3[^ref-62]. The game utilized TrueMotion video compression to provide near 30 frames per second video playback[^ref-63]. Rather than blurring violent or sexual content, the developers shot alternate clips for the password-protected parental lockout setting[^ref-64]. Separate retail versions were still produced: Sam's and Wal-Mart carried only a PG-13 version, and a special version for Best Buy in Texas removed the phrase "pig in puke" from the box[^ref-64].

The video files were stored as DUK (Duck) movies, played in 16-bit color under Windows; under DOS, CLU files converted them to 8-bit color[^ref-65]. The extensive full-motion video meant the game shipped on five CD-ROMs, according to CNET Gamecenter's 1996 report, Wikipedia and German Wikipedia[^ref-66][^ref-1][^ref-83]; the Los Angeles Times article of November 30, 1996 instead calls it a "seven-CD-ROM title"[^ref-4]. Asked in 1997 about reports that it was originally to ship on seven CDs like its predecessor, Shannon said: "There was no plan to ship on 7 CDs, but we cut a lot of stuff due to time and budgetary restrictions anyway."[^ref-84] Published system requirements differ between sources: Computer Gaming World's review listed a 486DX-66, 8MB RAM and a 2x CD-ROM drive[^ref-78], while MobyGames lists a Pentium, 12MB RAM and a 4x CD-ROM drive[^ref-67]. In 2014, a GOG forum thread asked for the Windows Enhanced version to be added, and a reply from GOG quoted in the thread said technical issues had prevented the Windows version from working and that only the DOS version was reliable enough to be released[^ref-68]. As of November 2026, PCGamingWiki lists the GOG release as pre-packaged with ScummVM, with a DOSBox version also available through GOG Galaxy, and says the Steam release uses DOSBox[^ref-62]; GOG's store page states that the game is powered by ScummVM[^ref-82]. Sierra's website in December 1996 listed a Macintosh CD as "available mid-February 1997"[^ref-69], but MobyGames records only DOS and Windows releases[^ref-9].

## Legacy

Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh holds historical significance as one of the first commercial video games to feature a canonically bisexual protagonist, with GLAAD calling Curtis Craig "arguably the first playable LGBTQ protagonist in gaming history (among commercial games, at least)"[^ref-70]. Bloody Disgusting described its unflinching treatment of sexuality, including BDSM and a bisexual protagonist, as having set it apart from everything before it, and also highlighted its handling of mental illness[^ref-71]. GLAAD noted that "Phantasmagoria: A Puzzle of Flesh might not have changed the world overnight, but its authentic LGBTQ storytelling absolutely helped move the needle for acceptance, at a time when it was desperately needed"[^ref-72].

The Sierra Chest describes it as one of Sierra's last FMV games and attributes the end of the series to the gradual fall of FMV games and the sequel's lower sales[^ref-73]. Sierra had for a while planned a Phantasmagoria 3 and asked Roberta Williams to return as lead designer, but after the commercial disappointment of A Puzzle of Flesh the project never materialized[^ref-74]. In 2021, GLAAD wrote that the game "continues to inspire and empower LGBTQ people through its renewed popularity and cult following"[^ref-75].
