# NASCAR Legends

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

NASCAR Legends is a racing simulation developed by [[Papyrus Design Group]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] under its Sierra Sports label for Windows in late 1999.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-11] Sources disagree on the exact day: Wikipedia and PriceCharting give November 22, 1999, IGN and LaunchBox give November 20, 1999, and Metacritic lists October 31, 1999.[^ref-1][^ref-7][^ref-4][^ref-13][^ref-15] GameSpot called the game "an homage to the 1970 NASCAR season," and it recreates the drivers, cars and venues of the 1970 NASCAR Grand National Series.[^ref-1][^ref-3] It uses the same engine as NASCAR Racing 3.[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-5]

The game features 42 drivers from the 1970 season and 16 of that year's tracks.[^ref-3][^ref-5] Wikipedia counts 48 real events in the 1970 season, while GameSpot's review gives 49.[^ref-1][^ref-3] Players can drive the Plymouth Superbird, Dodge Daytona, Ford Torino or a Mercury, and MobyGames notes that each of the four cars has "a different look and feel."[^ref-3][^ref-6] The racing rules follow the period: there is no pit road speed limit and no restrictor plates.[^ref-5]

NASCAR Legends was well reviewed on release. GameSpot gave it 9/10 and IGN 8.9/10.[^ref-3][^ref-4] Critics praised its recreation of the 1970 season. GameSpot and GameGenie.com both suggested it might have worked better as an add-on to NASCAR Racing 3 than as a standalone product.[^ref-3][^ref-5]

## Story Summary

NASCAR Legends has no narrative story mode. It is a historical simulation of the 1970 NASCAR Grand National Series season.[^ref-1][^ref-3] According to GameSpot, it includes online bios of the drivers and a detailed history of the 1970 season, with anecdotes and comments from some of the drivers.[^ref-3]

The 1970 season featured the winged "aero" cars, the Plymouth Superbird and Dodge Daytona, with their protruding nose cones and high-mounted rear wings.[^ref-14] In the game they race alongside the Ford Torino and a Mercury.[^ref-3][^ref-6] Players race as, or against, 42 drivers from that season, and IGN notes that any driver's name can be changed to the player's own.[^ref-3][^ref-4]

GameGenie.com noted that the game follows the rules of 1970, so there is no pit road speed limit and no restrictor plates.[^ref-5]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

IGN recommended playing with a force-feedback steering wheel, and GameSpot praised the game's "superb force feedback support," which carries over from NASCAR Racing 3.[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-6] Multiplayer works over LAN/IPX, TCP/IP or Won.Net's servers.[^ref-4] The ESPN/GameSpot index lists support for 1–20 players.[^ref-2] A bundled paint-shop utility lets players design custom liveries.[^ref-3][^ref-4]

### Structure and Progression

According to IGN, the game offers three kinds of play:[^ref-4]

- **Single Race:** Individual races with a chosen driver and track, and as much or as little car setup as the player wants[^ref-4]
- **Championship Season:** A schedule of races with points awarded by race length and finishing position. Progress can be saved.[^ref-4]
- **Multiplayer:** LAN or internet competition[^ref-4]

GameGenie.com noted that there is no career mode spanning several seasons, and 1970 is the only season included.[^ref-5]

### Tracks and Venues

The game features 16 tracks from the 1970 season.[^ref-3][^ref-5] They include:

- Riverside, a nine-turn road course[^ref-3][^ref-5]
- Ontario, a defunct track. According to MobyGames, it wasn't raced on until 1971.[^ref-5][^ref-6]
- Texas World Speedway. MobyGames notes that the real 1970 race there was canceled mid-season.[^ref-6]
- Bowman Gray Stadium, a quarter-mile track, and North Wilkesboro Speedway[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- A venue that GameGenie.com describes as "actually a high school track"[^ref-5]

Daytona International Speedway is absent, reportedly because of licensing issues.[^ref-3][^ref-4] Hickory and the Smokey Mountain Speedway are also missing.[^ref-3]

### Car Selection and Mechanics

Players choose from four cars:[^ref-3][^ref-6]

- **Plymouth Superbird:** Winged aero car[^ref-3][^ref-14]
- **Dodge Daytona:** Winged aero car[^ref-3][^ref-14]
- **Ford Torino**[^ref-3]
- **Mercury**[^ref-3][^ref-6]

The game automatically uses the aero version of a car on speedways and road courses, and the base car on short tracks.[^ref-1]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

As a racing simulation, NASCAR Legends has no traditional puzzles. Its challenge lies in car setup and race strategy.[^ref-3][^ref-4] The bias-ply tires of the era make it easier to recover from skids and spins, but they wear out much faster than modern radials. GameSpot notes that managing tire pressure and temperature plays a crucial role in full-length races.[^ref-3] In the garage screen, players can adjust tire pressure, gear ratios, camber, weight, wheel lock and suspension.[^ref-3] GameGenie.com found that the cars "handle slightly different than those of today, but the change is not large."[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

NASCAR Legends was well reviewed on release. Some critics questioned whether it should have been a standalone product rather than an expansion pack for NASCAR Racing 3.[^ref-3][^ref-5]

**GameSpot** gave the game **9/10 (Superb)**. Reviewer Stephen Poole called it "the only game for nostalgic NASCAR fans to turn to."[^ref-3] The score breakdown was Gameplay 10, Graphics 8, Sound 9, Value 8 and Reviewer's Tilt 9.[^ref-2] Poole wrote that "it's that very simplicity that makes the simulation both appealing and addictive - and downright fun to play," while also judging that "NASCAR Legends probably would have fared better as an add-on to NASCAR Racing 3."[^ref-3]

**IGN** scored the game **8.9/10**. Reviewer Marc Saltzman wrote that "while Papyrus' NASCAR Legends is a wonderfully-conceived and crafted racing game, it's primarily for older gamers with an affinity for this sports' glory days or hardcore NASCAR fans looking to race against yesterday's heroes in classic cars."[^ref-4] He concluded that "NASCAR Legends is a highly entertaining, challenging and gratifying way to forever immortalize this classic season in American racing history."[^ref-4]

**GameGenie.com** reviewer James Allen was more critical. He wrote that "this game should have been presented as an add-on to NASCAR 3, rather than a completely different game," and advised: "Unless you really, really want to race in the 70s, buy NASCAR Racing 3 and pretend the cars are old."[^ref-5] Allen did acknowledge Papyrus's standing: "Ever since the success of their racing games, Papyrus has been considered the authority of racing simulation."[^ref-5]

### Modern Assessment

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 76% critics' average (7 ratings)[^ref-6]
- **My Abandonware:** 4.59/5 user rating (34 votes, as of October 2026)[^ref-8]
- **Metacritic:** No Metascore ("tbd"); no critic or user reviews are listed[^ref-15]
- **ESRB Rating:** Everyone[^ref-6][^ref-7]

GameSpot also raised technical criticisms. It said "the car graphics seem just a bit flat compared with what you'll find in NASCAR 3" and that "NASCAR Legends' sound effects are also initially disappointing." It added that players can adjust the engine pitch to recreate the old V-8 rumble.[^ref-3]

## Development

### Origins

NASCAR Legends came from Papyrus Design Group, whose NASCAR and IndyCar titles Race Sim Central describes as having "consistently pushed the genre forwards."[^ref-12] GameGenie.com called Papyrus "the authority of racing simulation." It noted that after releasing NASCAR Racing 3, the follow-up to NASCAR 2, Papyrus "decided to go back in time to 1970."[^ref-5] NASCAR Racing 3 shipped in September 1999, shortly before Legends.[^ref-16] The 1970 setting let the game feature the winged Plymouth Superbird and Dodge Daytona.[^ref-3][^ref-14]

### Production

Wikipedia, IGN and GameGenie.com all state that the game was built on the NASCAR Racing 3 engine.[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-5] GameSpot specifies that Papyrus used the NASCAR 3 physics engine.[^ref-3] MobyGames' description instead says it used "a modified version of the NASCAR 2 engine," which conflicts with the other sources.[^ref-6] GameSpot found the tracks "rendered very accurately." GameGenie.com observed that some tracks were the NASCAR Racing 3 versions with "some scenery" removed.[^ref-3][^ref-5]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-17]
- **Project Director:** David Matson
- **Lead Engineer:** Charlie Heath
- **Designer:** Richard Yasi
- **Producer:** Matt Marsala

Period details include the absence of pit road speed limits and restrictor plates, and the inclusion of defunct tracks such as Riverside and Ontario.[^ref-5] The game also reflects the different tire technology of the era, with bias-ply tires that wear faster than modern radials.[^ref-3]

### Technical Achievements

The game supports force-feedback steering wheels.[^ref-3][^ref-6] GameSpot noted that it keeps NASCAR Racing 3's smoke effects, persistent skid marks and crash effects. It added that the Internet multiplayer mode was "allegedly smoother than in NASCAR 3."[^ref-3]

- **Audio:** 16-bit Sound Board

- **Operating System:** Windows 95/98
- **DirectX:** Required

**Media:**
- **Format:** CD-ROM[^ref-6]

- **Retail Price:** $49.95 at launch[^ref-5]

### Cut Content

Several tracks from the real 1970 NASCAR season are missing from the game. The most notable is Daytona International Speedway, reportedly left out because of licensing issues.[^ref-3][^ref-4] Hickory and the Smokey Mountain Speedway are also absent, along with many lesser-known venues.[^ref-3] Sources differ on the size of the real 1970 schedule: Wikipedia gives 48 events, GameSpot 49.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Ontario Motor Speedway Anachronism:** According to MobyGames, the track wasn't raced on until 1971, so its inclusion is inaccurate for the 1970 season[^ref-6]
- **Canceled Race:** The Texas World Speedway race was canceled mid-season in the real 1970 season[^ref-6]
- **High School Track:** GameGenie.com notes that one of the venues is "actually a high school track"[^ref-5]
- **Period-Accurate Rules:** The game has no pit road speed limit and no restrictor plates[^ref-5]
- **Shared Engine:** The game shares NASCAR Racing 3's engine and renderers, which led critics to suggest it should have been an expansion pack[^ref-5][^ref-8]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for NASCAR Legends have been published. It launched at a retail price of $49.95.[^ref-5] PriceCharting tracks current collector-market prices for the retail release (UPC 020626708470).[^ref-7]

### Collections

MobyGames lists NASCAR Legends as included in the **NASCAR Acceleration Pack** (2000).[^ref-6]

### Related Publications

- **User Manual:** Included with the retail release, written by Steve Vandergriff[^ref-17]

### Critical Perspective

At the time, critics questioned whether NASCAR Legends deserved a standalone release or should have been an expansion pack for NASCAR Racing 3.[^ref-3][^ref-5] GameSpot nonetheless concluded that it was "the only game for nostalgic NASCAR fans to turn to." The review also said that Sierra's decision to release Legends and NASCAR Racing 3 so close together was "ultimately to the benefit of racing fans."[^ref-3]

Players on abandonware sites still post compatibility advice and rate the game highly.[^ref-8]
