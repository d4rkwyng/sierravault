# Kempelen

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Kempelen is a chess program developed by Andromeda Software and published by Sierra On-Line for the Commodore 64 in 1986[^ref-16][^ref-2][^ref-3]. Sierra's own price list and the period press called it *Kempelen Chess*[^ref-21][^ref-24]. It is named after Wolfgang von Kempelen, the Hungarian inventor who built "The Turk", an 18th-century chess-playing automaton later shown to be a hoax[^ref-1][^ref-10]. The Commodore 64 title screen credits Attila Kovács, István Bilek and Tamás Révbíró[^ref-17][^ref-16].

Sierra also sold an Atari ST version, which magazines previewed in 1986 and reviewed in 1989[^ref-23][^ref-24][^ref-26][^ref-27]. An MSX2 version followed in Japan in December 1988, ported by Pony Canyon under the title ケンペレンチェス[^ref-5][^ref-7]. Under the Kempelen name, the program played in the World Microcomputer Chess Championships of the 1980s, but it finished at or near the bottom each time[^ref-1][^ref-12][^ref-18][^ref-20].

The Spacious Mind, a retro computer-chess site, puts the Commodore 64 version's strength at roughly 1150–1250 Elo. The site calls the program "suitable for beginners and casual players"[^ref-6].

## Story Summary

Kempelen is a chess program and has no story. On the Commodore 64 it is a one-player game against the computer[^ref-16][^ref-2]. The Japanese MSX2 version also lets two people play each other[^ref-5][^ref-7].

The name refers to Wolfgang von Kempelen (1734–1804), whose chess "automaton", the Turk, was first shown in 1770[^ref-10][^ref-11]. The Turk appeared to play chess on its own, but a chess master hidden inside the cabinet was moving the pieces. It won most of its games, including games against Napoleon Bonaparte and Benjamin Franklin, and was exhibited for 84 years until a fire destroyed it in 1854[^ref-11]. When ST-Log previewed Sierra's Atari ST version in 1986, it noted that "Kempelen is thought to be the father of game-playing machines"[^ref-23].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The Commodore 64 version shows the board from above and draws the pieces as graphics, while moves are typed at a text prompt. It accepts only legal moves[^ref-16]. The Spacious Mind lists the keyboard for both move entry and option selection[^ref-6]. Lemon64 gives the main control as a joystick in port 2[^ref-2].

The Spacious Mind ran the game through a PC link and describes its commands as PC keystrokes. In that setup, the player holds TAB and presses a number key to change level. TAB + P makes the program play its move at once, and TAB + N starts a new game[^ref-6]. Pressing the space bar shows the move list and chess clocks[^ref-6].

The Atari ST version, by contrast, was built around a manipulable 3-D board. ST-Log reported that "the board can be rotated or tilted, in order to be seen from another perspective (including 3-D), and colors can be set to your preference"[^ref-23]. Atari 1ST Magazine found that the board's position, orientation and tilt, the piece set and the colours could all be reconfigured. Moving pieces, however, meant dragging a white square around the board with a jerky mouse[^ref-27]. Micro News likewise found mouse movement so awkward that it was better to type moves at the keyboard[^ref-26].

### Structure and Progression

The Commodore 64 version has 10 playing levels[^ref-16][^ref-6]. The Spacious Mind treats level 3 as its "active" level, level 5 as its tournament level, and level 9 as its infinite level[^ref-6]. The MSX2 version offers seven difficulty levels and lets players face the computer or a friend[^ref-5].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The Spacious Mind's feature table for the Commodore 64 version lists the following[^ref-6]:

| Feature | C64 version |
|---|---|
| Opening library | Yes |
| Take back moves | Yes |
| Position setup / verification | Yes / Yes |
| Display move info / analysis | Yes / No |
| Solve mate | Yes |
| Save game / ponder | Yes / No |
| Hint / teach mode | No / No |

The MSX2 version animates the pieces as they move and offers several board designs and play options[^ref-5].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Sierra's first announcement was confident. In January 1986, *Computer Entertainer* reported that Kempelen Chess "has been battling it out with Hayden's SARGON III in Europe for several months, with the Sierra game the apparent victor". Sierra had therefore decided to release it in the U.S. for the C64/128 and Atari ST[^ref-21]. ST-Log's report from the 1986 Consumer Electronics Show repeated the claim that it was "said to be one of the most powerful chess games on any microcomputer"[^ref-23].

The reviews of the Atari ST version that have been located were negative:

- **Atari 1ST Magazine** (France, February 1989, issue 17) matched four ST chess programs against each other. Kempelen came last with 0 points, behind Psion Chess, Sargon 3 and Chessmaster[^ref-27]. The reviewer dismissed its configurable 3-D board as mere gadgets. He also said it made crude errors and that its move-entry system was "assez lamentable" ("rather pitiful"). He concluded that he could find no positive point that would lead him to recommend it[^ref-27].
- **Micro News** (France, September 1989, issue 24; reviewer "J.-Y. T.") praised the 3-D board as "un modèle du genre". However, it found the opening library thin and called the endgame play "une véritable catastrophe". It said that, next to Sargon 3 or Psion Chess, Kempelen Chess looked like a bad joke. The review closed: "Un soft formellement déconseillé tant que le nain ne sera pas fourni avec !" ("Firmly not recommended until the dwarf comes with it!"), which refers to the Turk's hidden operator[^ref-26].

No contemporary review of the Commodore 64 version has been located.

### Tournament Results

The Chess Programming Wiki lists four World Microcomputer Chess Championship (WMCCC) appearances for Kempelen: 1985, 1986, 1988 and 1989[^ref-1]. The wiki's standings pages show the following results:

- **WMCCC 1985 (Amsterdam), Amateurs section:** Kempelen ran on a Commodore 128 and scored 1½ out of 7, finishing 4th of 5. Its only win was against PK83, and it drew one other game[^ref-12][^ref-1].
- **WMCCC 1986 (Dallas):** Kempelen ran on a 68000 machine and scored 0 out of 7, finishing 14th of 14[^ref-18].
- **WMCCC 1988 (Almería):** Kempelen does not appear in the wiki's standings. The Hungarian entry that year was Pandix, by Gyula and Zsuzsa Horváth[^ref-19].
- **WMCCC 1989 (Portorož), Software Group:** "Kempelen Atari" scored 1 point from 8 games, finishing 7th of 9[^ref-20].

### Modern Assessment

In The Spacious Mind's active-game testing, the Commodore 64 version played 50 games, with 14 wins, 8 draws and 28 losses (36.00%). That gives an Elo of 1156[^ref-6]. The site says these games were "played with a Commodore 64 or 128". The software was loaded from a PC onto real hardware through C64TPC; the games were not played in an emulator[^ref-6]. The Chesstroid blog later ranked Kempelen 16th of 22 Commodore 64 chess programs at 1156 Elo. That blog says its ratings were "collected on internet", so its figure is not independent of The Spacious Mind's[^ref-14]. In a Lemon64 forum thread about the best C64 chess game, one member recommended Kempelen and noted that it was made by Hungarian programmers[^ref-15].

## Development

### Origins

Andromeda Software was a British distributor that acted as a go-between for Eastern Bloc programmers, mostly Hungarian, and Western publishers. Robert Stein founded it in 1982, and the Hungarian software group Novotrade stood behind it[^ref-28]. Kempelen is one of the C64 games C64-Wiki lists under Andromeda[^ref-28].

The Chess Programming Wiki describes Kempelen as "a chess program written by Attila Kovács for 6502 and later 68000 based home computers". It says the commercial Commodore version was co-authored by Grandmaster István Bilek and Tamás Révkirá, and that the name honours Wolfgang von Kempelen[^ref-1].

### Production

**Commodore 64.** The C64 title screen credits Attila Kovács, István Bilek and Tamás Révbíró, under Andromeda Software and Sierra branding with a 1986 copyright[^ref-17]. MobyGames credits the same three people[^ref-16]. Lemon64 lists all three as coders[^ref-2]. No source located gives Bilek a separate role, such as chess consultant.

**Spelling conflict.** The third name is spelled differently across sources. MobyGames credits "Tamás Révbíró"[^ref-16]. The title screen's small pixel font reads with a "b" (not a "k") and ends in "-ró"[^ref-17]. Lemon64 and the Chess Programming Wiki give "Tamás Révkirá"[^ref-2][^ref-1]. This page follows the in-game title screen and MobyGames.

**Atari ST.** A first-hand account complicates the Kovács-only credit for the 68000 version. In *Mikroszámítógép Magazin* (July 1987), Gyula Horváth wrote about the 1986 Dallas championship and called the Atari Kempelen "my program". The magazine's game score likewise credits "Atari Kempelen (Készítette: Horváth Gyula)", meaning "made by Gyula Horváth"[^ref-25].

Horváth also described how the ST version came about. In 1985 Andromeda commissioned Novotrade to produce a chess program that would sell well in the U.S. market. The result looked attractive, with three-dimensional graphics, but the client rejected it because its playing strength still fell short after half a year. Horváth was then given the program's "thinking" part, which he had two and a half months to write on a machine and language new to him. As a result, he said, it went to Dallas without thorough testing[^ref-25]. The Chess Programming Wiki still lists Attila Kovács as the programmer of the 1986 entry[^ref-18]. Its photo caption for 1985 says Kempelen was "operated by Gyula Horváth"[^ref-1].

**MSX2.** The 1988 MSX2 version credits "Logic by" to Attila Kovács. MobyGames lists "Made by" as SCH and Kadocsa Tassonyi (credited as "Kadocsa")[^ref-29]. Generation MSX lists Andromeda Software as developer and Pony Canyon as the porter[^ref-7].

**Development credits (as given by each source):**
- **C64:** Attila Kovács, István Bilek, Tamás Révbíró / Révkirá[^ref-17][^ref-16][^ref-2]
- **Atari ST (WMCCC 1986 entry):** Attila Kovács per the Chess Programming Wiki[^ref-18]; Gyula Horváth by his own 1987 account[^ref-25]
- **MSX2:** Logic by Attila Kovács; made by SCH and Kadocsa Tassonyi[^ref-29]

### Technical Achievements

The Spacious Mind's feature table shows the C64 version could set up and verify positions, solve mates, take back moves and save games. It also had an opening library. It did not offer a hint mode, a teach mode, analysis display or pondering[^ref-6]. In his 1987 article, Horváth said a particular kind of attacking move cost the ST version game after game in Dallas, because the program did not foresee it. He wrote that he could correct the flaw only after returning home[^ref-25].

### Technical Specifications

**Commodore 64 version** (from The Spacious Mind's specification table unless noted)[^ref-6]:
- **Processor:** 6502, 8-bit, 1.023 MHz (NTSC)
- **ROM / RAM:** 20 KB / 64 KB
- **Programming language:** 6502 Assembly
- **Disk image:** D64, 171 KB
- **Move entry / options:** Keyboard; Lemon64 lists Joystick (Port 2) as the main control[^ref-2]
- **Playing levels:** 10
- **Players:** 1[^ref-16][^ref-2]
- **Language:** English[^ref-2]

**MSX2 version:**
- **Minimum system:** MSX2[^ref-5][^ref-7]
- **RAM / VRAM:** 64 KB / 128 KB; sound by PSG[^ref-7]
- **Input:** Keyboard, according to MobyGames[^ref-5]; Keyboard and joystick, according to Generation MSX[^ref-7]
- **Players:** Up to 2, one at a time[^ref-7]
- **Note:** Generation MSX warns that the program does not work properly if a mouse is plugged in[^ref-7]

### Technical Issues

The Spacious Mind states that "Playing KEMPELEN with an Emulator should play exactly the same as using a Commodore 64 or 128"[^ref-6]. Generation MSX notes that the MSX2 version malfunctions when a mouse is connected[^ref-7]. Micro News reported that the ST version was slow to choose its moves and could bug out when a game went badly[^ref-26].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Historical namesake:** The program is named after Wolfgang von Kempelen, builder of the Turk chess automaton[^ref-1][^ref-10][^ref-11].
- **Title-screen art:** The C64 title screen shows a figure seated at a chessboard above the Kempelen logo, alongside the Sierra and Andromeda logos[^ref-17].
- **Japanese title:** The MSX2 version is titled ケンペレンチェス (Kempelen Chess)[^ref-5][^ref-7].

## Voice Cast

Kempelen has no voice acting.

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for Kempelen have been located. Atari 1ST Magazine remarked in 1989 that the program was little known in France and had never had significant commercial success there[^ref-27].

### Collections

No compilation releases of Kempelen have been documented.

### Fan Projects

No fan remakes or modifications have been documented. The Commodore 64 version is preserved as abandonware[^ref-3].

### Related Publications

No hint books or strategy guides have been located, and no manual scan has been located either.

### Critical Perspective

Sierra's own price list advertised Kempelen Chess as "State-of-the-art in chess simulation"[^ref-24]. The record does not bear that out. The French reviews of the Atari ST version were harsh[^ref-26][^ref-27], and the program's championship results were poor[^ref-12][^ref-18][^ref-20]. Its main historical interest is in its origins: it was a Hungarian program brought to Western publishers through Andromeda Software[^ref-28][^ref-25].
