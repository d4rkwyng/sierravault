# Jones in the Fast Lane

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Jones in the Fast Lane is a computerized board game and life simulation published by Sierra On-Line in 1990[^ref-18][^ref-35]. Sources disagree on the year: PCGamingWiki and MobyGames both date the DOS release to 1991[^ref-9][^ref-1]. It began as an unpublished game by Meredith, Christopher and Robert Whaley and Kelly Walker, which Sierra bought and redeveloped in-house[^ref-10][^ref-15][^ref-35]. Sierra's own magazine quoted Bill Davis, the game's designer and Sierra's creative director, as saying: "This is the game you'll bring out when friends drop by, or when the family just can't take one more evening of Trivial Pursuit."[^ref-35] It was built on Sierra's SCI1 interpreter[^ref-18][^ref-41].

*COMPUTE!*'s Eddie Huffman placed Jones in the Fast Lane "somewhere between a conventional board game and a computer adventure game," calling it "a tricky trip through real life."[^ref-collection] One to four players, including the computer-controlled Jones, set their own goals for money, happiness, education and career, and then race to be first to reach them[^ref-1][^ref-36].

## Story Summary

There is no plot in the adventure-game sense. The manual opens with a mock plea from Jones himself: "My name is Jones, and I'd like you to meet my beautiful family. We may not be rich or famous, but at least we're honest and hardworking." The plea goes on to beg players not to pirate the game[^ref-10]. In play, each player starts with $200, no education and no job, and must find work, buy food and pay rent while building toward their goals[^ref-39]. A solo player can race against the computer-controlled Jones; otherwise up to four people compete[^ref-1][^ref-36][^ref-39].

The game's name and goals are a play on the idea of "keeping up with the Joneses"[^ref-2]. Its original title was *Keeping Up With Jones*[^ref-35][^ref-36].

What a player did over the weekend is reported at the start of each turn, along with what it cost[^ref-39]. *COMPUTE!* warned of "spoiled groceries, unexpected doctor bills, and that inescapable rent payment"[^ref-collection].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The game is played mainly with a mouse, with the arrow keys, numeric keypad or a joystick as alternatives[^ref-10]. The board represents the city where the players live[^ref-1]. Time is the main resource. Each turn is one week of the character's life, and moving between locations, working, studying and resting all use up the time left in that turn[^ref-1][^ref-2]. *Computer Gaming World*'s 1990 preview described a game-clock icon that turns red as time runs out[^ref-36].

The walking game pieces and shopkeepers are digitized images of live actors, and Sierra staff played all of the parts[^ref-35][^ref-36]. In a later interview, Schwader confirmed that the game used rotoscoping[^ref-15].

### Structure and Progression

Players enroll in school, pay rent and buy food and other necessities[^ref-23][^ref-39]. At first the employment office offers only menial jobs, so players have to study to qualify for better-paid work[^ref-37][^ref-39]. Prices and wages move with an in-game economy that is affected by events such as a Wall Street scandal or an oil crisis, which players learn about by buying a newspaper[^ref-36].

Lead programmer Warren Schwader's advice for winning: "Generally you just need to stick to the basics: eat every week, go to school, then work and watch that economy."[^ref-15]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The winner is the first player to reach all of their goals. Success is measured by happiness as well as wealth[^ref-1][^ref-5]. The four goals are:
- **Money**: how much money the player has[^ref-5]
- **Education**: study at the game's educational institutions[^ref-36]
- **Career**: moving up the ranks at one of the game's employers[^ref-36]
- **Happiness**: raised by time off work and by small luxuries such as a VCR or concert tickets[^ref-5][^ref-39]

The game's fast-food restaurant, Monolith Burger, is named after the restaurant of the same name in Sierra's Space Quest series[^ref-2].

*COMPUTE!* concluded: "While essentially a board game played on a computer, Jones is a compelling diversion enhanced by appealing graphics and clever asides."[^ref-collection] The *Los Angeles Times* noted that Sierra shipped the game without copy protection and at a lower price than usual[^ref-20]. The German *Power Play* found the jokes went stale with repeated play and the multiplayer turns slow[^ref-38].

### Modern Assessment

In 2018, Jimmy Maher called the game "Fun in short doses, but a little too shallow and random to be given the status of classic."[^ref-22] Players on MyAbandonware rate it 4.39/5 (153 votes)[^ref-23], and MobyGames gives it a 67% critic average from nine reviews[^ref-1].

## Development

### Origins

The game began as *Keeping Up With Jones*, an unpublished game by Meredith, Christopher and Robert Whaley and Kelly Walker[^ref-10][^ref-15]. Sierra bought the rights in 1989[^ref-35]. Maher describes it as "an unsolicited outside submission"[^ref-22]. The sources disagree about what form it arrived in. *Computer Gaming World* wrote in 1990 that Sierra's executives had seen "an EGA version" with part of the final game's framework[^ref-36]. Schwader later recalled that the game was "in text form" when Sierra acquired it[^ref-15]. It was a computer game from the start. When the interviewer remarked that Jones would have made a great board game, Schwader agreed but added, "As far as I know it was never considered though."[^ref-15]

Sierra hired Bill Davis as creative director in July 1989, after a career as a television director and animator[^ref-22]. According to Schwader, Davis was brought in to move Sierra away from the programmer-as-developer approach, worked mainly on art direction, and championed the use of storyboards[^ref-15]. Maher says Davis personally shepherded Jones to completion[^ref-22].

### Production

Schwader, the lead programmer[^ref-8], recalled that "Jones had a four-person design team"[^ref-15]. *Computer Gaming World* reported that Sierra teamed him with Bill Davis and Bill Skirvin, with Ken Williams closely involved[^ref-36]. The manual credits:[^ref-10]
- **Creative Director:** Bill Davis
- **Producer:** Guruka Singh Khalsa
- **Lead Programmer:** Warren Schwader
- **Artists:** Jim Larsen and Andy Hoyos
- **Composer and sound effects:** Ken Allen
- **Dialogue:** Josh Mandel, Sol Ackerman and Warren Schwader

*Computer Gaming World* called Jones "Sierra's first VGA product"[^ref-36]. Ken Allen's score gives each location and character its own theme[^ref-36]. The game supports the Roland MT-32[^ref-30][^ref-31].

### CD-ROM Version

Sierra's Fall 1990 magazine announced a 256-color CD-ROM version for spring 1991[^ref-35]. The Fall 1991 issue of *InterAction* listed the multimedia Jones as "Available Now," with "a real digitized human voice" for each character[^ref-40]. Maher calls it Sierra's second-ever CD-ROM product, released before the CD version of King's Quest V[^ref-22]. MobyGames dates the CD-ROM edition's DOS and Windows 3.x releases to 1992. That edition adds Red Book CD audio for the voice acting, extra animation and actor portraits, and in its Windows version a native Windows executable[^ref-16]. MobyGames lists the CD voice cast as including Josh Mandel, Mark Crowe, Ken Allen, George Esparza and Robert Eric Heitman, among others[^ref-28].

### Technical Achievements

The game supports EGA, MCGA, VGA and Tandy/PCjr graphics. It needs 512 KB of RAM, or 640 KB for 256 colors[^ref-31][^ref-20]. Sound support covers AdLib, Game Blaster (CMS), Sound Blaster, Roland MT-32, Tandy and the PC speaker[^ref-31]. The game has no copy protection, and only one game can be saved at a time[^ref-3][^ref-20].

## Legacy

The game did not sell well. Maher writes that "it never sold all that well despite a big promotional push, and Sierra would never again make anything quite like it"[^ref-22]. Interviewing Schwader, *Retro Gamer* said it "sold relatively poorly" but has a cult following and several fan remakes[^ref-15]. Sierra never made a sequel, and no Amiga version was produced. *Power Play*'s 1991 review listed an Amiga version as "nicht geplant" (not planned)[^ref-38][^ref-15]. Producer Guruka Singh Khalsa later described it as "a stat-driven life game – long before the incredible success of The Sims"[^ref-15].

Both the floppy and CD-ROM versions run in DOSBox[^ref-33]. A fan-made Flash remake appeared in 2007[^ref-34]. Wikipedia lists *Jones - Life in the fast lane*, released on Google Play in 2014, among the game's unofficial remakes[^ref-2].
