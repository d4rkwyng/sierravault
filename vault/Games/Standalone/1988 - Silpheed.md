# Silpheed

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 29, 2026</small>

## Overview

Silpheed is a vertical scrolling shooter that debuted on the Japanese NEC PC-8801 on December 5, 1986, before being brought to North American audiences by Sierra On-Line in 1988-1989.[^ref-1][^ref-39][^ref-59] Developed by Game Arts, the game used 3D polygonal graphics on a tilted third-person playfield, and IGN later called it "one of the most ambitious technical achievements ever on 8-bit formats."[^ref-1][^ref-16][^ref-30] Music was central to its appeal: Sierra's 1988 announcement noted that each level had its own original composition and that an album and a book of sheet music had already been released in Japan, and Hardcore Gaming 101 says it "featured a phenomenal soundtrack, which really pushed people's perceptions of computer game music."[^ref-57][^ref-16]

In Silpheed, the player pilots a spaceship through twenty levels in a pseudo-3D vertically scrolling field, shooting enemies while collecting power-ups and earning new weapons.[^ref-7][^ref-54] Instead of dying from a single hit, the player's ship has a shield; once it is gone, further hits damage individual parts of the ship, a mechanic AllGame described as "the novel concept of sectional damage to shooters."[^ref-8][^ref-16] COMPUTE! called it "classic arcade fun with a little more depth than you find in many action-oriented games."[^ref-10]

Sierra published Silpheed in the United States after Ken Williams and his staff travelled to Tokyo in February 1988 to sign Game Arts, the developer of Thexder, which Sierra had already published.[^ref-11][^ref-57][^ref-59] The game was later bundled with Creative Labs' Game Blaster sound card.[^ref-56][^ref-60]

## Story Summary

The game is set after the year 3032, when an abandoned space shuttle built by an unknown alien race was discovered orbiting Pluto.[^ref-16][^ref-55] By careful analysis, Earth's scientists and engineers were able to duplicate it, making the colonization of other worlds possible for the first time and giving rise to the Milky Way Union.[^ref-16]

Xacalite, the outlaw leader of a gang of interplanetary terrorists, has seized the newly built battleship Gloire as the first step in a plan to take over the Union.[^ref-16][^ref-18] The supercomputer Yggdrassil has determined that the best plan for defeating Xacalite is to send a single pilot in a new prototype fighter, the SA-08 Silpheed, against him.[^ref-13][^ref-16] Xacalite taunts the player at the start of the game: "I am Xacalite, emperor of the universe. You can never defeat me as long as I have Gloire!"[^ref-20]

COMPUTE! summed up the premise: "When you take the controls of a Super Air Fighter SA-08 Silpheed prototype, you take the hope of civilization with you."[^ref-10] The player must fight through Xacalite's forces across twenty areas set in outer space, inside enemy bases and above planets, then destroy Gloire itself.[^ref-18][^ref-23][^ref-54]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Silpheed uses a tilted third-person perspective, with enemies and other objects shrinking as they move toward the top of the screen.[^ref-1][^ref-16] The DOS version supports the keyboard and an analog joystick; the second release tweaked the joystick routines to make them more compatible with faster machines.[^ref-8][^ref-60][^ref-61] Players move freely within a trapezoid-shaped area of the screen while firing at incoming enemies, and Hardcore Gaming 101 notes that the oblique view makes judging distances difficult.[^ref-8][^ref-16]

The game includes digitized speech in all versions except the TRS-80 CoCo port: the antagonist introduces himself as a giant disembodied head at the start of the game.[^ref-16] Computer Gaming World found the digitized Shakespeare quotation spoken by this talking face "completely unintelligible" on the DOS version.[^ref-54]

### Structure and Progression

The game consists of twenty levels, not counting the final battle with Gloire.[^ref-54] Dragon described four battle areas: space, above planets, in the fortress, and in the asteroid belt.[^ref-55]

- **Outer Space Zones:** Open areas with enemy squadrons and, in some levels, asteroid fields[^ref-13][^ref-55]
- **Fortress Interiors:** Narrow passages inside enemy installations[^ref-13][^ref-16]
- **Planetary Surfaces:** Combat above a planet, with no power-ups available[^ref-13]
- **Final Confrontation:** The assault on the battleship Gloire[^ref-7][^ref-54]

Computer Gaming World's reviewer counted "at least 15 different types of enemies," each with "different characteristics" and "different flight patterns."[^ref-54] Sierra's own later retrospective described "more than 30 increasingly powerful enemies," and its advertising promised "over 30 different types of alien enemy aircraft."[^ref-59][^ref-23] Every time the player earns 50,000 points, a new weapon is added to the Silpheed's arsenal.[^ref-54]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Rather than traditional puzzles, Silpheed focuses on combat mechanics and survival strategies. When the shield is depleted, further hits damage individual parts of the ship: losing a weapon pod disables that weapon, and engine damage slows the ship.[^ref-8][^ref-16] TV Tropes puts the shield's capacity in the original game at five hits.[^ref-20]

**Weapon System:**
Players can equip different weapons on the left and right sides of their ship, choosing at the tender between areas, and new weapons become available as points are earned.[^ref-13][^ref-54] Computer Gaming World's reviewer wished the game allowed "at least 2 weapons per left and right sides of the ship instead of one."[^ref-54]

**Power-Up System:**
- **Shield Restoration:** Repairs damage to the ship's protective systems[^ref-10][^ref-13]
- **Temporary Invincibility:** Grants immunity to damage for approximately 30 seconds[^ref-7]
- **Barrier Power-Up:** A rare "Easter Egg" power-up that nullifies bullet damage, only available on certain levels[^ref-7][^ref-27]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Contemporary reviews were positive. Dragon (issue #151, November 1989) gave the DOS version five stars, the top "Superb" rating on its scale, and concluded that "Silpheed is highly addictive, extremely colorful, and requires hours of enjoyable practice to master."[^ref-55] ACE (Advanced Computer Entertainment) scored the game 905 out of 1000 in its September 1989 issue.[^ref-1][^ref-29]

Computer Gaming World's August 1989 review by Sheila A. Carter, "Samurai Space Pilot," praised the score: "With the expertise of Bob Siebenberg of Supertramp, the accompanying score is better than the music one usually hears on arcade machines." It found the background scenes changed "very little from area to area" and concluded: "Silpheed is exhilarating, nerve racking on the screen excitement."[^ref-54]

COMPUTE! Magazine reviewed the game in September 1989: "If you're looking for a new arcade challenge, try Silpheed. It's classic arcade fun with a little more depth than you find in many action-oriented games." The reviewer did note some limitations: "The theme music is practically overwhelming. Unfortunately, weapon fire and rocket sounds are limited to beeps and boops; all that beautiful sound is limited to mood music."[^ref-10] COMPUTE! returned to the game in April 1990, writing that Silpheed "uses the card to produce a stunning musical backdrop for its plot."[^ref-56]

Sierra's own catalog copy called it "A classic action game that transcends state-of-the-art," with "a complete soundtrack with nine original music compositions."[^ref-58] The 2023 Nintendo eShop description of the EGGCONSOLE re-release calls it "The best 3D shooter game of the 80's."[^ref-18][^ref-23]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have kept their appreciation for Silpheed's historical importance while acknowledging its gameplay limitations. In a 2008 review of the 1993 Sega CD version, IGN's Travis Fahs called the original 1986 release "One of the most ambitious technical achievements ever on 8-bit formats"; he scored the Sega CD game 7 out of 10 and called it "a classic case of style over substance."[^ref-30] Hardcore Gaming 101 wrote that the original "featured a phenomenal soundtrack, which really pushed people's perceptions of computer game music," but judged that today "it hasn't aged so well."[^ref-16]

AllGame reviewer Kyle Knight called the DOS version "a decent enough PC shooter" that "falls short when compared with Arcade shooters of its era."[^ref-8]

- **GameFAQs:** 8/10 (single user review)[^ref-21]

## Development

### Origins

Silpheed was developed by Game Arts, a Japanese studio whose founders included developers of ASCII's AX series of games, several of them members of the University of Tokyo Microcomputer Club (UTMC).[^ref-32] Game historian John Szczepaniak wrote: "The consensus was that Game Arts was born from the passion and camaraderie of the AX series. During my trip to Japan I interviewed AX developers Akira Takiguchi (see below), Masakuni Mitsuhashi (Silpheed), and Kohei Ikeda (Thexder)."[^ref-32]

The game was inspired in part by the 3D shooting section of the arcade game Major Havoc. An annotation to Szczepaniak's translated interview with Akira Takiguchi says: "Like Theseus, Thexder and The Quest of Ki were influenced by the latter part of Major Havoc. In addition, the 3D shooting part in the first half was apparently the inspiration for Silpheed."[^ref-32]

Joseph Redon, co-founder of the Game Preservation Society, emphasized the efficiency of Game Arts' work: "Silpheed is only two disks. Really it's a marvel, that kind of game on only two discs."[^ref-33] He also praised the studio's consistent quality: "There's no crappy game from Game Arts. It's a team, from the beginning, of people who want to make great games."[^ref-33]

### Production

Silpheed was made during Japan's 1980s economic boom. Describing the PC market of that era in general, Redon said: "When you sell 50,000 copies, you're rich."[^ref-33] He added that Silpheed took so long to finish that "people thought it would never come out."[^ref-33]

Sierra secured the game in early 1988. In the Spring 1988 Sierra Newsletter, Ken Williams wrote that "This February several of my staff and I traveled to Tokyo to meet with Game Arts," and that the first copy of Silpheed had arrived at Sierra's offices about four months earlier.[^ref-57] He announced that "The IBM version of Silpheed will be programmed by Gary Butts" and the Apple IIGS version by John Rettig. The preview described "Each level (14 total)" as having its own original composition; the shipped game has twenty levels.[^ref-57][^ref-54] Sierra's Winter 1988 newsletter reported that IIGS versions of Gold Rush!, King's Quest IV and Silpheed were in development.[^ref-58] Sierra's 1990 retrospective dates the US release of Silpheed to April 1989.[^ref-59] The game was distributed on both 3.5" and 5.25" floppy disks.[^ref-35][^ref-61]

The TRS-80 CoCo port presented particular challenges. Programmer Robert Lindsley, who was only 17 years old at the time, recalled: "My entire life for the next three months was creating and entering hex codes into the Tandy CoCo 3. I was working 16 hour days and loving it."[^ref-16] The CoCo version required severe size reduction to fit both CoCo 1/2 and CoCo 3 versions on a single 16K ROM cartridge, resulting in missing features including fortress/planetside levels and music.[^ref-16]

### Technical Achievements

Silpheed was a notable technical achievement for its era. Every enemy craft, as well as the player's ship, is built from simple, flat-shaded polygons on an oblique playfield.[^ref-1][^ref-16] AllGame reviewer Kyle Knight noted: "Silpheed manages to create some fairly convincing 3D graphics through the use of shading and clever design work."[^ref-8]

The soundtrack was a major part of the game's identity: Sierra promoted it as a complete soundtrack of nine original compositions, and Computer Gaming World credited Bob Siebenberg of Supertramp with bringing "expertise" to the score.[^ref-58][^ref-54] The original PC-88 version targeted the PC-8801mkII SR,[^ref-1] whose Yamaha YM2203 sound chip provided mono output[^ref-4] across six channels (three FM, three SSG).[^ref-53] A MobyGames user review quotes the site's user-written feature "Ten of the Best Games You've Ever Played": "If you're lucky enough to own an MT-32, your starship-blasting experience will be augmented by some of the most catchy music ever to be heard in a PC action game."[^ref-14]

On DOS, music plays through the supported sound hardware, but sound effects always come through the internal PC speaker. The only exception is version 3.2, which added sampled sound effects and speech for the IBM PS/1 Audio Card.[^ref-13][^ref-60]

### Cut Content

The original PC-88 release disk contained two hidden minigames: a text-based game and an ASCII-based vertical shooter.[^ref-16][^ref-38] The fan site Silpheed.org also notes that the plot says Xacalite stole "missiles capable of destroying entire planets," but they never appear in the game.[^ref-38]

The TRS-80 CoCo versions were significantly reduced from the original, missing fortress and planetside level types entirely, as well as lacking the game's signature music.[^ref-16] This severe downgrade was necessary to fit the game onto the limited cartridge format.

### Technical Issues

The oblique viewpoint, while visually impressive, created gameplay challenges. Hardcore Gaming 101 wrote that it "makes spatial awareness and judging the distance between enemies, projectiles and power-ups almost impossible," and that "if you travel upwards, perhaps to grab a power-up, movement becomes erratic and any kind of precision is impossible."[^ref-16] Aiming, it added, requires "you to spray-and-pray rather than be accurate."[^ref-16]

The contemporary COMPUTE! review warned that "Most people will find Silpheed a tough challenge," advising: "Use the tips provided in the manual if you want to get past the first battle area."[^ref-10]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Hidden Minigames:** The original PC-88 release disk contained two hidden minigames[^ref-16][^ref-38]
- **Name Origin:** The game's name is likely derived from the ballet "La Sylphide"[^ref-1]
- **Japanese Title:** シルフィード (Shirufīdo)[^ref-1]
- **Barrier Power-Up:** Described as the "Easter Egg" power-up, the Barrier is only available on certain levels and nullifies bullet damage[^ref-7][^ref-27]
- **Star Fox Similarities:** Hardcore Gaming 101 notes that Nintendo's Star Fox "has some rather uncanny similarities to the 1986 release of Silpheed," and TV Tropes says Star Fox homages its wireframe launch sequence and that Andross resembles Xacalite[^ref-20][^ref-43]
- **Sound Card Bundle:** Silpheed came free with the Creative CMS Game Blaster sound card[^ref-56][^ref-60]
- **English Voice Acting:** Despite being made by a Japanese company, the game is voice-acted exclusively in English[^ref-44]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Silpheed was ported to several computer platforms and was followed by a 1993 Sega CD remake.[^ref-1][^ref-63] According to Wikipedia, the Sega CD version was a bestseller in Japan and a runner-up for GamePro's 1993 CD Game of the Year award.[^ref-1]

In the West, the DOS version reached new buyers as a pack-in with Creative Labs' Game Blaster sound card. COMPUTE! listed the bundle as "Game Blaster, Intelligent Organ, Silpheed, manuals" and wrote that the game "provides an extra premium to users interested in buying a sound card for their PC."[^ref-56]

### Collections

Silpheed has been preserved and re-released through various channels:

- **EGGCONSOLE Silpheed PC-8801mkIISR:** Released on Nintendo Switch by D4 Enterprise on December 21, 2023 in North America and Japan and on January 11, 2024 in Europe, bringing the original PC-88 version to modern audiences[^ref-15][^ref-18][^ref-63]
- **Sega Genesis Mini 2:** The Sega CD version was included on this retro compilation console in 2022, ported by M2[^ref-20]
- **SILPHEED: PC Sound of Game Arts:** A 2006 soundtrack compilation with music from Thexder, Silpheed, Tactical Gladiator Veigues and Fire Hawk[^ref-6][^ref-16]

### Fan Projects

Sierra's DOS and Apple IIgs versions have not been re-released; TV Tropes notes that they are "only available as Abandonware, likely due to rights issues."[^ref-44] A GOG wishlist request for the IBM version notes that the game works in DOSBox.[^ref-46]

### Related Publications

- **20-Page Instruction Manual:** Included with the original game, containing gameplay tips and story background[^ref-8][^ref-10]
- **Japanese Album and Sheet Music:** Sierra reported in 1988 that a Silpheed album and a book of the sheet music had been released in Japan[^ref-57]
- **Silpheed Official Game Music Sound (1993):** Soundtrack album released by Youmex/Futureland featuring both Sega CD and PC-88 versions[^ref-47]
- **QY Sound of Game Arts (2006):** Arranged soundtrack album by Packen Software[^ref-48]

### Critical Perspective

Silpheed holds an important place in video game history as one of the notable titles to demonstrate 3D graphics on home computers. While the gameplay itself follows familiar shoot-'em-up conventions, its polygonal visuals were remarkable for 1986.[^ref-16][^ref-30] Game Arts later became known for the Lunar and Grandia RPG series.[^ref-12]

Hardcore Gaming 101 sees a lasting influence in Nintendo's Star Fox, which it says "has some rather uncanny similarities" to the original game.[^ref-43] Its soundtrack remained the most praised element, from Computer Gaming World's 1989 review to modern retrospectives.[^ref-54][^ref-16]
