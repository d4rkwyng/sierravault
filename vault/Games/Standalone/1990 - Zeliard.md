# Zeliard

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Zeliard is a side-scrolling action role-playing game developed by [[Game Arts]] that combines platforming mechanics with RPG elements, originally released for the NEC PC-8801mkII SR in Japan on December 19, 1987[^ref-24][^ref-1]. A Sharp X1/turbo version followed on May 13, 1988[^ref-24], before Sierra On-Line brought the game to North America and Europe on MS-DOS in 1990 under its licensing agreement with Game Arts[^ref-2][^ref-3]. It was Game Arts' third game and its first RPG, and it was released roughly midway between Zelda II (January 1987) and Ys III (1989)[^ref-3].

Comma Eight Comma One called the game "a curious footnote in the history of Japanese computer games"[^ref-4], while Sierra's box copy promised "all the entertainment of an arcade game, and all the adventure of a role-playing game"[^ref-30]. Players take control of Duke Garland, a knight tasked with rescuing Princess Felicia and defeating the ancient demon Jashiin, who has cursed the kingdom of Zeliard with 115 days of devastating sand rain[^ref-5]. Game Arts' own product page describes eight underground worlds[^ref-24], and one MyAbandonware commenter wrote that "This game is what Zelda II should have been"[^ref-6].

Sierra's Western release made considerable changes to the game engine, resulting in a version that "players and veterans consider to be a hard game, but ended up being much easier than the other two versions"[^ref-7]. After Ken Williams licensed Game Arts' Thexder on a 1986 trip to Japan, Sierra went on to port three more Game Arts titles to DOS: Fire Hawk: Thexder 2, Silpheed and Zeliard[^ref-3].

## Story Summary

The narrative of Zeliard centers on an ancient demon called Jashiin, self-dubbed the "Emperor of Chaos," who emerges from a two-thousand-year sleep to unleash his wrath upon the Kingdom of Zeliard[^ref-5]. According to legend, the rulers of Zeliard had allegedly harmed Jashiin in ages past, and now the awakened demon seeks his revenge[^ref-9]. For 115 days, a devastating sand rain falls upon the kingdom, transforming the once-prosperous land into a barren desert[^ref-5].

To complete his cruel revenge, Jashiin turns Felicia, the princess of Zeliard, into stone[^ref-2]. King Felishika appoints Duke Garland, guided by the Guardian Spirit, to reclaim the holy crystals and end Jashiin's reign[^ref-8]. Garland must go into deep labyrinths and defeat eight of Jashiin's most deadly minions before facing Jashiin himself[^ref-35], collecting the Nine Tears of Esmesanti, which alone can reverse the curse[^ref-2].

The quest takes Duke Garland through a series of interconnected caverns and towns[^ref-12]. The bosses include Cangrejo (a giant crab), Pulpo (an octopus) and Pollo, a giant chicken[^ref-12][^ref-18], whom one boss guide introduces with the line "Madre de Dios! Es el Pollo Diablo!"[^ref-13]. Upon finally reaching Jashiin, the demon taunts the player with: "Finally, you reached me. I enjoyed your show. Come on! I'll kill you"[^ref-14]. Only by collecting the Nine Tears and defeating Jashiin can Garland restore the princess to her true form[^ref-2].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Zeliard is "a screen scrolling, action-fantasy-adventure game"[^ref-35] that can be played with the keyboard or a joystick (Ctrl-J and Ctrl-K switch between them)[^ref-11]. The arrow keys move Duke Garland, with the up arrow to jump and the down arrow to kneel; the spacebar talks to townspeople and swings the sword, the Alt key casts the active spell, and Enter opens the inventory screen[^ref-11]. Joysticks can be troublesome on faster machines: a 2025 VOGONS forum thread reports that Sierra revised the joystick detection in version 2.0, though even with v2.0 one user's MT-32 setup only kept the joystick working on the first load after a cold boot[^ref-16].

The function keys provide important controls. F1 toggles music; when the game is installed with PC Speaker as the music device, F1 cycles through "Music On; Single Channel", "Music Off", "Music On; Multiple Channels" and "Music Off"[^ref-17]. F2 turns sound effects on and off, F7 restores a saved game, and F9 adjusts the game speed from 0 (slow) to 9 (fast)[^ref-11], with 5 as the default[^ref-7]. Games are saved at a town Sage's hut with the file extension .USR[^ref-11], and each save file needs only 256 bytes[^ref-7]. Players can only carry 5 items at a time, requiring strategic decisions about inventory management[^ref-13].

### Structure and Progression

The game has eight worlds[^ref-24][^ref-3], each linking towns to caverns. Levels pair interlocking cavern maps, connected by colour-coded doors[^ref-18]. Towns serve as hubs where players can buy weapons, armor and potions, and each town offers a slightly more powerful selection than the last[^ref-18]. Dying resurrects the player with no gold unless it was deposited in the bank beforehand[^ref-18].

**Towns, caverns and bosses** (per the NeoZenogias walkthrough)[^ref-12]:
1. **Muralla Town** – Cavern of Malicia (boss: Cangrejo)
2. **Satono Town** – Cavern of Peligro (boss: Pulpo)
3. **Bosque Town** – Caverns of Madera and Riza (boss: Pollo)
4. **Helada Town** – Caverns of Escarcha and Glaciar (boss: Agar)
5. **Tumba Town** – Corroer and Cementar (boss: Vista)
6. **Dorado Town** – Caverns of Tesoro and Plata (boss: Tarso)
7. **Llama Town** – Caverns of Caliente, Reaccion and Correr (boss: Dragon)
8. **Pureza Town**[^ref-7] – Caverns of Absor, Milagro, Desleal and Faltar, leading to the final cavern
- **Esco Village** – a secret, hidden village[^ref-35]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The RPG elements include character leveling, with Duke Garland gaining levels as he progresses[^ref-6]. Currency is obtained by exchanging enemy spirits called "almas" at town banks, and the exchange ratio differs from town to town[^ref-35][^ref-7]. Visiting each town's Sage (after Muralla Town) rewards players with a new spell[^ref-19].

Equipment progression includes multiple swords and shields, and shields can break from overuse[^ref-4]. Special items such as the Hero's Crest, Ruzeria Shoes, Pirika Shoes, Slikarn Shoes, Asbestos Cape, Elf Crest and Fairy Enchantment Sword are needed to progress[^ref-12]. The DOS version added health regeneration by standing still, a feature absent from the Japanese originals[^ref-3].

Boss fights require specific strategies. One boss guide notes that Vista "is an interesting boss, in the sense that only the Knight's Sword can harm him," and calls the Dragon "probably the hardest boss fight in the game"[^ref-20]. The game employs a grid-based screen redrawing technique that enables smooth scrolling even on slower computers[^ref-17].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The Italian magazine *The Games Machine* reviewed the DOS version in issue 29 (March 1991), listed at L. 59.000, and gave it "Globale 81%"[^ref-36]. Reviewer Giorgio Baldaccini noted 512K RAM and CGA, EGA (640x200, 16 colours, badly dithered) and VGA (320x200, 16 colours out of 64) graphics with AdLib or Roland sound. The verdict box told readers not to be put off by the "orribile confezione" (awful packaging) with its badly drawn bearded Viking, and called the game "Più che consigliabile" (more than recommendable)[^ref-36].

Before release, Sierra's own *Sierra News Magazine* (Fall 1990) previewed Zeliard as "Game Arts' answer to the role playing adventure" and listed it at $34.95[^ref-37].

### Modern Assessment

Wikipedia summarises a Game Arts retrospective in the Spanish magazine *Planetstation* (issue 41, April 2002) as calling the game a classic with personality, recommending it to players who could accept its harsh difficulty, and finding the gameplay repetitive[^ref-1]. SierraVault has not seen that issue.

Hardcore Gaming 101 observed that "What could have been a classic of the genre is sadly held back by three big issues: the difficulty, the choppy controls and the disorienting level design," while also noting that "Playing Zeliard is a bit like taking on a new hobby: first you get intrigued and decide to try it out, then there is a learning phase where most attempts don't go as planned"[^ref-3]. The CRPG Addict (2011) found the images "rather lovely" and said "the sound and music edge just north of 'tolerable' (which is actually quite good for this era)," concluding "It's fun. It won't become a part of my soul or anything, but it's a good play"[^ref-21]. He gave it a final score of 36[^ref-18]. Comma Eight Comma One (2015) praised the music, writing "This game has some of the best chip tunes of any computer game," and concluded "It won't be the best JRPG you'll experience, but it's certainly worth looking into"[^ref-4].

MobyGames user reviews call it "an impressive action adventure game from the makers of Thexder," an "Excellent early platform game. And you can't go wrong with game arts," and "A very consolish action platform game with an adventurey quality"[^ref-2].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 7.1 (Moby Score), 51% (Critics, 3 ratings), 3.7/5 (Players, 35 ratings)[^ref-2]
- **MyAbandonware:** 4.67/5 (85 votes)[^ref-6]
- **Comma Eight Comma One:** 7/10[^ref-4]
- **PixelatedArcade:** A[^ref-23]

## Development

### Origins

Zeliard was Game Arts' third game and its first RPG, and Hardcore Gaming 101 sees it as the studio's first step towards its later Lunar and Grandia series[^ref-3]. The game combined platforming with RPG progression, and Game Arts' Japanese marketing promised "計算しつくされたマップや仕掛けがキミを待つ" (calculated maps and mechanisms await you) and "超リアルな動作に思わず感情移入してしまう" (super-realistic movement that makes you emotionally invested)[^ref-24].

The game was originally designed for the NEC PC-8801mkII SR. The main character alone had 336 patterns multiplied by the sword patterns, multiplied by 2 for left and right[^ref-24]. The original Japanese release retailed for ¥8,100 (¥7,500 before tax) on 5" 2D floppy disks[^ref-24].

### Production

On a 1986 trip to Japan, Ken Williams acquired the rights to publish a DOS port of Game Arts' Thexder; in the following years Sierra also ported Fire Hawk: Thexder 2, Silpheed and Zeliard[^ref-3]. The DOS port was the first lead project at Sierra for [[Josh Mandel]][^ref-3], who is credited as producer of the English version[^ref-11]. After obtaining the PC-88 version, Sierra made considerable changes to the game engine[^ref-7], including changes that reduced the difficulty[^ref-3].

The localization introduced notable changes: enemies give more experience points, the Alma:Gold exchange rates are more favorable, health regenerates by standing still, and the death penalty is less severe[^ref-3]. Most object and place names in the English version are Spanish: "Alma" means "soul", the caverns Malicia, Peligro and Madera mean malice, danger and wood, the town Bosque means forest, and the spell Espada means sword[^ref-21]. The DOS version dropped the digitized voices of the Japanese original, including the sound of falling rain and Felicia's scream in the introduction[^ref-2][^ref-7].

### Technical Achievements

Zeliard supported a wide range of graphics modes: MCGA (64 colors), EGA (16 colors, at an unusual 640x200 resolution rather than the standard 320x200), Tandy (16 colors), CGA (4 colors) and Hercules (2 colors)[^ref-3]. The game employed a grid-based screen redrawing technique that enabled smooth 2D scrolling even on slower computers of the period[^ref-17].

Zeliard supported multiple audio configurations including Roland MT-32, AdLib, Tandy 3-voice and the PC internal speaker[^ref-23]. Players could choose different hardware for music and sound effects, a feature that was still somewhat uncommon at the time, and the game could play multiple-channel music through the PC Speaker[^ref-17]. The complete soundtrack spans approximately 35-43 minutes depending on the platform version, with recordings preserved from the original Roland MT-32 MIDI score[^ref-26].

### Cut Content

The MS-DOS version omitted several features present in the original PC-8801 release. Most notably, the digitized voices were not carried over, including the falling-rain sounds of the introduction and Princess Felicia's scream when she is turned to stone[^ref-2][^ref-7]. The hidden string "Special mode !!" appears at offset 8ED in zeliard.exe; how to see it is unknown[^ref-7].

### Technical Issues

Joysticks can fail on faster CPUs. A 2025 VOGONS forum thread reports that Sierra revised the joystick detection code in Zeliard and Silpheed for faster CPUs, and that version 2.0 fixed the problem for one user[^ref-16]. The same user later found that, even with v2.0, a setup using an MT-32 through SoftMPU needed a fresh boot, and the joystick only worked on the first load until the next cold start[^ref-16].

The game may have installation problems under DOSBox, and in the worst case it will only run in CGA mode with PC speaker sound[^ref-4]. The Speedrun.com guide sets DOSBox cycles to a fixed 50000 so that runs are comparable[^ref-28]. A common error when using DOSBox is "Error in RESOURCE.CFG" on launch[^ref-27].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Zeliard contains several hidden secrets and bonus content that dedicated players can discover:

- **Secret Village:** Esco Village is a hidden location[^ref-35]
- **Secret Equipment:** A secret sword, free swords, a secret shield and the Feruza Shoes can be found[^ref-35]
- **Hidden String:** An odd string "Special mode !!" appears at offset 8ED in zeliard.exe, purpose unknown[^ref-7]
- **El Pollo Diablo:** The third boss, Pollo (a giant chicken)[^ref-18], gets the line "Madre de Dios! Es el Pollo Diablo!" in a boss guide[^ref-13]
- **Spanish Naming:** Most object and place names are Spanish words, such as Alma (soul), Malicia (malice), Peligro (danger), Madera (wood) and Bosque (forest)[^ref-21]
- **Command Line Parameter:** Typing "zeliard" followed by a save file's name loads that game directly from DOS, bypassing the opening[^ref-11][^ref-35]
- **Speed Adjustment:** The F9 key sets the game speed from 0 to 9[^ref-11]; one walkthrough suggests lowering it for bosses or tricky platforms[^ref-12]

### Notorious Box Art

Sierra's Western box art departed sharply from the Japanese original. *The Games Machine* complained in 1991 about the "orribile confezione" with its badly drawn bearded Viking[^ref-36], and Hardcore Gaming 101 suggests the Viking look came from the in-game sprite's helmet, whose "horns" are actually feathers[^ref-3]. In its 2017 feature "The worst PC game box art ever", PC Gamer noted that Sierra chose this look to represent the game's anime-style art when it brought the game west, adding: "I don't even know what's going on with that slug's lips"[^ref-29]. The back-of-box tagline "Get into Zeliard — because you can" has become an object of amusement[^ref-3][^ref-30].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for Zeliard were found in the sources consulted. MyAbandonware places it among the "flurry of imported games that resulted from Sierra's deal with Japanese publisher Gamearts"[^ref-6]. Blogger Dan Dickinson recalled it as "my first real exposure to Japanese gaming mentality", and remembered a side-scrolling action RPG as a rarity on DOS[^ref-30].

### Collections

In 1992 Sierra sold Zeliard in the **Action 5** bundle with Thexder, Silpheed, Firehawk and Oil's Well, all five for $39.95[^ref-38]. MobyGames lists the compilation as *Action Five* (1992)[^ref-2].

Zeliard is not sold on GOG, where fans have a wishlist entry requesting it[^ref-31].

### Fan Projects

Due to Zeliard's "rather unsatisfying ending, several gamers have been left with the urge to play a sequel"[^ref-32]. The fan community has attempted various projects including Flash-based remakes and modern engine recreations[^ref-32]. As one fan site notes, "Although compared to modern games, Zeliard may look like something trivial, but there is still a considerable amount of effort that goes into creating a game engine for it and getting all the aspects of the game play right"[^ref-32].

A fan walkthrough was written "at the insistance of a friend of mine, who happens to be the president of the Zeliard fan Club"[^ref-12]. Community-created maps for the caverns were contributed in 2008 and 2009, and boss guides remain available through GameFAQs and Neoseeker[^ref-14].

### Related Publications

- **Original Manual:** Included with the retail release; contains installation instructions, controls and credits[^ref-11]
- **Cavern Maps Poster:** Large poster containing maps of each game cavern included with retail copies[^ref-17]
- **Fan-Created FAQs:** Walkthroughs written between 2000 and 2005, including versions by AFranciskovic (v1.2, 2001), NeoZenogias (2000) and Sashanan (v1.3 Boss Guide, 2005)[^ref-14]

### Critical Perspective

Zeliard was one of several Japanese computer games Sierra brought to Western audiences around 1990, and it never achieved the recognition of contemporaries like Zelda II or the Ys series, partly because of its computer exclusivity and relentless difficulty curve[^ref-3]. Hardcore Gaming 101 sees it as Game Arts' first step towards its later Lunar and Grandia series[^ref-3].

Zeliard never received a sequel from Sierra or Game Arts, and fans have tried to fill the gap themselves[^ref-32]. Its difficulty and "choppy controls" held it back[^ref-3], yet it still has a speedrunning community on Speedrun.com, whose guide standardises settings for comparable runs[^ref-28]. The fan site's own summary is "Although the graphics aren't excellent, but the game play is"[^ref-15].
