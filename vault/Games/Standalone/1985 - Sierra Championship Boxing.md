# Sierra Championship Boxing

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 29, 2026</small>


## Overview

Sierra Championship Boxing is a boxing simulation developed by Evryware and published by Sierra On-Line. It was Evryware's first PC game, and the Murry brothers later recalled that Sierra published it in 1983.[^ref-20] Contemporary reviews of the IBM PC version appeared in 1984, and ports for the Commodore 64, Macintosh and Apple II followed in 1985–1986.[^ref-2][^ref-23][^ref-25][^ref-26] InfoWorld called it "by far the most extensive sports game created for a microcomputer" and suggested it "could usher in a new era of sports simulation games."[^ref-2][^ref-6] Guinness World Records lists it as the "First boxing videogame with real-world boxers,"[^ref-24] although SSI's 1983 boxing simulation *Ringside Seat* also included "over 45 great fighters past and present."[^ref-28]

Players could pick from a roster of real fighters from boxing history or build their own boxer with customized abilities.[^ref-24][^ref-23] The roster also included a few novelty fighters, among them a boxing kangaroo.[^ref-20][^ref-23]

Each bout could be played in one of three ways: an arcade mode in which the player controls every punch, a coaching (strategy) mode in which the player gives instructions between rounds, and a fully simulated mode in which the computer handles the fight.[^ref-7][^ref-8] PC Magazine's Corey Sandler called it "one of those breakthrough games that comes along only ever so infrequently these days" and concluded that it "is a knockout."[^ref-23]

## Story Summary

As a sports simulation, Sierra Championship Boxing has no story. Its "currency" is the single match: the game models one bout at a time under simplified WBA rules, and has no career, championship or tournament mode.[^ref-12]

The appeal lies in fantasy matchups across eras. The Murrys described it this way: "You could simulate matches between fighters of different eras like Jack Dempsy vs Ali."[^ref-20] The roster runs from bare-knuckle-era champion John L. Sullivan to the heavyweights of the early 1980s.[^ref-23][^ref-12] At the gym, the trainer greets the player with "I can make ya or break ya, punk!"[^ref-27]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Sierra Championship Boxing is played mainly with the keyboard. MobyGames also lists analog joystick support for the IBM PC and Apple II versions and a digital joystick for the Commodore 64.[^ref-7] In arcade mode on the PC, the player can tell the fighter to step back, move forward, jab, throw an uppercut, punch to the body or head, or cover up. In a two-player game, one boxer is controlled with the function keys and the other with the cursor keys.[^ref-23] Data Driven Gamer, playing the 1985 booter disk, found it differently: "Arcade mode can be played with two players, but the second player uses the strategy mode and watches the AI control their boxer." This may be a version difference.[^ref-12] The IBM PC version was released as a self-booting disk.[^ref-2]

### Structure and Modes

The game is built around individual bouts rather than a campaign.[^ref-12] A typical session runs as follows:

- **Fighter Selection:** Pick from the supplied roster. PC Magazine counted 53 fighters in the IBM PC version, while Retro365 describes "a list of 50 famous boxers."[^ref-23][^ref-6]
- **Character Creation:** Alternatively, create a new fighter by setting name, age, height, weight, reach, endurance, style and other factors.[^ref-23] The Macintosh version calls this area "The Training Room."[^ref-21]
- **Match Setup:** Choose the match parameters, such as the time per round, the number of rounds and the scoring system.[^ref-18]
- **The Bout:** Fight the match in one of the three play modes.

Between rounds, players can issue strategic instructions to their boxer.[^ref-23][^ref-20]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Sierra Championship Boxing has no puzzles. Its focus is statistical modeling, and Data Driven Gamer described it as "ultimately more of a stats-heavy simulation than a game."[^ref-12]

**Three Play Modes:**[^ref-7][^ref-8]
- **Completely Interactive:** The player controls every move of the fighter.
- **Coaching Mode:** The player gives instructions between rounds (aggressive, jabbing, defensive, etc.) and the computer fights the round.
- **Completely Simulated:** The computer handles all the fighting, based on the boxers' statistics.

Statistics are entered as text values. This lets players customize a boxer in detail, or recreate historical fighters and match them against each other.[^ref-7]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

InfoWorld's Scott Mace reviewed the game in June 1984. He called it "by far the most extensive sports game created for a microcomputer" and suggested it "could usher in a new era of sports simulation games."[^ref-2][^ref-6]

In PC Magazine (November 13, 1984), Corey Sandler scored the game 14.5 out of 18 (Fun 5.0, Challenge 4.0, Color/Graphics 5.5, each out of 6). He wrote that "this is truly one of those breakthrough games that comes along only ever so infrequently these days," and that "Championship Boxing, programmed by Dave Murry with graphics by brother Barry, is a knockout."[^ref-23]

Richard Bollar reviewed the Macintosh version in the April 1986 Washington Apple Pi Journal, calling it "Highly recommended, but not for the squeamish or morally disinclined." The second half of that line is a joke that ends "not anyone who would read anything I've written, I'm sure."[^ref-21] He praised the presentation: "The animation is excellent, screen refresh rates are fast even by Macintosh standards, and the image masking is well executed." He concluded that "Sierra has designed an easily playable and enjoyable game."[^ref-21][^ref-5]

Rick Teverbaugh reviewed the Commodore version in Computer Gaming World's April 1986 issue (#27) and was negative. He wrote that if options or graphics were all that mattered "this game would be heavyweight champ," but "if feeling like real boxing is a consideration, this game should throw in the towel." He reported that none of his 42 bouts went past eight rounds.[^ref-22]

### Modern Assessment

Data Driven Gamer's 2023 retrospective was blunt: "As a game, Sierra Championship Boxing is terrible." The same piece argued that rating it as a game "misses the point." In its view, the game is a simulation for sports fans who care about statistics, with animated graphics that are "above-average for the era."[^ref-12]

A HOTUD review on MyAbandonware called it "A great first game by any standard." It quoted the Murrys' own Games Domain description of the game's features.[^ref-10]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 81% (critics, 1 rating)[^ref-7]
- **Abandonware DOS:** 3.88/5.00[^ref-1]
- **MyAbandonware:** 4.75/5 (8 votes, checked October 29, 2026)[^ref-10]
- **Vizzed.com:** 7.5/10[^ref-13]
- **IMDB:** 5.0/10 (10 ratings, checked October 29, 2026)[^ref-14]

## Development

### Origins

Dave and Barry Murry began making computer games together in 1979. Around 1980 they started writing arcade-style games for Heathkit's H89 computer, and they made about a dozen before switching to the IBM PC.[^ref-20] In their words, "Our first game for the PC was called Championship Boxing."[^ref-20] Retro365 says the brothers, as fans of the sport, wanted to create "the most elaborate boxing game to date."[^ref-6] Data Driven Gamer described Evryware as "A literal Mom & Pop shop."[^ref-12]

The release date is reported inconsistently. The Murrys date Sierra's release to 1983, and Sierra Chest gives a first release of 1984.[^ref-20][^ref-9] A Computer Outlet mail-order advertisement in PCjr Magazine's April 1984 issue already listed "Championship Boxing" at $22 among its PCjr recreation titles, so the game was on sale by early 1984.[^ref-29] Most surviving copies carry in-game copyrights from 1985, and MobyGames dates its releases from 1985 on.[^ref-12][^ref-26]

### Production

Sierra was not the game's original publisher. In the Murrys' account, "We took it to Microsoft and they were going to publish it under a new home software division. At the last minute, they decided the home market wasn't big enough yet."[^ref-20] Retro365 likewise names Microsoft's "new Home Software Division."[^ref-6]

The Murrys' contact at Microsoft was a friend of Ken Williams of Sierra On-Line and told him about the game. "A few days later we had a contract with Sierra."[^ref-20]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-7][^ref-23]
- **Program:** Dave Murry
- **Graphics/Animation:** Barry Murry

### Technical Achievements

The IBM PC version used 4-color CGA graphics and could be played in composite mode for better colors on NTSC-capable monitors.[^ref-6] Sierra Chest lists the engine as machine language.[^ref-9] The PC release used key-disk copy protection.[^ref-18]

The Macintosh version was praised for animation and screen refresh rates that were fast "even by Macintosh standards."[^ref-21]

### Technical Specifications

**IBM PC Version:**[^ref-23][^ref-6]
- **Requirements:** 128K RAM, color/graphics adapter (also ran on an enhanced PCjr)
- **Graphics:** CGA 4-color; composite mode supported on NTSC monitors
- **Media:** Self-booting 5.25" floppy disk[^ref-2]
- **List Price:** $34.95

**Macintosh Version (as archived):**[^ref-5]
- **Requirements:** 128K or 512K Mac (MFS disk image); 512Ke or newer for the repackaged copy
- **OS Compatibility:** Mac OS 1.0 through Mac OS 6.0
- **Processor:** Motorola 68K
- **MultiFinder Compatible:** No

**General:**[^ref-7]
- **Players:** 1-2
- **Perspective:** Side view
- **Input:** Keyboard; analog joystick (IBM PC, Apple II); digital joystick (Commodore 64)

### Technical Issues

Some players on abandonware sites report problems running the game in browser emulation. One wrote: "Didn't work. When I tried starting a fight the game crashes." Another asked: "How do you throw punches???"[^ref-15]

Data Driven Gamer notes that the game looks much better in composite mode than in RGB, and that this requires an unofficial DOSBox branch such as DOSBox-X.[^ref-12]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Novelty Fighters:** The roster includes Matilda the boxing kangaroo and a wimp named Peewee.[^ref-23] The Murrys also recalled a "boxing space alien."[^ref-20]
- **Era-Spanning Matchups:** Players could simulate fantasy fights between boxers from different eras, such as Jack Dempsey against Muhammad Ali.[^ref-20]
- **Collectible Packaging:** Original copies came with a Sierra Championship Boxing ticket.[^ref-6]
- **Comprehensive Manuals:** The package included instructions, a booklet on the theory of boxing, and a history of the sport with biographies of most of the boxers.[^ref-23][^ref-18]
- **Guinness Record:** Guinness World Records recognizes it as the "First boxing videogame with real-world boxers."[^ref-24] The claim is open to question: SSI's *Ringside Seat*, which MobyGames dates to 1983 on the Apple II and Commodore 64, already included data for "over 45 great fighters past and present."[^ref-28]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures are available. Retro365 notes that Sierra kept porting the game and was still marketing it in 1988.[^ref-6]

Retro365 also notes that "No other sports titles from Evryware or Sierra On-Line using the same formula materialized."[^ref-6] The Murrys went on to make The Ancient Art of War for Broderbund, and then the Manhunter adventures for Sierra.[^ref-20]

### Related Publications

- **Game Manuals:** The PC version came with "How to Play," "Program Guide," "History of Boxing" and "Boxing Clinic" documents.[^ref-18]
- **Sierra Championship Boxing Ticket:** A physical collectible in the original packaging.[^ref-6]

### Critical Perspective

Critics split along a clear line. Reviewers who valued options and simulation depth praised it: InfoWorld called it the most extensive sports game for a microcomputer, and PC Magazine called it a breakthrough.[^ref-2][^ref-23] Reviewers who wanted it to feel like real boxing, or to play well as an action game, did not. Computer Gaming World said it "should throw in the towel," and Data Driven Gamer called it "terrible" as a game while placing it alongside SSI's stat-driven sports simulations.[^ref-22][^ref-12]
