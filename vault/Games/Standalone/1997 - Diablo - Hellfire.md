# Diablo: Hellfire

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Diablo: Hellfire is the only expansion pack released for Blizzard Entertainment's 1996 action RPG Diablo.[^ref-2] Sierra billed it as "the only authorized expansion pack available for Diablo,"[^ref-23] but it was not a Blizzard product: GOG.com notes that Hellfire "was not an official Blizzard product and did not carry Blizzard's endorsement or label."[^ref-9] Released in North America on November 24, 1997,[^ref-17] this single-player expansion was developed not by Blizzard North, but by [[Synergistic Software]], a studio Sierra On-Line had acquired in 1996.[^ref-20][^ref-15] The expansion introduced a new playable character class—the Monk—along with two new dungeon tilesets, additional quests, new items including oils and runes, and a host of interface improvements that players would come to appreciate.[^ref-3]

The expansion's storyline centers on Na-Krul, a powerful demon who once ranked highly in Diablo's hierarchy before turning on his master.[^ref-4] When a sorcerer unknowingly performs a ritual that releases Na-Krul unto the town of Tristram, the player must venture into the demon's lair to stop this new threat.[^ref-5] Despite being designed as an optional, off-the-beaten-path addition to the original game's content, Hellfire became, according to Polygon's account of studio head Bob Clardy's view, far and away Synergistic's most financially successful project.[^ref-20]

The expansion's development was notably contentious, with Blizzard North imposing numerous restrictions on what Synergistic could include.[^ref-6] Most significantly, Hellfire shipped without Battle.net support, making it a single-player-only experience—a surprising omission given that Diablo was one of the most popular multiplayer games of all time.[^ref-4][^ref-21] Despite these limitations and a rushed development cycle, the expansion generally received favorable reviews from gaming publications at the time and has since been preserved through digital distribution, being included as a free add-on with Diablo purchases on GOG.com since June 2019.[^ref-6]

## Story Summary

The narrative of Hellfire begins when a sorcerer, while performing a ritual, unknowingly releases the demon Na-Krul from his ancient prison.[^ref-5] Na-Krul was once Diablo's chief minion, a demon of tremendous power who had served in the Lord of Terror's hierarchy before turning against his master.[^ref-1] The sorcerer manages to seal the doors before Na-Krul can fully escape into the world above, but the threat of this newly awakened evil looms over Tristram.[^ref-5]

The player, having already faced the horrors beneath the cathedral in the original game, must now venture into two new four-level dungeons—the Festering Nest ("called 'The Hive' by many," as one 1998 review put it) and the Demon Crypt—to confront Na-Krul in his lair.[^ref-25][^ref-4] These new areas feature distinct visual themes: the Nest presents an organic, insectoid environment teeming with new monster types, while the Crypt offers a more traditional undead-filled tomb setting.[^ref-5] The dungeons are designed to integrate seamlessly with the original Diablo experience, though they can be avoided entirely if players choose not to engage with the expansion content.[^ref-5]

Hellfire's storyline is not taken into account in Diablo II.[^ref-3] GOG.com describes it as "a non-canonical single-player storyline,"[^ref-9] while the Diablo Wiki concludes that Hellfire's "status in Diablo canon is unclear."[^ref-2]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Hellfire retains the point-and-click, isometric gameplay that defined the original Diablo, presenting players with procedurally generated dungeon layouts to explore and loot.[^ref-8] The expansion introduces several welcome interface improvements that enhance the overall experience. Most notable among these is the jog toggle feature, which allows players to run around town quickly—a significant quality-of-life improvement given how frequently players must return to town and how spread out the various merchant characters are.[^ref-7]

Additional interface enhancements include new spells designed for convenience: one spell highlights floor objects, making loot easier to spot against the dark dungeon backgrounds, and another teleports the player directly to the nearest staircase.[^ref-5] These additions addressed common complaints about the original game, where loot could be difficult to spot as it blended with the scenery.[^ref-8] The Search spell in particular became essential for many players, with one reviewer noting "I really can't imagine playing the game without it."[^ref-8]

### Structure and Progression

The expansion integrates directly into the base Diablo experience, adding its content as optional areas that players can explore alongside the original sixteen-level dungeon.[^ref-5] The new dungeon settings feature distinct tilesets and enemy types that provide visual and gameplay variety:[^ref-1]

- **The Festering Nest ("the Hive"):** An organic, insectoid dungeon environment featuring new bug-like monsters and a distinctly different atmosphere from the cathedral and caves of the original game.
- **The Demon Crypt:** A gothic, undead-filled tomb setting and Na-Krul's home, with enemies such as Liches and Crypt Demons.[^ref-4][^ref-24]

The expansion also includes additional quests.[^ref-1] Because of its design, Hellfire's dungeons can be avoided entirely, allowing players to experience the original Diablo content without engaging with the expansion material if they prefer.[^ref-5]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Hellfire introduces the Monk class, the only new character class that Synergistic Software was permitted to add to the game.[^ref-6] The Monk specializes in staves and fights well barehanded, gaining defense from light armor rather than heavy armor—a different gameplay experience from the Warrior, Rogue, and Sorcerer classes available in the original game.[^ref-20][^ref-21] The expansion also contains two hidden character classes—the Barbarian and the Bard—which were left in the game code but require players to edit a Command.txt file to access them.[^ref-3]

New item types significantly expand the equipment options available to players. Oils can be applied to items to affect their statistics, while runes can be placed as traps to damage enemies.[^ref-5] The expansion adds a new page of spells, new affixes for weapons and armor, and new shrines.[^ref-5] Diablo himself receives a boost to his strength and power in Hellfire, providing an additional challenge for returning players.[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Diablo: Hellfire generally received favorable reviews from gaming publications upon its release, though critics noted significant shortcomings.[^ref-3] GameSpot awarded the expansion a 7.5 out of 10, with reviewer Desslock noting that "Hellfire fails to provide as compelling an experience as Diablo, but does provides a good quantity of new monsters, items, spells, and settings."[^ref-4] He particularly criticized the lack of multiplayer support as "an especially surprising omission considering that Diablo is one of the most popular multiplayer games of all time."[^ref-4]

Computer Gaming World's Scorpia gave Hellfire four stars out of five in the April 1998 issue. She listed "No multiplayer mode whatsoever" and an end boss who "is a big disappointment" among its cons, but concluded that "Hellfire is still a first-class job."[^ref-21] PC Games gave Hellfire an A- rating in Barry Brenesal's review, posted February 9, 1998.[^ref-24] Computer Games Strategy Plus also covered the expansion, with Cindy Yans reviewing it on January 16, 1998; she warned that "No multiplayer is implemented."[^ref-25] In its 1999 Premier Awards (April 1999), Computer Gaming World named Hellfire one of the "Other Add-On Finalists" for Best Add-on, an award won by StarCraft: Brood War.[^ref-22]

Critics generally agreed that while Hellfire offered more of the addictive Diablo gameplay, it lacked the atmospheric effectiveness of the original.[^ref-3] GameSpot's review concluded with qualified praise: "Hellfire is definitely more of the same, but that's not necessarily a bad thing, considering that Diablo was GameSpot's 1996 Game of the Year."[^ref-4]

### Modern Assessment

Modern assessments of Hellfire tend to view it as a worthwhile addition for Diablo enthusiasts, despite its rough edges. HowLongToBeat users give the expansion a 71% rating, with reviewers noting its value for fans of the original game.[^ref-26][^ref-7] One user review states: "If you are already planning on playing through the first Diablo game, there is absolutely no reason you should ignore the Hellfire expansion."[^ref-7]

The Cynical Gaming Blog offers a positive retrospective assessment, calling Hellfire "an excellent addition to the unique real time rogue-like (sort of) classic rpg we know and love."[^ref-11] However, other retrospective reviews are more critical. Klardendum's review concludes: "Hellfire, though... I'm glad I've finally played it, but I do not recommend playing it," citing poor design and tonal inconsistency.[^ref-10]

Phenixx Gaming awarded the Diablo/Hellfire bundle an 8.5 out of 10 in their 2020 retrospective, praising the package as "a masterwork of sound design, particularly when it comes to the music."[^ref-8]

## Development

### Origins

Following Diablo's enormous success as GameSpot's 1996 Game of the Year, there was significant market demand for additional content.[^ref-4] However, Blizzard North made the decision to focus their efforts on developing Diablo II rather than creating an expansion pack for the original game.[^ref-4] Executives at Sierra's parent company, CUC, pressed for an expansion instead; as Polygon put it, "To CUC's executives, abandoning Diablo was as good as leaving money on the table."[^ref-20]

The expansion development was enabled through corporate relationships: in February 1996 Comp-U-Card International (CUC) agreed to buy both Sierra On-Line and Davidson & Associates, Blizzard's parent, and on July 24, 1996 it completed both mergers, acquiring the companies they owned.[^ref-20][^ref-6][^ref-28] A November 1997 PC Gamer news item summed it up: "CUC owns two game companies: Sierra Online and Blizzard."[^ref-27] Blizzard's protests "fell on deaf ears," and CUC moved ahead with the expansion.[^ref-20] This corporate interference created a lack of cooperation between the subsidiary companies and set the stage for a contentious development process.[^ref-6]

### Production

Development of Hellfire was handled by Synergistic Software, a Sierra division. Blizzard North agreed to help on condition that it could "executive-produce it and get final approval on everything."[^ref-20] The project faced significant constraints from the outset, with Blizzard North limiting Synergistic to one new character class, eight dungeon levels in two new environment types, "a couple of music tracks," and a smattering of items and spells.[^ref-20] Blizzard North's David Brevik later said that "the Synergistic team did a pretty good job. There were just certain requests we made, and they were summarily ignored."[^ref-20]

The development timeline was extremely compressed: Synergistic was first asked to make the expansion in six weeks, and CUC later extended that deadline to four months.[^ref-20] This rushed schedule contributed to many of the expansion's issues, including problems with pathfinding algorithms for ranged-attack monsters and the failure to carry over many bug fixes from Diablo patches.[^ref-3] The single character class Synergistic was permitted to add was the Monk; the Barbarian class was specifically vetoed by Blizzard North to preserve exclusivity for Diablo II.[^ref-20]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-16]
- **Designers:** Kirt Lemons, Donald Tsang, Peter Watje, Jim Edwards, Mike McMillen
- **Lead Programmer:** Donald Tsang
- **Art Director:** Kirt Lemons

- **Sound Effects/Mixing:** Craig Utterback

The internal conflict resulted in what Wikipedia describes as "a lot of frustration among Synergistic employees and a weaker expansion than Synergistic was willing and able to deliver."[^ref-6]

### Technical Achievements

Despite the challenging development circumstances, Hellfire introduced several improvements to the Diablo experience. The jog toggle feature for faster town movement addressed one of players' most common complaints about the original game.[^ref-5] New spell functionality, including the Search spell that highlights floor items and the teleportation spell for quick staircase access, demonstrated thoughtful quality-of-life improvements.[^ref-5]

Most of the new monsters use original artwork rather than recolored Diablo monsters. GameSpot judged the Demon Crypt tileset "as detailed as any of the four tilesets in Diablo," but found the Festering Nest tileset "a bit too disorienting and cluttered."[^ref-4] The new levels also came with their own music; CGW found the new graphics and music "on a par with Diablo," while GameSpot thought the mostly ambient scores did not measure up to the original's.[^ref-21][^ref-4]

### Cut Content

A significant amount of proposed content was vetoed by Blizzard North during development.[^ref-6] The Barbarian class, which Synergistic had added late in development, was ordered removed because Blizzard North was saving that class for Diablo II.[^ref-20] Both the Barbarian and a Bard class were left in the game code but hidden, accessible only through editing the Command.txt configuration file.[^ref-3][^ref-19]

Additional hidden or cut content includes:
- **The Theo Quest:** A hidden quest, given by a little girl near Adria's shack, that can be enabled through Command.txt.[^ref-3][^ref-19] Brevik objected to this "teddy bear quest," but it stayed in the game.[^ref-20]
- **The Cow Quest:** A hidden quest, also enabled through Command.txt, featuring a character called "Complete Nut" wearing a cow suit.[^ref-3][^ref-19]
- **The Wandering Trader:** A cut character that cannot be restored with Command.txt.[^ref-2]
- **Easter eggs:** Blizzard asked for the Easter eggs to be removed; programmer Jim Edwards recalled, "Obviously, we didn't, although we did hide them pretty well."[^ref-20]

### Technical Issues

Hellfire shipped with several technical problems that were never fully addressed. The expansion had no Battle.net support, making online multiplayer impossible.[^ref-3] Changes made while playing Hellfire do not transfer to online characters, but the expansion does not interfere with the player's ability to keep playing the original Diablo on Battle.net.[^ref-5][^ref-6] A hidden multiplayer mode could be unlocked, but CGSP's reviewer found that it left out the new levels and was "quite unstable."[^ref-25][^ref-20]

The expansion was released only for Windows; there was no Macintosh version.[^ref-15][^ref-1] Many bug fixes implemented in patches for the original Diablo did not carry over to Hellfire, and Sierra only released one patch that fixed a small number of Hellfire-exclusive bugs.[^ref-3]

Gameplay issues included problems with pathfinding algorithms for ranged-attack monsters, making certain encounters more frustrating than intended.[^ref-3] The Barbarian class (when accessed via the hidden code) suffered from a rage ability that one reviewer described as "basically suicide."[^ref-11]

Modern players may experience additional issues including mouse problems, DEP crashes, and launch failures on contemporary systems, though GOG.com has released updates addressing many of these concerns.[^ref-9]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Despite Blizzard's restrictions, Hellfire contains several hidden features and Easter eggs that dedicated players discovered:

- **Hidden Character Classes:** The Bard and Barbarian classes can be unlocked by editing the Command.txt file in the game directory, adding secret character options that were officially cut but left in the code.[^ref-3]
- **The Cow Quest:** A hidden quest featuring a character called "Complete Nut" dressed in a cow suit, accessible through configuration file modifications.[^ref-3] Programmer Jim Edwards, who voiced the cow, called it "a tongue-in-cheek nod to the Internet rumor about the secret cow quest in Diablo."[^ref-20]
- **The Theo Quest:** A hidden quest given by a little girl who appears near Adria's shack when enabled.[^ref-3]
- **Cornerstone of the World:** A special tile that allows players to transfer items one at a time between their own saved games.[^ref-2]
- **Title Removal:** Hellfire removes the "Diablo" title from the main menu entirely, renaming itself simply to "Hellfire"—a rebranding that struck many players as odd.[^ref-8]
- **StarCraft Dungeon:** One of Hellfire's dungeons was criticized for strongly resembling environments from StarCraft, though some fans defended this aesthetic choice as fitting the dark Diablo setting.[^ref-11]

## Voice Cast

Hellfire keeps the original game's town characters and their recorded lines; one 1998 review complained of "the exact same NPCs (with one addition) who say exactly the same things."[^ref-25] Blizzard North also gave Synergistic unused dialogue audio that was already on Diablo's CD-ROM.[^ref-20] New voice work is credited to Jim Edwards, Eve Forward and Craig Utterback,[^ref-16] and Edwards recalled, "I was the voice of the cow."[^ref-20]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Despite the contentious development process and lack of official Blizzard support, Hellfire was far and away Synergistic's most financially successful project, according to Bob Clardy as paraphrased by Polygon's David L. Craddock.[^ref-20] The expansion capitalized on Diablo's enormous popularity, providing additional content for a player base hungry for more dungeon-crawling action. However, Hellfire was not included in the Diablo Battle Chest compilation; instead, it received its own separate Diablo/Hellfire bundle release in 1998.[^ref-3][^ref-19]

### Collections

Hellfire has appeared in the following compilations and digital releases:

- **Diablo/Hellfire Bundle (1998):** Physical retail release combining the original game with its expansion[^ref-3][^ref-15]
- **GOG.com Digital Release (June 5, 2019):** First digital distribution, included as a free add-on with Diablo purchase[^ref-6]
- **Battle.net Release (January 2024):** Added to Blizzard's official launcher alongside Diablo[^ref-2]

The GOG.com release was particularly notable as Diablo had been the most voted game on the GOG.com wishlist for years before its eventual release.[^ref-9]

### Fan Projects

The modern Diablo/Hellfire community has developed several projects to enhance the classic games:

- **DevilutionX:** An engine port that provides quality-of-life improvements including native resolution widescreen support, modern control options, and cross-platform compatibility.[^ref-10]

### Related Publications

- **Original Game Manual:** Included with the retail release; MobyGames credits it to Suzanne David, Linda Westerfield, Ann Dickens Clardy and Kirt Lemons[^ref-16]

### Critical Perspective

Diablo: Hellfire occupies a unique position in gaming history as an authorized expansion that was never fully embraced by its parent franchise's creators. The expansion demonstrates both the opportunities and pitfalls of corporate-mandated development, where business interests pushed for content creation despite the original developers' focus lying elsewhere.[^ref-6]

The expansion's legacy is complicated by its uncertain canon status: its storyline is not taken into account in Diablo II, and GOG.com calls it "non-canonical."[^ref-3][^ref-9] Yet for players seeking more of the addictive gameplay loop that defined the original Diablo, Hellfire delivered exactly what was promised: additional content, quality-of-life improvements, and new character options that extended the life of a beloved game.[^ref-4]

From a historical perspective, Hellfire is a case study in the relationship between a parent company and its studios during the late 1990s: CUC pushed the expansion through over Blizzard's objections.[^ref-20][^ref-6] Brevik later reflected, "It was frustrating for us that Diablo was our IP and they did whatever they wanted to."[^ref-20]
