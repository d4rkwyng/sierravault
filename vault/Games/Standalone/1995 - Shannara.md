# Shannara

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Shannara is a first-person point-and-click adventure game released in late 1995, based on Terry Brooks' bestselling fantasy novel series of the same name [^ref-1][^ref-3]. Developed by Legend Entertainment and designed by [[Corey Cole]] and [[Lori Ann Cole]]—the celebrated creators of Sierra's Quest for Glory series—the game presents an original story set between the first and second books of Brooks' original trilogy [^ref-2]. The game was developed during what *The Digital Antiquarian* calls the second "bookware" era, when book publishers such as Random House again invested in turning their properties into computer games [^ref-3].

The game places players in the role of Jak Ohmsford, the son of Shea Ohmsford (the protagonist of *The Sword of Shannara*), who must unite the various races of the Four Lands to combat the returned Warlock Lord Brona [^ref-36][^ref-38]. Legend Entertainment's graphic adventures had evolved from text-based games, and the studio was known for eloquent, detailed writing [^ref-2]. The lush, colorful SVGA graphics represented a significant leap beyond any of Legend's previous games, but *Hardcore Gaming 101* found Shannara "remarkably stripped down" next to them, with far less descriptive text and dialogue than Legend's earlier *Death Gate* and *Companions of Xanth* [^ref-2].

Terry Brooks was consulted but did not write the game. Corey Cole wrote to *Computer Gaming World* in early 1996 that "We met with Mr. Brooks to discuss the game concept and his idea of how a computer game based on his novels should look, and we continued to provide updates to Mr. Brooks and his Random House editor, Owen Locke, during the course of development. Other than that, the work is completely ours" [^ref-37]. *The Digital Antiquarian* likewise reports that Brooks "gave the Coles his all-important nod of approval after they met with him" [^ref-3]. By 2012, though, Brooks no longer recalled the project: asked by *Lightspeed* whether he had interacted with the Coles, he answered "I don't . . . you know, I don't know anything about it," and, asked whether he remembered signing the contract, "No, I don't remember a thing" [^ref-6].

## Story Summary

The game opens in a time of crisis for the Four Lands. The evil wizard Brona, also known as the Warlock Lord, who was dispersed decades ago by Shea Ohmsford wielding the legendary Sword of Shannara, has returned "to wreak revenge on those who killed him," as *Hyper* summarized the premise [^ref-38]. The druid Allanon visits Jak Ohmsford—Shea's son—to tell him of Brona's return and send him on his way [^ref-36].

Jak's quest becomes one of diplomacy as much as adventure. The only way to defeat Brona, besides wielding the legendary Sword of Shannara, is to travel to the lands of the different races populating the world and convince them to put their differences aside and join forces against the common enemy [^ref-10]. Jak must visit the lands of the Elves, Trolls, Dwarves and Gnomes; in each he must find a magic item and a member of that race who can use it, and persuade that person to join the party, because only the items' combined powers can repair the shattered Sword [^ref-36].

Throughout his journey, Jak encounters numerous characters drawn from the Shannara novels, including the druid Allanon, King Eventine Elessedil of the Elves, Menion Leah, and the Troll King [^ref-13]. The game features multiple party members who join Jak at various points, including companions such as Shella, Brendel, and Davio, each with their own inventory and abilities [^ref-14]. The story incorporates magical runestones that Jak must collect, riddles he must solve, and challenges that test both his wit and his ability to inspire cooperation among disparate peoples [^ref-15].

The narrative builds toward a climactic confrontation with Brona, but the game notably provides "a significant moral dilemma" that adds depth beyond simple good-versus-evil fantasy tropes [^ref-16]. The storyline is original and slots between *The Sword of Shannara* and *The Elfstones of Shannara* [^ref-3]; *Computer Gaming World*'s Scorpia called it "more a sequel than an adaptation of a literary work" [^ref-36].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Shannara utilizes a point-and-click interface consistent with Legend Entertainment's other adventure games of the period [^ref-18]. The game is viewed from a first-person perspective, and navigation is done by "jumping" from screen to screen rather than continuous movement [^ref-7]. Unlike LucasArts and Sierra games of the era, Legend's adventures were written in the first person, meaning players don't see the character they are playing on screen [^ref-19].

Commands are built by clicking on an object and choosing a verb from a list that appears beside it; party members are addressed by clicking their icons [^ref-8]. Players interact with the environment using a standard point-and-click system with verb commands, inventory management, and conversation trees [^ref-10]. The game uses the same interface as Legend's earlier title *Death Gate* [^ref-21].

### Structure and Progression

The game features several distinct modes of play that players cycle through as the adventure progresses [^ref-14]:

- **Story Mode:** The main adventure interface where players explore locations, examine objects, and interact with characters
- **Combat Mode:** Simplified turn-based fighting encounters against various enemies
- **Map Mode:** A top-down world map for traveling between major locations, similar to Ultima's overworld system [^ref-2]
- **Dream Mode:** Special sequences involving visions and supernatural elements

The game progresses through various regions of the Four Lands as Jak seeks allies and artifacts. Key locations include the various racial kingdoms—Elven lands ruled by Eventine Elessedil, Dwarf territories, Troll strongholds, and human settlements like Tyrsis and Leah [^ref-13].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

According to GameSpot's review, "the real soul of Shannara lies in its puzzles" [^ref-22]. The game features inventory-based puzzles, conversation trees, and riddle-solving challenges [^ref-10]. Players must talk extensively with NPCs, including their own party members, to uncover clues—advice stressed in one walkthrough: "Just remember to talk to everybody including the members of your party" [^ref-23].

The game incorporates light role-playing elements, including party members who travel with Jak and have their own inventories and abilities, but lacks a full RPG system with experience points or character advancement [^ref-7]. Scorpia noted in *Computer Gaming World* that fighting "gains you nothing, as there is no experience or treasure to be obtained after a battle" [^ref-36].

Combat is turn-based and, notably, the game manual explicitly advises that "fighting should be avoided if at all possible" [^ref-8]. Multiple reviews echo this sentiment—one reviewer stated players should "AVOID ALL COMBAT when given a choice" [^ref-24], while Cindy Yans noted "you really want to avoid combat at all costs" [^ref-1]. The combat system received criticism for feeling "somewhat lifeless," with player impact limited to general commands like attack, retreat, and defend that "never really deliver the feeling of control that you need in a combat system" [^ref-22]. Characters don't gain experience levels through combat encounters [^ref-25][^ref-36].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Reviews were mixed. *The Digital Antiquarian* sums them up as tending "to be unkind," with hardcore gamers unimpressed by the game's simple storybook approach [^ref-3], although several critics praised its graphics and story and recommended it to newcomers [^ref-22][^ref-36].

*PC Gamer US* gave the game a score of 72% in their April 1996 issue [^ref-1]. *Computer Game Review* was more enthusiastic, awarding scores of 89/90/88 in their February 1996 review [^ref-1]. GameSpot rated the game 7 out of 10 (Good), with their staff reviewer noting that "Shannara is an excellent pick for novice users or fans of Mr. Brooks, but experienced players should probably keep looking" [^ref-22]. They praised the presentation: "Combining crisp, realistic graphics and a sparse but powerful soundtrack, Legend has created an atmosphere-filled adventure that is surprisingly evocative considering its medium" [^ref-22].

*Computer Gaming World*'s adventure columnist Scorpia (March 1996) found the puzzles mostly easy, the graphics "good although not spectacular" and the voice acting "adequate," disliked the combat, and objected strongly to the fate of Shella, the game's only woman. She concluded that Shannara "overall is an average adventure game, suitable more to the casual or novice gamer, and Shannara fans" [^ref-36]. The Australian magazine *Hyper* (March 1996) was harsher, scoring it 65 overall: reviewer Julian Schoffel called the plot "terribly cliched" and the in-game art "very average" next to "an absolutely magnificent rendered intro," and concluded, "after Mission Critical, I expected more from Legend" [^ref-38].

*Next Generation* (issue 17, May 1996) gave it 2 out of 5; according to Wikipedia's summary of that review, the reviewer felt it failed both as a Shannara story and as an adventure game [^ref-1]. Gordon Aplin's January 1996 review, archived at metzomagic.com (AbandonwareDOS credits the same text to *Quandary*), was more balanced. He found the Super VGA graphics "excellent -- as we have come to expect from Legend," and wrote: "Perhaps I am being too harsh. Other players may well disagree with my assessment of the puzzles... Shannara does have wonderful graphics, an involving and absorbing story which I enjoyed, and it even provides a significant moral dilemma." He still found it "an entertaining game and well worth playing" [^ref-25][^ref-16].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have generally positioned Shannara as a competent but unremarkable entry in Legend Entertainment's catalog. Hardcore Gaming 101's retrospective noted that "the world is quite pretty, at least—the lush, colorful SVGA graphics are a huge leap beyond any of Legend's previous games," while concluding that "the result is something remarkably average" [^ref-2].

Multiple sources describe the game as particularly suitable for adventure game beginners. Claire White's MobyGames review stated: "It's easier and more accessible than many adventure games, and a good introduction to the genre" [^ref-19]. A GameFAQs reviewer echoed this: "If you're an experienced adventure gamer, Shannara will give you a pleasant, low-challenge experience with a surprisingly strong story" [^ref-18].

## Development

### Origins

The development of Shannara came during what *The Digital Antiquarian* calls the second "bookware" era, when book publishers rushed into CD-ROM software [^ref-3]. In the summer of 1994 Random House, whose Del Rey imprint published Brooks, invested $2.5 million in Legend Entertainment, and the bestselling Shannara series was its first candidate for a game [^ref-3]. Legend's own company profile later called Shannara "the first game to come out of the new relationship with Random House" [^ref-39].

The project was helmed by [[Corey Cole]] and [[Lori Ann Cole]], famous for creating Sierra's Quest for Glory series [^ref-29]. This pedigree generated excitement among adventure gaming fans—as one commenter on GOG's wishlist page for the game put it in 2019, it was "Made by the people who made Quest for Glory" [^ref-30]. However, the Coles approached the material with some skepticism. Corey Cole later admitted: "We considered it a blatant Lord of the Rings copy" [^ref-3]—a criticism that had dogged Brooks' novels since *The Sword of Shannara* was first published in 1977 and "heavily criticized for blatantly ripping off J.R.R. Tolkien" [^ref-2].

Legend Entertainment was founded in 1989 by Mike Verdu and Bob Bates. Its early games used a text-and-graphics interface, and in 1993 it "broke away from the text tradition" with the point-and-click interface of *Companions of Xanth*, followed by the Super VGA *Death Gate* in 1994 [^ref-39].

### Production

MobyGames credits FAR Productions and Triton Interactive as the developers, with Legend as publisher [^ref-7]. Short of in-house designers, Legend contracted the Coles, who worked with Bob Heitman's Triton Interactive, using Legend's technology; Corey Cole recalled that they "set up an office in Oakhurst" [^ref-41]. Work began in earnest in the fall of 1994, with a one-year schedule and a budget of $362,000 [^ref-3]. *The Digital Antiquarian* describes the result as a "troubled" project, "plagued by communications problems and a mismatched set of expectations" between Legend and the Coles. Producer Bob Bates eventually brought Corey Cole and another Oakhurst programmer to Virginia to finish the game alongside Legend staff. Bates recalled: "We coded like hell until the thirteenth of November. We hand-flew the master to the duplicators and the game came out Thanksgiving week." The final cost was $528,000 [^ref-3]. Legend's own 1996 company profile dated the release to December 1995 [^ref-39].

Lori Ann Cole directed the voice recording [^ref-8][^ref-13]. The manual's credits list 31 voiced roles, recorded at B & J Studios in West Los Angeles [^ref-8], and almost all characters were drawn from the Shannara novels [^ref-21]. Legend's product page advertised a "FREE book enclosed, 'The Sword of Shannara,' by Terry Brooks" [^ref-40]; Aplin noted that the Australian release did not include the novel [^ref-25].

### Technical Achievements

Shannara represented a significant visual advancement for Legend Entertainment. The SVGA graphics were noted as excellent by contemporary reviewers [^ref-25], representing "a huge leap beyond any of Legend's previous games" [^ref-2]. The game used high-resolution SVGA backgrounds that showcased detailed, illustrated artwork [^ref-21].

The audio utilized the AIL/Miles Sound System for music and sound effects [^ref-7]. The game featured a "sparse but powerful soundtrack" that contributed to its atmospheric quality [^ref-22]. The manual credits George Alistair Sanger as music producer, with the music composed by "Team Fat": Sanger, David Govett, Joe McDermott and K. Weston Phelan [^ref-8].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

According to *The Digital Antiquarian*, "Upon its release, Shannara approached 100,000 units in sales, enough to turn a solid profit," though its impact was less than Random House and Brooks had perhaps hoped [^ref-3]. At the time, the game's manual described Brooks as having "twelve million books in print" [^ref-8]. The game was re-released in budget editions in 1997 for both US and German markets [^ref-27].

### Collections

MobyGames lists Shannara as included in two compilations: *The Trophy Case II* (1997) and *Big 10 Strategie-Sport-Adventure* (1998) [^ref-7]. The game remains unavailable on modern digital storefronts like GOG or Steam, which has led to a dedicated community of fans requesting its re-release—the GOG wishlist page for Shannara features comments from enthusiasts hoping to see the game made available [^ref-30].

### Fan Projects

The game is preserved through various abandonware sites and can be played through browser-based DOS emulation on sites like ClassicReload.com [^ref-33]. The Internet Archive maintains a playable version compatible with AO486 MiSTer FPGA hardware [^ref-34].

### Related Publications

- **The Sword of Shannara** (novel): Bundled with American retail copies of the game [^ref-25][^ref-40]
- **Game Manual:** Included comprehensive gameplay instructions and combat advice [^ref-8]
