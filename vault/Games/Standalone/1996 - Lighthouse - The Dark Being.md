# Lighthouse: The Dark Being

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Lighthouse: The Dark Being is a first-person adventure game developed and published by Sierra On-Line in 1996[^ref-1]. The game was conceived when Sierra's Ken Williams challenged designer Jon Bock to create something similar to Myst, pulling out a copy of the game and asking "Can you do this?"[^ref-4][^ref-2]. Set in a "supernatural mechanical world," the game follows a struggling writer who becomes embroiled in a parallel dimension crisis involving a mysterious lighthouse and its eccentric inhabitants[^ref-3].

Often dismissed as a "Myst clone" due to its first-person perspective and pre-rendered environments[^ref-29], Lighthouse manages to leave its own mark on the genre by carrying Sierra's traditionally challenging puzzle design into the new style of graphic adventure games[^ref-4]. The game features pre-rendered 3D graphics created using Autodesk 3D Studio and Alias, with Jon Bock describing it as a "Science Fiction Folk Tale" inspired by the machine age, Leonardo da Vinci drawings, and H.G. Wells stories[^ref-4][^ref-28].

PC Gamer (US) called Lighthouse "a fantasy-horror adventure that's a cut above most post-Myst games"[^ref-17]. Comparing it to its obvious inspiration, PC Gamer's T. Liam McDonald asked "Is it better than Myst? Much better (though, around here, that's not saying much)".[^ref-17] The game's tagline—"Outwit Evil in a Supernatural Mechanical World"[^ref-3]—captures its blend of horror, science fiction, and steampunk aesthetics.

## Story Summary

The player takes control of a struggling writer who has recently moved into a cottage on the Oregon coast[^ref-6]. Near the cottage stands a lighthouse inhabited by the eccentric Dr. Jeremiah Krick and his daughter Amanda[^ref-7]. Dr. Krick's experiments have opened a portal to a parallel world, and a creature he calls the "Dark Being" comes through it and steals baby Amanda[^ref-17].

The protagonist must venture into this alternate dimension to rescue Amanda, encountering a mechanical birdman, a Nautilus-like submarine, an ornithopter and a sunken wreck along the way[^ref-17]. GOG's store description calls it "a world teeming with invention, discovery... and evil"[^ref-3]. Bock describes the result as "a steam punk-like universe" inspired by the machine age and by folk stories[^ref-4].

PC Gamer likened its premise to H.P. Lovecraft's concepts of alternate realities, dark gods, and scientists who toy with forces they do not understand, mixed with Jules Verne wonder and imagination.[^ref-17] One puzzle even features a Chinese puzzle box "right out of Hellraiser".[^ref-17]

An interesting detail: the player character's sex is never revealed; The Collection Chamber joked about the rescued Amanda being stowed in the protagonist's "handbag/manbag".[^ref-collection]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Lighthouse employs a first-person perspective with locations represented as pre-rendered still screens[^ref-12]. Interaction with the environment is performed through a simple single-cursor, point-and-click interface, similar to Myst's navigation system[^ref-12]. The game's version 2.0 patch added a "Highlight Cursor" option, with Sierra recommending players enable this feature "unless the player wants the added challenge of the inactive cursor"[^ref-13].

The interface drew criticism: before Sierra's patch it was very unclear what the player could interact with, since the detailed graphics did little to draw attention to hotspots, leaving stuck players clicking on everything on-screen.[^ref-collection] This design philosophy aligns with Sierra's traditional approach of challenging players to thoroughly explore and experiment.

### Structure and Progression

The game offers multiple paths and alternate puzzle solutions, providing various ways to experience the story and reach different endings[^ref-14]. Sierra's InterAction magazine said the endings "range from total success to total failure and, in one case, death"[^ref-28]. Unlike traditional Sierra adventures, Lighthouse does not feature a point system[^ref-15].

The game's non-linear structure allows for different approaches to puzzle-solving and story progression.

### Puzzles and Mechanics

As with Myst, puzzles are notable for their difficulty and rely more on logic than inventory management, consisting of careful observation, clue gathering, and environmental manipulation[^ref-12]. The game includes a significant underground maze section[^ref-4]; the Adventure Gamers reviewer wrote that it "takes up almost a quarter of game play"[^ref-29]. Sierra's traditional "player-hostile house style" is evident in the challenging nature of the puzzles, which can leave players genuinely lost or stranded in the bizarre world[^ref-4].

The Collection Chamber found the puzzles "a bit too obtuse" and noted that the difficulty "became a major talking point" in reviews; the game "can be brutally difficult with many unforeseeable deaths."[^ref-collection]

The version 2.0 patch modified some of the more difficult puzzles and added indicator lights in various locations to improve playability[^ref-13]. Sierra later released patches that addressed some of the game's difficulty spikes and added a much-needed hint system[^ref-4][^ref-13].

Contemporary reception was mixed. Rebecca Anderson's GameSpot review noted that "graphically, Lighthouse is, at its heart, a moving picture book," praising its visual presentation while warning "novices and wanna-be adventurers, beware" about its difficulty.[^ref-27]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been more appreciative of the game's ambitious design. Adventure Classic Gaming awarded it 4/5 stars in 2009, while Just Adventure gave it a B+ rating in 2003[^ref-19]. Adventure Gamers rated it 3.5/5 stars ("A solid adventure that is generally enjoyable, though it lacks enough polish or ambition to recommend without caution")[^ref-16], with its review's verdict reading "Entertaining and beguiling game, however, if you hate mazes steer clear"[^ref-29]. GOG.com users have rated it 4.1/5 based on 41 reviews (as of October 2026)[^ref-20]. MobyGames lists a critic average of 70% based on 28 ratings[^ref-1].

## Development

### Origins

The game's development began when Sierra's Ken Williams called Jon Bock into his office, showed him a copy of Myst, and asked "Can you do this?" Bock responded affirmatively, and the game went into development as Sierra's attempt to capitalize on Myst's success[^ref-4][^ref-2]. Sierra's InterAction magazine described Lighthouse as "Bock's first feature title"; since 1989 he had contributed art and direction to titles such as Space Quest 6, Phantasmagoria and King's Quest 7[^ref-28]. Bock himself recalls having been art director on The Island of Dr. Brain and Outpost before pushing for a full-time design role[^ref-4].

The timing was deliberate: Myst had taken the gaming world by storm in 1993, and Sierra sought to capture some of that market while maintaining their own design philosophy. Unlike pure Myst imitators, Lighthouse retained Sierra's challenging puzzle design and willingness to let players fail—characteristics that defined the company's adventure games.

### Production

The game's landscapes were created using Autodesk 3D Studio and Alias software, with models built and animated on SGI machines and motion capture handled by Biovision[^ref-4][^ref-28][^ref-7]. One of the parallel-world areas, the Roost, grew out of a role-playing module Bock had written years earlier[^ref-4][^ref-28].

The voice cast included Phil Proctor as Dr. Jeremiah Krick, Kerrigan Mahan as the Dark Being, Romy Cutler-Lengyel as Liryl, and Leigh French as Mom[^ref-23]. Marc Eckelberry voiced the Computer, and Andy Goldberg played the Editor[^ref-7]. The manual credits Brian Min as composer and Victor Crews as assistant composer, with Jay Usher as senior sound consultant[^ref-7]; InterAction instead named Usher as the team's composer[^ref-28].

Jon Bock drew inspiration from diverse sources, referring to the game as a "Science Fiction Folk Tale" that blended machine age aesthetics with fantasy elements[^ref-4].

### Technical Achievements

Lighthouse utilized Sierra's SCI (Sierra Creative Interpreter) engine[^ref-5][^ref-12] and featured pre-rendered 3D environments[^ref-12]. Sierra billed it as "the first Sierra title to have fully 3-D rendered character animation"[^ref-28], and Bock considers it "one of the first adventure games with fully rendered and motion captured characters"[^ref-4].

## Legacy

Lighthouse is still commonly discussed as Sierra's take on Myst[^ref-25][^ref-4]. The game's challenging puzzle design and atmospheric presentation have been both praised and criticized, with some noting that it "manages the awkward feat of carrying Sierra's frustrating, player-hostile house style over to this new style of adventure game"[^ref-4].

Collection Chamber's retrospective noted: "Lighthouse: The Dark Being takes the former approach and teaches you everything about how not to be a parent"[^ref-collection], highlighting the game's dark narrative themes. Despite sharing many genes with Myst, Lighthouse is very much a game fathered by Sierra[^ref-collection], blending the contemplative exploration of Cyan's masterpiece with Sierra's trademark difficulty.

Sierra's version 2.0 patch (listed by MobyGames as v2.0a, May 1997) added a hint system and highlight cursor, eased some of the harder puzzles and added visual clues such as indicator lights[^ref-13][^ref-26]. Modern players can access the game through GOG.com, where it runs on ScummVM[^ref-20]; the GOG release includes the manual and a hint book as extras[^ref-20].
