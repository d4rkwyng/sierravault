# Codename: ICEMAN

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>


## Overview

Codename: ICEMAN is a graphical adventure game made with the SCI engine and published by Sierra On-Line.[^ref-1] Sources disagree on the release year: MobyGames records a December 1989 US release for DOS, and IGDB, Wikidata and PCGamingWiki also give 1989. Wikipedia gives March 1990 (see Version History).[^ref-13][^ref-15][^ref-16][^ref-17][^ref-1] The lead designer was [[Jim Walls]], a retired California Highway Patrol officer who also designed the first three Police Quest games.[^ref-27][^ref-28] *Computer Gaming World* introduced the game as "the latest adventure by retired law enforcement officer Jim Walls."[^ref-23] The game was an ambitious attempt to combine a parser-driven adventure with detailed simulations of real-world procedures, from CPR to piloting a submarine.[^ref-2]

Set during a near-future global oil shortage, the game casts the player as naval officer Johnny Westland, who is sent on a mission to rescue a kidnapped US ambassador from terrorists in Tunisia.[^ref-1] It is known for its emphasis on realistic military procedures, which require players to consult the manual often.[^ref-3] Sierra's product description calls the game "a thrilling adventure" with "a story as chillingly authentic as tomorrow's headlines."[^ref-4]

Mention of a "Codename: PHOENIX" in Sierra promotional material suggests that ICEMAN was meant to be the first part of a series, but no sequel was released.[^ref-1][^ref-20][^ref-26] Wikipedia attributes this to disappointing sales. The 2007 *Adventure Gamers* review it cites says only that Sierra "tossed around the name Codename: PHOENIX in a few 'Coming Soon' notices," and no period sales figures have been found.[^ref-1][^ref-26] The game has since earned a notorious reputation in adventure gaming circles for its difficulty and unconventional design choices.[^ref-5]

## Story Summary

The story takes place during a future global oil shortage, when Tunisia is discovered to possess a surplus of high-grade oil.[^ref-1] While the Soviet and US governments try to acquire as much of the oil as they can, Soviet-backed terrorists kidnap a US ambassador in hopes of provoking an international incident.[^ref-1] Sierra's description sets up "a kidnapped U.S. ambassador and the only man who can save him - Codename: ICEMAN."[^ref-4]

Naval officer Johnny Westland is informed about the situation while on leave in Tahiti and is called back for the rescue mission.[^ref-1] The night before returning to duty, he meets a woman named Stacy and has a one-night stand.[^ref-1]

In the Pentagon, Westland is briefed and learns that Stacy is also an agent whom he must meet as soon as he reaches his goal.[^ref-1] To reach his target, Westland travels in a nuclear-powered submarine, the USS Blackhawk.[^ref-1] During the journey, Westland battles Soviet vessels, navigates an iceberg field, and plants bombs to penetrate the harbor surveillance of Tunisia.[^ref-1] Sierra's own product description names "Oil shortages, international politics and radical terrorists" as the backdrop for the story.[^ref-4]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Codename: ICEMAN is a text parser-based adventure game: commands must be typed, such as CLIMB UP to ascend a ladder.[^ref-1] The player moves the protagonist in eight directions in a third-person view and types text commands to interact with the environment. Typing "LOOK" on its own gives different descriptions depending on where the protagonist stands on screen.[^ref-7] *Computer Gaming World* noted that Sierra had "improved the parser control features and added more 'point and click' interaction."[^ref-21] Another CGW column singled out the "mouse interface so that one may 'point and click' rather than 'look this' and 'look that'."[^ref-22] According to the manual, the character can be moved with the arrow keys, the numeric keypad, a joystick or the mouse.[^ref-12]

One reviewer described control as easy and simple, with the num-pad (or the arrow keys) controlling movement.[^ref-3] However, the parser has been criticized for rejecting logical commands.[^ref-8] The Adventurers' Guild wrote that "There were numerous times throughout the game that I was put off the correct scent by the parser not accepting my very Sierra safe commands."[^ref-8]

### Structure and Progression

The game is divided into several distinct sections that blend adventure gameplay with simulation elements:

- **Tahiti Opening:** The player begins on leave in Tahiti. *Computer Gaming World* advised readers to read the newspaper daily and check in at the hotel's front desk.[^ref-21] *ST Format* noted that the manual's walkthrough of the opening includes "a fascinating lesson on Cargiopulmonary Resuscitation and how to get a girl drunk."[^ref-9]
- **Pentagon Briefing:** Westland receives his mission briefing and must prepare for the operation.[^ref-1]
- **USS Blackhawk Operations:** A substantial portion of the game takes place aboard the nuclear submarine, requiring the player to manage navigation and weapons systems.[^ref-1]
- **Combat and Navigation:** The player must battle Soviet vessels and navigate an iceberg field.[^ref-1]
- **Tunisia Mission:** In Tunisia, Westland meets Stacy, who is disguised as a local, and together they rescue the ambassador.[^ref-1]

Like the Police Quest series, the game relies on realistic procedures, in this case military instructions, first aid and other protocols.[^ref-7] If some of these procedures are not followed properly, the player may reach a dead end and have to restore an earlier save.[^ref-7]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The Adventurers' Guild found traditional adventure-style puzzles mainly at the beginning and end of the game, and called the rest "really just one mini-game after another."[^ref-8] The game features several unique mechanics beyond standard adventure gameplay:

**Boss Dice:** A dice game that one reviewer said "needs to be done to make life much easier when playing the game." It includes protection against save/restore cheating.[^ref-3] *Computer Gaming World*'s hints explained that the prize for winning is "an electronic device that will provide easy access to the harbor, later in the game. Yet, the clever player might find another way."[^ref-21]

**CPR Simulation:** The manual includes a section on Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR), which the player needs for an emergency early in the game.[^ref-12][^ref-9] *Computer Gaming World* wrote that the game "will also teach the adventurer something about CPR and naval etiquette."[^ref-23]

**Submarine Controls:** A considerable portion of the game is a submarine simulation, in which the player navigates the submarine and fights enemy craft.[^ref-7] CGW's Winter CES report said that "the controls are customized to facilitate game play, but it operates authentically."[^ref-23] The Adventurers' Guild wrote that "Controlling the submarine involves referring to the complicated manual instructions time and time again."[^ref-8]

The game has been heavily criticized for letting players move on after missing vital information and items, with no warning that they will be needed later.[^ref-8] Its unwinnable situations have contributed to its reputation for difficulty.[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Michael Chaut reviewed the IBM version in *Computer Gaming World* #72 (June 1990) under the headline "The Iceman Computeth!"[^ref-21] He judged that "Overall, the 'Sub Simulator' is exciting." He also wrote that "Aesthetically, the game has much to commend it," crediting lead artist Cheryl Loyd (spelled "Sheryl Loyd" in the magazine).[^ref-21] He called Mark Seibert's score "simply spectacular."[^ref-21] On the down side, he found that "the iceberg navigation sequence seemed too long and quickly became boring," and that "some of the puzzles are quite difficult."[^ref-21] He concluded that for Sierra fans or anyone who likes James Bond movies, "this is a game that you will probably not want to miss."[^ref-21]

*ST Format* #21 (May 1991) reviewed the Atari ST version and scored it 77% overall.[^ref-9] Reviewer Ed Ricketts opened with "You can instantly recognise a game by Sierra On-Line." He noted that the company had "developed a 3D adventure environment and used it in virtually all of their games."[^ref-9] He criticized the version's speed: "Simply walking across a screen seems to take an eternity, and to top it all, moving to another screen for the first time requires disk accessing - and that takes ages."[^ref-9] Still, he acknowledged that "the backgrounds are well drawn and some of the animation is nicely done - particularly when you're drowning..."[^ref-9]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have mostly been critical. *Adventure Gamers* gave the game 1.5 stars in 2007, with the verdict "For submarine simulator fans and Sierra completists only."[^ref-26] The Adventurers' Guild blog was harsh too: "It's no secret that I didn't think much of this game. It was ambitious to say the least, and offered something fresh, but in the end it was just a bit of a chore to get through to be honest."[^ref-8]

George Starostin's 2025 review for *Only Solitaire* argued that the game "represented a bold and challenging attempt to push the agenda of virtual realism in computer games even further — as far as it could substantially and technically go in 1989–1990." He added that it went "(unfortunately), even quite a bit further than that."[^ref-2] A 2010 AGDInteractive forum thread asked whether it was "Sierra's worst adventure game?"[^ref-11] Starostin countered that it "never lacked" imagination.[^ref-2]

*Time Extension* wrote that when people discuss the most difficult Sierra adventure games, "there is one name that usually comes up more than any other: Codename: Iceman."[^ref-5] The site described the game as "notorious for its challenging mini-games, unwinnable situations, and its frustrating emphasis on military and medical procedures and protocols."[^ref-5]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **ST Format:** 77% (Atari ST)[^ref-9]
- **GOG.com:** 3.1/5 (25 reviews)[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

Codename: ICEMAN was designed by Jim Walls, who had established himself at Sierra with the Police Quest series.[^ref-1][^ref-27] It was meant to be the first game in a new adventure series, and a sequel was mentioned in Sierra's news magazine but never released.[^ref-10] *Computer Gaming World* described it as "Written and designed by Police Quest creator Jim Walls."[^ref-21]

Sierra's product description introduces the game as coming "From Jim Walls, designer of two of the most exciting and realistic graphics adventure games ever written."[^ref-4] George Starostin notes that it is unclear whether the spy-thriller pitch came from Walls himself or from Ken Williams or another Sierra executive.[^ref-2]

The rumor column in Sierra's own *Sierra News Magazine* (Spring 1990) noted that the "eagerly awaited" game was late to ship. It jokingly blamed the delay on the opening of the Berlin Wall, which had "created a bigger hole in Jim's planned political thriller." The magazine's editors warned that the column should be taken "with more than a few grains of salt."[^ref-25]

### Production

The game was built on Sierra's SCI0 engine.[^ref-6][^ref-17] *Computer Gaming World* reported that Sierra had "improved the parser control features and added more 'point and click' interaction." The magazine also said the game "supports over ten (10) different sound cards."[^ref-21]

- **Development System:** [[Jeff Stephenson]], Bob Heitman, Pablo Ghenis, Stuart Goldstein (as listed in the manual)[^ref-12]

### Technical Achievements

The submarine sequence was a central selling point. *Computer Gaming World* wrote that "Although not as complex as thoroughbred simulations, this sequence offers a real-life quality due to the fact that it is interlaced within an adventure game."[^ref-21] One GameFAQs reviewer praised the detail: "I especially like the detail that went into the control panel of the submarine and various other locations around your vessel."[^ref-3]

The Adventurers' Guild found that the simulation sections demanded constant reference to the manual, and that "just when you get the hang of things, the game asks you to do something else that you have absolutely no clue how to do."[^ref-8]

### Technical Specifications

**IBM PC Version:**
- **Graphics:** 16-colour; it "supports several 16 color graphic" cards[^ref-22] (PCGamingWiki lists CGA, EGA, Hercules, Tandy and VGA modes)[^ref-17]
- **Engine:** SCI0[^ref-6]
- **Audio:** Roland, Ad Lib, IBM, CMS and Tandy sound[^ref-21]
- **Media:** Nine 5.25" disks or four 3.5" disks[^ref-22]
- **Input:** Text parser plus mouse "point and click"; keyboard, keypad or joystick movement[^ref-22][^ref-12]
- **Price:** $59.95[^ref-22][^ref-21]

### Technical Issues

The game suffered from significant speed issues on the Atari ST, with screen transitions and multiple moving objects causing noticeable slowdowns.[^ref-9] *ST Format* wrote: "If you have more than one moving object on screen at once, then everything slows down even more."[^ref-9]

The text parser has been criticized for inconsistent command recognition. The Adventurers' Guild reported being "put off the correct scent by the parser not accepting my very Sierra safe commands."[^ref-8] Its examples include failed attempts to "sit with Stacy," "get the duct tape off the fridge," and enter "the coordinates into the diver."[^ref-8]

One reviewer noted an issue with the game's internal clock: "in some situations in the game it has an inbuilt clock which speeds up along with the movement speed, therefore you may find yourself looking at a restore, restart or quit screen for no apparent reason."[^ref-3]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The title is styled several ways: CODE-NAME: ICEMAN on the front box art, Codename: Iceman on the back box art, and CODE NAME: ICEMAN on the title screen.[^ref-1]
- Winning the Boss Dice game earns a device that makes it easier to reach the harbor later. Starostin notes that the player must later remember *not* to use that gadget.[^ref-21][^ref-2]
- One reviewer, after the parser refused his attempt to take the duct tape off the fridge, wrote: "I'm still angry about the duct tape."[^ref-8]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sequel followed. Sierra had floated the name Codename: PHOENIX in "Coming Soon" notices.[^ref-26] Wikipedia says disappointing sales ended the would-be franchise, but no sales figures have been found to confirm this.[^ref-1] The game is currently owned by Activision and remains available for purchase through digital distribution.[^ref-6][^ref-4]

The game has become notorious among Sierra fans. As George Starostin put it, "if you are a child of the Eighties, chances are that Codename: ICEMAN will be one of the first titles to pop into your head at the respective call for «the worst game ever made»."[^ref-2]

### Collections

The game is available for digital purchase through GOG.com. The store tags it with descriptors including "Adventure," "Action," "Classic," "Puzzle," "Difficult," "Naval," and "Espionage."[^ref-4]

### Fan Projects

In November 2024, a fanmade prequel titled "Kidname: Iceboy" was released by developer Grayson.[^ref-5] Created in Adventure Game Studio, the game is described as "a hilarious parody of the 1989 original focusing on the early years of that game's hero, the Naval officer Johnny Westland, as he visits a submarine camp at the age of 10, in 1973."[^ref-5]

The fan game includes "a bunch of jokes that poke fun at JW's frequently naive and patriotic outlook on the world, as well as ridiculous cameos from American historical figures like the (then) US President Richard Nixon and Navy Admiral John S. McCain Jr."[^ref-5] It premiered on the Twitch stream of adventure game podcaster Jess Morrissette.[^ref-5]

### Related Publications

- **Codename Iceman Manual:** Original documentation, including the U.S.S. Blackhawk procedures and a CPR section[^ref-10][^ref-12]
- **Codename Iceman Hint Book:** Official Sierra hint book[^ref-10]
- **Codename Iceman Map:** Game map included with the packaging[^ref-10]

### Critical Perspective

Codename: ICEMAN is usually remembered as both ambitious and deeply flawed. George Starostin argued that, far from being the worst game ever made, "Codename: ICEMAN is the farthest thing from such a game, because one thing it never lacked was imagination."[^ref-2]

However, *Time Extension* observed that the game doesn't tend to command "the same amount of fondness as Sierra's other famous series like King's Quest, Space Quest, and Gabriel Knight, instead often finding itself as the butt of various community in-jokes."[^ref-5]

Codename: ICEMAN was the only game Jim Walls designed at Sierra outside the Police Quest series. He went on to design Police Quest III: The Kindred (1991) before leaving the company.[^ref-27][^ref-28] He then designed Blue Force (1993) for Tsunami Media, and later worked at Westwood Studios, where he earned credits on Blade Runner (1997).[^ref-27][^ref-28][^ref-11]
