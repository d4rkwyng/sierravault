# Conquests of the Longbow: The Legend of Robin Hood

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Conquests of the Longbow: The Legend of Robin Hood* is a graphic adventure game developed and published by Sierra On-Line in 1991, representing the second and final installment in the Conquests series following *Conquests of Camelot: The Search for the Grail*.[^ref-1] Designed by [[Christy Marx]], the game casts the player as the legendary English outlaw, whom Adventure Gamers' 2009 review called "one of the most appealing characters to ever appear in an adventure game."[^ref-8] The game is notable for the considerable amount of historical and cultural research incorporated into its setting and puzzles, with the manual listing twenty-eight volumes in its bibliography.[^ref-1]

GOG's product description calls it "Sierra's richest, most complex game yet."[^ref-3] Adventure Classic Gaming describes its design philosophy as "dramatically different" from "most other adventure games, in which losing paths (especially long and detailed losing paths) are rare."[^ref-4] By contrast, *Conquests of the Longbow* embraces multiple winning and losing paths, with "the possibilities for tragedy, callousness and folly" being "integral to the interactive story told by the game."[^ref-4] Depending on player choices throughout the adventure, the game offers four possible endings, "ranging from Robin getting hanged to a happy conclusion of his love life."[^ref-5]

Despite being released during Sierra's golden age of adventure gaming, the title remains "one of their lesser known games," often overshadowed by flagship series like *King's Quest* and *Space Quest*.[^ref-6] Adventure Gamers' 2009 retrospective called it "a true classic that deserves to be in every adventurer's collection,"[^ref-8] and one Glitchwave user comment calls it "Probably Sierra's most underrated title."[^ref-7]

## Story Summary

The year is 1193, and the Third Crusade has just ended. Richard the Lionheart, King of England, is returning to Europe from the Holy Land when he is captured by Austrian Duke Leopold and held for an enormous ransom.[^ref-5] In England, Prince John plots to seize the throne by preventing the ransom from being paid, leaving the country in turmoil. The player assumes the role of the legendary Robin Hood, living as an outlaw in Sherwood Forest, who must work to restore King Richard I to the throne of England.[^ref-10]

Robin Hood's quest involves far more than simply robbing the rich to give to the poor. He must raise money for the king's ransom while protecting Maid Marian from the machinations of the Sheriff of Nottingham, fooling the Sheriff at every turn, and even contacting forest spirits for aid.[^ref-11]

Each day brings a series of problems to overcome, and in solving them Robin uncovers a plot against King Richard.[^ref-6] The romance between Robin and Marian serves as a central motivation throughout the adventure, with one modern commentator observing that Robin's "only motivation to do anything is to find a way to sleep with Maid Marian."[^ref-9] The theme of the game is described as simple—"love and honor may conquer all"—but "en route, the twists and turns make this conquest all the more poignant."[^ref-4]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

*Conquests of the Longbow* uses Sierra's standard icon-driven point-and-click interface first introduced in *King's Quest V*, featuring an icon-based system common to most contemporary Sierra adventures.[^ref-1][^ref-5] This represented a significant upgrade from the text parser used in *Conquests of Camelot*, as the newer SCI1 engine provided 256-color VGA graphics compared to the earlier game's SCI0 engine.[^ref-9] The game supports multiple input devices including keyboard, mouse, and analog joystick.[^ref-13]

### Structure and Progression

The game takes place over thirteen days; each day is completed when certain tasks have been fulfilled.[^ref-6][^ref-24] This day-based structure creates a sense of urgency and progression while allowing players to explore the game world systematically. The design notably avoids stalemate situations—"the player's actions can always lead to an ending, even if it's an unhappy ending"—yet "particularly reckless actions can quickly close off the possibility of an ideal ending."[^ref-4]

The game structure includes:

- **Outlaw Camp:** Robin's home base in Sherwood Forest where he meets with his Merry Men
- **Sherwood Forest:** Various locations including the shooting glade, Watling Street for ambushes, and mystical groves
- **Nottingham:** The town with its market fair, castle, and various shops
- **The Fens:** Marshy monastery area
- **St. Mary's Abbey:** Location for key encounters with religious figures

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The game reduces traditional inventory-based puzzles in favor of exploration and varied tasks, incorporating combat, puzzles, and arcade sequences that can be "set to whatever degree of difficulty you like."[^ref-1][^ref-5] Designer Christy Marx incorporated medieval knowledge and lore into the puzzles, requiring players to know "what properties gemstones have or learning to identify and name druid trees."[^ref-9]

Mini-games include:
- **Combat sequences:** Quarterstaff battles and sword fighting
- **Archery contests:** Skill-based challenges at the fair
- **Nine Men's Morris:** A traditional board game[^ref-5]
- **Druid hand-code system:** A puzzle system where each phalanx represents a letter[^ref-8]

The copy protection was innovatively integrated into gameplay rather than using typical word verification, requiring players to reference the manual for information about medieval gemstones, trees, and other period-appropriate knowledge.[^ref-15]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

*Conquests of the Longbow* received strong reviews upon release, particularly for the DOS version. French gaming magazine *Génération 4* awarded the PC version 93% in December 1991.[^ref-16] *Joystick* gave it 92% the same month.[^ref-16] *ACE: Advanced Computer Entertainment* awarded a score of 985 in February 1992 (reviewer Gary Whitta).[^ref-16]

*Dragon* magazine gave the game a perfect 5 out of 5 stars, with reviewers Hartley Lesser, Patricia Lesser, and Kirk Lesser praising its quality.[^ref-1] *White Wolf* magazine's Jim Trunzo wrote in the May/June 1992 issue: "Most impressive is the game's diversity. Combat, puzzles, and arcade sequences (set to whatever degree of difficulty you like) all add to the overall feel of the product."[^ref-1]

Swedish publication *Datormagazin* awarded the PC version 4/5 in May 1992 (reviewer Göran Fröjdh) and the Amiga version 90% in September 1992 (reviewer Pia Wester).[^ref-16] *Amiga Games* gave the Amiga version 87% in November 1992.[^ref-16]

The Amiga version received notably lower scores due to technical issues with the port. *Amiga Joker* awarded just 62%, with reviewer C. Borgmeier criticizing the conversion: "ohne Harddisk ist diese PC-Konvertierung mal wieder hart an der Grenze zur Unspielbarkeit" ("without a hard disk, this PC conversion is once again on the verge of being unplayable").[^ref-17]

### Modern Assessment

Retrospective reviews have been overwhelmingly positive. Adventure Gamers awarded the game 4.5 out of 5 stars in a January 2, 2009 review by Andrea Morstabilini, who wrote: "Too often forgotten in the midst of more famous Sierra series, with its captivating plot and appealing characters, this game is a true classic that deserves to be in every adventurer's collection."[^ref-8] The review praised Christy Marx's writing as "top-notch" and called Robin "one of the most appealing characters to ever appear in an adventure game."[^ref-8]

## Development

### Origins

In Marx's own account, she "was beginning work on something different involving Greek mythology" when, with "about three Robin Hood movie projects in development at the time," Roberta Williams "dropped a couple of heavy hints about wanting a Robin Hood game." Marx agreed, and was then "left entirely alone to create the game."[^ref-15] When the idea was suggested to her, she was "delighted," as "Robin Hood has always been one of my favorite heroes, not to mention how much I love the Medieval time period."[^ref-18] Adventure Gamers' retrospective frames the switch as a commercial decision: after *Conquests of Camelot* sold less well than Sierra's main "Quest" series, Marx and Ken Williams "decided to let King Arthur go" for a hero "better suited for popular appeal at the time" thanks to the film *Prince of Thieves*.[^ref-8] Lemon Amiga's reviewer describes 1991 as a year with "an omnipresent 'Robin Hood' hype," with two Robin Hood films in cinemas.[^ref-18] The Digital Antiquarian likewise notes that the game's sales were helped by *Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves* being "the second biggest cinematic blockbuster of 1991."[^ref-25]

### Production

Development at Sierra in this era was described by Marx as "like being on the frontier during the Wild West. Nobody really knew what they were doing, and I sure as hell didn't when I joined them."[^ref-15] Marx conducted extensive research for the project into outlaws of the period and the Robin Hood ballads, contacted a museum in Nottingham that sent her historical material about Nottingham Castle, the tunnels beneath it and the Trip to Jerusalem pub, and used a book of reproduced period maps of England's shires, through which she discovered Watling Street.[^ref-15]

Marx had learned about player behavior while making her first game, *Conquests of Camelot*, watching playtesters who "would do anything in any order with no rhyme or reason." She said: "By the time I was working on Longbow, I had a more solid sense of designing and writing a non-linear story while maintaining a strong storyline."[^ref-15] For *Longbow* she wanted something "more complex and nuanced" than *Camelot*'s three win-or-lose decision points, and worked with the programmers on "a weighted point system" in which the player's choices added up to one of several endings.[^ref-15]

Some of Marx's ambitions exceeded technical capabilities. She noted: "What I wanted was beyond the technical capabilities of the time," with battle scenes ending up more simplistic than intended due to hardware limitations.[^ref-15] The game's art was "created with the brilliant palette of an illuminated medieval manuscript," resulting in what GOG's product description calls "a festival of light and color."[^ref-3]

### Technical Achievements

The game utilizes Sierra's SCI1 engine, providing 256-color VGA graphics and support for sound hardware including the Roland MT-32 and Sound Blaster.[^ref-9][^ref-13] Marx wanted music that suited the time period; the project's art director "kept pushing for a more modern sound," but she insisted on the period approach, and composer Mark Seibert "was fully in sync" with her.[^ref-15]

The original soundtrack was later re-orchestrated by Mark Seibert in summer 1992 for inclusion on the *Sierra Soundtrack Collection*, recorded at Maximus Recording Studios in Fresno, California.[^ref-21]

### Technical Issues

Sierra's official patch (LBPAT, 14.3 KB) fixed three known DOS bugs:[^ref-13]

- **1535.TEX error:** An error message that occurs if Robin orders a drink in the pub
- **Green Man loop:** A looping problem when Robin talks to the Green Man
- **Forest hiding bug:** Some players not given time to hide from the Sheriff's men in the forest the second time

Other reported issues:

- **Animation issues:** Some animations (including Robin's head) display incorrectly; addressed by NewRisingSun's unofficial patch, not by Sierra[^ref-13]
- **Day 1 bug (Amiga):** A Lemon Amiga user comment reports that in the Amiga version, giving money to the rescued peasant woman on Day 1 makes the game treat her as dead, blocking the best ending[^ref-18]
- **Graphics glitch:** John appears instead of knight's corpse, causing game crash when leaving scene[^ref-4]

According to the Speedrun.com Any% guide, if you use version 1.1 (the version distributed by GOG), "catching the pixie is nearly impossible if you run the game unthrottled."[^ref-26]

The Amiga version suffered from severe performance issues. Reviewer Steve Wilkins noted: "It's damned slooooow! On a 'naked' standard Amiga the slider bar for the speed almost appears to be sarcastic: From sleepwalking to slow-motion to slide show."[^ref-18] He found the graphics "somehow washed-out and blurred," particularly the conversation portraits.[^ref-18] *Amiga Joker* wrote that without a hard disk the conversion was on the verge of being unplayable, with eight disks to swap.[^ref-17]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The game contains numerous references to other Sierra titles and in-jokes:[^ref-4][^ref-11][^ref-12]

- **Sierra staff at the fair:** Try talking to everyone at the Nottingham Fair and you will find several Sierra programmers and employees, including the Two Guys from Andromeda (creators of *Space Quest*)
- **Political figures:** George Bush and Dan Quayle appear as NPCs at the fair
- **Daventry reference:** The rug seller's last name is Oakhurst (where Sierra On-Line was located), and he claims his rugs come from Daventry, the kingdom of the *King's Quest* games
- **Police Quest reference:** One rug features the shield of Lytton, the city from *Police Quest* 1-3
- **Larry & Patti cameo:** Characters from *Leisure Suit Larry* appear in an old Norman monastery[^ref-17]
- **Christy Marx cameo:** The game designer appears as an NPC in the Nottingham market[^ref-15]
- **Control panel secret:** Opening the control panel and hitting the Sierra button shows credits, then informs players that "no guys from Andromeda" partook in the creation[^ref-12]
- **Talking rats:** Hidden dialogue with rats in the torture chamber[^ref-4]

The game is also referenced in another Sierra title: in the VGA remake of *Space Quest 1*, the "Don't Touch" button in the escape pod sends the player to Nottingham Castle (in the original EGA version it sent them to Daventry).[^ref-15]

Designer Christy Marx included a bibliography of research sources in the game's manual, with twenty-eight volumes listed including works by J.C. Holt, Maurice Keen, and Robert Graves.[^ref-1] The manual also includes essays by Marx outlining the history of the legend and approximate dates at which different characters were incorporated into the Robin Hood legend, such as Friar Tuck and Marian in the 15th century.[^ref-1]

### Multiple Endings

The game has four possible endings, determined by what Marx called "a weighted point system" in which the player's choices add up over the course of the game.[^ref-15][^ref-1] The game keeps score in three ways: points for puzzles solved, the money collected for King Richard's ransom, and the number of Robin's outlaws still alive.[^ref-1]

**Possible Endings:**[^ref-1]
1. Robin is hanged for his crimes
2. Robin is pardoned but receives no other reward
3. Robin is pardoned and offered a post in the king's service, but denied Maid Marian's hand
4. Robin is granted a noble title and married to Maid Marian

At the end, Robin stands trial and each of his "victims" testifies about how he treated them.[^ref-18] Reviewer Richard noted: "The way in which you deal with the problems each day will have an effect on how the game ends. This feature was way ahead of its time back when adventure games were fairly linear."[^ref-6]

The maximum possible score is 7325 points across all 13 game days.[^ref-24]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No unit sales figures have been published, but the game sold well: according to The Digital Antiquarian, the spring 1992 issue of Sierra's official magazine listed it as "the company's biggest current seller, edging out Police Quest 3, Leisure Suit Larry 5, and King's Quest V."[^ref-25] The game was released alongside nearly 20 other Sierra titles in 1991.[^ref-6]

Lemon Amiga describes the Amiga version as Sierra On-Line's last title for the Amiga, although *King's Quest VI* was later ported to the Amiga by Revolution Software in autumn 1994.[^ref-18]

### Collections

The game is still sold digitally:
- **GOG.com:** Released May 4, 2017[^ref-3]

### Fan Projects

The game has an active speedrunning community, with 180 runs by 13 players listed on its Speedrun.com page as of October 28, 2026.[^ref-23] Categories include Any%, Low%, 100%, Best Ending, and a unique "13 Deaths in 13 Days" challenge.[^ref-23] As of the same date, the Any% world record is 10 minutes 50 seconds, held by speedrunner SnowyP on MS-DOS.[^ref-23]

In January 2023, the speedrunning community discovered "Snowy Skips," new glitches that further optimized completion times.[^ref-26] Fan projects have also included a rendering of the game in "4K simulated VGA", posted to Reddit's r/Sierra in September 2023.[^ref-27]

### Related Publications

- **Game Manual:** Includes essays by Christy Marx on Robin Hood history, a bibliography of 28 research volumes, and copy protection information integrated into gameplay puzzles[^ref-1][^ref-15]
- **Sierra Soundtrack Collection (1992):** Includes two orchestrated tracks from the game: "Opening Sequence" (3:27) and "The Map - The Hedge Maze - The Abbey - The Fens" (5:18)[^ref-21]

### Critical Perspective

Adventure Classic Gaming describes the game as "a dramatically different design philosophy from most other adventure games," in which "the possibilities for tragedy, callousness and folly are integral to the interactive story."[^ref-4] Lemon Amiga notes that multiple endings were "nothing to be taken for granted back in the early nineties."[^ref-18]

Some players regard it as underrated within Sierra's catalog. Glitchwave user MisTurHappy commented: "Probably Sierra's most underrated title - maybe tied with Laura Bow 2 - and I can only assume it's just because it isn't part of one of their big-name series... The freedom it gives you to play Robin Hood as anything from a virtuous outlaw to a total scumbag highwayman is pretty unique too."[^ref-7]

The game's focus on historical accuracy and cultural research also distinguishes it from typical adventure fare. Puzzles draw on medieval knowledge and lore, such as "what properties gemstones have or learning to identify and name druid trees."[^ref-9]
