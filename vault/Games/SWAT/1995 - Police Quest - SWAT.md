# Police Quest: SWAT

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Police Quest: SWAT, officially titled *Daryl F. Gates' Police Quest: SWAT*, is the fifth installment in Sierra On-Line's Police Quest series and the first entry in what would become the SWAT tactical simulation franchise.[^ref-1] Released in autumn 1995 for DOS and Windows (GameSpot's original review page and Steam both give September 30, 1995, while Wikipedia's infobox says November 1995), with a Macintosh version following in December 1995, the game dropped the adventure format of its predecessors for a full-motion-video "tactical simulation" of life on the Los Angeles Police Department's Special Weapons and Tactics team.[^ref-44][^ref-22][^ref-1][^ref-6][^ref-40] The game carried the name of former LAPD Chief [[Daryl Gates|Daryl F. Gates]], whom Sierra's own marketing called the founder of SWAT; historical accounts credit Gates with approving and building the unit around an idea first proposed by officer John Nelson.[^ref-41][^ref-46]

Sierra shot the game's video with real LAPD SWAT officers at their training facilities and on location around Los Angeles County.[^ref-40][^ref-41][^ref-7] Critics were lukewarm to harsh about its interface and gameplay, but the game sold strongly: SWAT 3 developer Jim Napier later wrote that SWAT and SWAT 2 "received only marginal reviews, they sold like crazy. SWAT alone has sold over a million units to date" (March 2000).[^ref-5]

## Story Summary

Unlike the narrative-driven adventure games that preceded it in the Police Quest series, Police Quest: SWAT eschews a traditional story structure in favor of a procedural simulation framework. Players assume the role of the newest member of the LAPD's Special Weapons and Tactics team, referred to in the game as a "SWAT-Pup."[^ref-7][^ref-8] The manual reminds players that "SWAT is founded on a team concept."[^ref-8] Sierra's producer Tammy Dargan said some of the scenarios "are based on situations D Platoon has actually encountered."[^ref-40]

The game is "completely and utterly devoid of an actual story" in the traditional sense, instead presenting players with training exercises and tactical scenarios that mirror real SWAT operations.[^ref-9] The majority of gameplay is spent in highly detailed training exercises that prepare players for actual call-ups.[^ref-10] Players must master SWAT hand signals, LASH radio commands, and tactical procedures before being deployed to active missions.[^ref-9][^ref-15]

The game features only three actual mission scenarios. Sierra's own preview described them as "Crazy" Lucy Long barricaded in her house, an armed transient holding a hostage in the Kipland Industries warehouse, and a radical militant group that has taken over Eastman Enterprises and threatens to blow it up.[^ref-41][^ref-10] The missions are randomized, playing out differently on later run-throughs.[^ref-10][^ref-11] Players can pursue different career paths within SWAT, including element leader and sniper positions, with scenarios playing quite differently depending on the role.[^ref-11] The game has no traditional ending: as one GOG forum player put it, "the game never ends, you just keep playing the same scenarios (but with randomised elements each time)."[^ref-11]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Police Quest: SWAT uses a point-and-click interface built on the same SCI2-era technology as other Sierra games of the period.[^ref-1] Its implementation drew heavy criticism: GameSpot's Ron Dulin wrote that "SWAT is burdened by one of the most cumbersome interfaces ever devised," and Computer Game Review called the interface "extremely cumbersome."[^ref-12][^ref-7] A GameSpot user reviewer complained that "it will take multiple clicks to use pretty much anything."[^ref-13] Not every critic agreed; PC Gamer listed "Good graphics, interface" among the game's highs, and Hyper called the interface "a breeze to come to grips with."[^ref-18][^ref-42]

The game uses full-motion video for its basic gameplay; GameSpot user coolermaster123 likened it to "a slower paced Dragon's Lair-style FMV game."[^ref-14] Most backgrounds consisted of scanned photographs with green-screen actors composited into the scenes, using much of the same technology employed in Police Quest: Open Season.[^ref-1] PC Games' Peter Olafson wrote that "this approach makes for a handsome, COPS-like game, but it's a mistake in terms of game play."[^ref-15]

### Structure and Progression

The game is heavily reliant on training and following strict SWAT guidelines, and is "rather unforgiving with regards to following correct procedure during missions, referred to in the game as 'call-ups'."[^ref-16] The structure divides into two primary phases:

- **Training Phase:** Shooting ranges, combat drills, sniper training (including a Data Book and rifle zeroing), and tactical room entry techniques such as "Slice the Pie."[^ref-8][^ref-13] The training never formally ends.[^ref-11]

- **Mission Phase:** Three call-up scenarios that vary on each playthrough. Players serve in different roles ranging from new recruit to element leader, with missions featuring different configurations of hostages, suspects, and outcomes.[^ref-11][^ref-15]

GameSpot's Dulin found that in the missions "the linear structure simply requires the player to execute a predetermined set of actions, but it is never quite clear what those actions are."[^ref-12] Next Generation made a similar complaint: "even though you thought you did what was right, you'll still receive a reprimand from your sergeant, with absolutely no explanation of what you did wrong."[^ref-38] Players who try to skip the opening briefing are berated by their sergeant for disrespectful and insubordinate behavior; Hyper's reviewer noted that if you skip "a boring speech by your Commanding Officer... the bastard gives you a right scolding!"[^ref-10][^ref-42]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Rather than traditional adventure game puzzles, Police Quest: SWAT focuses on tactical decision-making and procedural adherence. Players who want the sniper role must pass a sniper qualification; the manual covers the sniper's Data Book and zeroing the rifle.[^ref-8] A GameSpot user reviewer noted that the manual "outlines equations for shooting distance and the drop loss of the bullet."[^ref-13]

The game emphasizes teamwork and proper procedure above action, with violations of SWAT protocols resulting in mission failure or negative consequences.[^ref-16] Intentionally killing anyone, including fellow SWAT members or unarmed suspects, results in the Lieutenant charging the player with first-degree murder.[^ref-17] The emphasis on procedure over gameplay led PC Gamer Online's Mike Wolf to observe that "it's more like work than fun."[^ref-18]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Police Quest: SWAT received mixed to negative reviews on release, with critics crediting its authenticity while faulting its interface and execution. PC Gamer (US) awarded the game 70%, with reviewer Mike Wolf concluding that "the emphasis on procedure over gameplay brings an intriguing idea to a halt. Only wannabe police officers need apply."[^ref-18] Next Generation (issue 16, April 1996) gave the game two stars out of five, praising how much information Sierra fit into one package but concluding: "All in all, this is a decent attempt at a police simulation, but your lack of control leaves much to be desired."[^ref-38]

Computer Game Review's March 1996 review carried editor scores of 62, 61 and 80 from Frank Snyder, Ted Chapman and Scott Gehrs, and concluded "Good concept, disappointing execution," predicting that "fans of the Police Quest series will be disappointed with the new look."[^ref-7] GameSpot's Ron Dulin (posted May 1, 1996) scored it 3.7 out of 10, calling the training areas repetitive and the missions "more like restrictive puzzles than strategic simulations."[^ref-44][^ref-12]

PC Games gave the game a grade of C, with Peter Olafson writing that "Daryl F. Gates' Police Quest: SWAT is, well... something of a bust."[^ref-15][^ref-1] Olafson praised the cinéma vérité presentation ("You're right there--providing cover for the unit scouts, checking out possible hiding places, confronting suspects and hostages"), but his copy also crashed repeatedly, and he ended: "The verdict? Busted."[^ref-15]

Some magazines were warmer. PC Zone scored it 83 with a "Recommended" badge; its April 1996 buyers' guide, recapping the review from the previous issue, said Gates "makes a welcome return with what has to be the best game in the Police Quest series," faulting only its lack of light-gun support.[^ref-37] Australia's Hyper (issue 29) gave it 80% overall, with reviewer Julian Schoffel calling it "Slickly presented, entertaining and highly informative," while mocking its "self-righteous lectures."[^ref-42]

### Modern Assessment

Later user opinion remains divided. On GameSpot, coolermaster123 (2019) wrote that the game "might actually be one of the worst video games ever made" and that "the acting is on the level of a bad police television drama," while ccooke (2007) called it "one of my favourite games mainly because of its authenticy and realism."[^ref-14][^ref-19]

On IMDb, reviewer euroasiangenetic wrote that "this is a swat simulation not action game, and it's glorious" and "any mistakes I make I learn and I know now thanks to this game what it's like to be a swat officer," though the review text closes "I love this game and give it 6/10" (the review's header shows 10).[^ref-17][^ref-20] Conversely, refinedsugar's 4/10 review said that "what the game does well is treats the members of these highly skilled units with respect. Explains some of their tools and tactics," while criticizing "the mix of FMV, unfulfilling gameplay and a harsh learning curve."[^ref-17][^ref-20]

## Development

### Origins

Police Quest's creator Jim Walls left Sierra around Police Quest III; as he later wrote, "after PQ 3, circumstances developed causing me to decide it was time that I leave."[^ref-23] Gates had come to the series with Police Quest: Open Season (1993), a partnership that drew criticism at the time, including from the president of the Los Angeles Urban League.[^ref-24] SWAT reunited Gates with Tammy Dargan, who had produced and directed Open Season; in a 1995 Sierra interview she was asked how SWAT differed from "your last game with Gates, Police Quest: Open Season."[^ref-23][^ref-40]

Sierra's marketing called Gates "a game designer for Sierra" on SWAT, but the game's credits list Tammy Dargan as designer, and Wikipedia describes Gates as "a principal consultant for Sierra's SWAT series."[^ref-40][^ref-45][^ref-46] Dargan credited Gates with access to the unit: "We wouldn't have been able to have even an introduction with them had it not been for the Chief."[^ref-40]

### Production

The game was designed, directed, and written by Tammy Dargan, a former producer of *America's Most Wanted*, who co-produced it with Phy Williams.[^ref-45][^ref-40] Gates praised her research: "The fact that Tammy read LAPD's SWAT manual from cover to cover, and knows it better than I do, added a lot."[^ref-40] Dargan explained that the team chose video over animation because "when you video-capture actual SWAT officers, you are interacting with real SWAT guys."[^ref-40] Computer Game Review likewise reported that the game "contains multiple video clips that were filmed on location using actual members of the L.A.P.D. SWAT team."[^ref-7]

Sierra's Holiday 1995 InterAction said the game was "Filmed on location in actual training facilities and on city streets all over Los Angeles County with the full assistance of the LAPD and real SWAT members."[^ref-41] Studio work was done in Oakhurst: actor Scotty Wixel, who played a character sharing his name, recalled on GOG.com in 2022 that "It was filmed at a production studio located in Oakhurst CA (just outside of Yosemite National Park)," that the cast "spent several days training with actual SWAT members from the LA Police Dept," and that "it was a blast working on this video game as an actor!!!"[^ref-3]

According to the SWAT 3 postmortem, Rod Fung and Tammy Dargan both worked on the original SWAT, and in doing so "developed several contacts within the LAPD SWAT community, including Police Chief Daryl Gates."[^ref-5]

### Technical Achievements

Police Quest: SWAT was built on the SCI 2.1 engine (Sierra Creative Interpreter), the same technology family used in Police Quest: Open Season and other contemporary Sierra games like Phantasmagoria and Gabriel Knight 2.[^ref-25][^ref-1] Because of the amount of FMV data, the game shipped on four CD-ROMs.[^ref-1][^ref-15]

### Technical Issues

Police Quest: SWAT had stability problems from the start. PC Games' Peter Olafson reported: "Under DOS, it crashed every time I clicked the gunsight on an object during the first scenario and almost every time I tried to access the fourth of the four CDs. Under Windows 3.11, it crashed when I interrupted my supervisor's opening schtick, and it crashed when I didn't."[^ref-15]

Players of the GOG.com release have reported further issues:[^ref-26][^ref-27]
- At the February 2011 GOG launch, saving did not work; GOG staff advised creating ten empty folders, SWATDATA.000 through SWATDATA.009, and promised a fixed installer
- One player reported the game crashing when the alarm sounds for the second mission call-up on Windows 8

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- If players attempt to skip the opening briefing, the sergeant berates the player character for disrespectful and insubordinate behavior[^ref-10][^ref-42]
- Intentionally killing anyone results in first-degree murder charges from the Lieutenant[^ref-17]
- Due to a timing quirk, players can shoot a surrendering elderly female suspect, win the mission, and still receive a commendation[^ref-17]
- The game is listed as "Police Quest 5" (PQ5) in file names and credits, though this numbering doesn't appear in the game itself[^ref-1]; Sierra's 1996 10-K and a 1998 PC Data sales chart both list it as "Police Quest V"[^ref-30][^ref-39]
- Sierra advertising claimed that "some of the country's major police departments" used Police Quest CDs "to train their officers"[^ref-43][^ref-41]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Despite its reviews, Police Quest: SWAT was a commercial success. By March 2000 it had sold over a million units, according to SWAT 3's Jim Napier.[^ref-5] Computer Gaming World's November 1998 letters page, quoting PC Data, listed it as "Police Quest V" in third place among the year's best-selling adventure games through July 1998, with 152,425 units and $1,622,405 in revenue, behind only Myst and Riven.[^ref-39] Wikipedia, citing PC Gamer US (April 1999), reports full-year 1998 sales of 253,128 units and $2.73 million, making it the 17th best-selling game of 1998 in the United States.[^ref-1]

Sierra's 10-K for the fiscal year ending March 31, 1996 stated that it had "released five titles and sold more than 1.2 million copies" in the Police Quest series.[^ref-30] The sales of SWAT despite poor reviews helped carry the name forward into later SWAT titles; Napier wrote that "the name recognition has been great for sales."[^ref-5]

### Collections

Police Quest: SWAT appeared in several Sierra compilation packages:[^ref-2]
- **Police Quest: Collection Series** (1997) – with the earlier Police Quest games
- **SWAT Career Pack** (2000)
- **Police Quest: SWAT 1+2** (1999) – paired with the sequel
- **Police Quest: SWAT Generation** (2003)

It was not part of the 1995 *Daryl F. Gates' Police Quest Collection: The 4 Most Wanted*, which contained only the first four Police Quest games; Sierra's Fall 1995 InterAction described SWAT as still "due in the near future" when that collection launched.[^ref-32][^ref-40]

### Fan Projects

According to GOG.com forum discussions, "back in the 2000s, there were a couple of attempts at remaking Police Quest II, none ever reached completion."[^ref-31] No fan remakes of Police Quest: SWAT itself are documented in the sources consulted.

### Related Publications

- **Police Quest: SWAT Manual** – Included with game, covering installation, the interface, shooting ranges, sniper training, and the Data Book[^ref-8]

### Critical Perspective

Police Quest: SWAT sits in an unusual spot in Sierra's history: a title that sold well while drawing mostly lukewarm reviews.[^ref-5] Giant Bomb describes it as "a major design shift for the Police Quest series, moving away from adventure games and into the FMV genre that was flourishing in the mid-1990s."[^ref-16] Reviews split over whether its procedure-heavy design was a strength or a chore, from Hyper's "highly informative" to PC Gamer's "more like work than fun."[^ref-42][^ref-18]

Jim Walls, creator of the original Police Quest games, later reflected that "the payoff came with the fan mail. When the letters came in, with some of the kids saying they wanted to grow up to be cops, we knew we were on the right track."[^ref-34] Sierra pushed the educational angle in its own advertising, claiming major police departments used Police Quest CDs for training, and a GOG user reviewer of the earlier Police Quest collection repeats the claim that "many law enforcement agencies used the series as a training tool."[^ref-43][^ref-35] Neither source names a department.

The title "truly did signal the end of the franchise" in terms of adventure gaming, marking Police Quest's permanent transition from point-and-click adventures to tactical simulations.[^ref-9] SWAT 2 (1998) became a 2D real-time strategy game made by an entirely different team, and SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle (1999) brought back Rod Fung and Tammy Dargan from the original game.[^ref-5]
