# SWAT: Target Liberty

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

SWAT: Target Liberty is a third-person tactical shooter developed by 3G Studios and published by Sierra Entertainment exclusively for the PlayStation Portable in October 2007.[^ref-1] The game represents a significant departure from the first-person perspective of its PC predecessors, adopting an overhead isometric view that GamesRadar likened to Killzone: Liberation,[^ref-2] a change Sierra producer Paul Pawlicki attributed to the PSP's single analog stick and the need to command two teammates.[^ref-4] Players take command of SWAT Element Leader Kurt Wolfe as he leads his team through a series of missions across New York City, confronting gang violence and ultimately uncovering a terrorist plot that threatens nuclear devastation.[^ref-3]

As the first SWAT game on the PSP,[^ref-22][^ref-25] Target Liberty attempted to bring the franchise's trademark tactical gameplay to portable gaming while maintaining core series values like less-than-lethal weapons and suspect restraint.[^ref-4] The game was marketed as "the most authentic and realistic squad-based tactical combat shooter available for the PSP handheld system," featuring randomized AI placement, team development mechanics, and ad-hoc multiplayer functionality.[^ref-5] Development was overseen with consultation from Ken Thatcher, a 29-year Los Angeles SWAT veteran, to ensure tactical authenticity.[^ref-6]

Despite its ambitious premise and pedigree, SWAT: Target Liberty received a lukewarm critical reception, earning a Metacritic score of 50/100.[^ref-1] Critics praised the game's variety of door-entry techniques and tactical options[^ref-20] while criticizing tedious gameplay, a slow pace,[^ref-7] and poor ally AI.[^ref-17][^ref-23] The game's storyline, written by Scott Rosenbaum of television's "The Shield,"[^ref-8] was criticized as absurd.[^ref-7]

## Story Summary

The story takes place in New York City, where a Korean Triad gang is terrorizing the city, attacking citizens in broad daylight and conducting bold robberies in front of hundreds of witnesses.[^ref-9] SWAT officer Kurt Wolfe is initially assigned to take down renewed Asian gang violence led by ethnic Korean-Americans, working to restore order amid a brutal conflict between rival Korean gangs known as the Jopoks and Gangpehs.[^ref-10] The early missions establish Wolfe and his team as they navigate the dangerous streets and subways of New York, attempting to contain the escalating violence.[^ref-11]

As Wolfe and his team delve deeper into the investigation, they discover that the gang war is merely a smokescreen for something far more sinister. Terrorist forces, including a group known as the "Hammer of God," are planning to detonate a nuclear weapon on American soil and pin the blame on the North Korean government.[^ref-12] This conspiracy threatens to drive the United States to the brink of a worldwide nuclear conflict, forcing SWAT to expand their operations beyond simple law enforcement into counter-terrorism.[^ref-3]

The narrative takes players through iconic New York City locations including Central Park, Grand Central Station and Coney Island as Wolfe races against time to uncover the full extent of the terrorist plot.[^ref-4][^ref-11] Along the way, players interact with various characters and make tactical decisions about how to handle suspects—whether to use lethal force or attempt to subdue and restrain them for interrogation.[^ref-4] The story culminates in Wolfe and his team working to prevent the nuclear disaster and bring those responsible to justice, though critics noted the plot often veered into implausibility.[^ref-7]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

SWAT: Target Liberty employs an isometric diagonal-down perspective rather than the first-person view of its PC predecessors. Pawlicki explained that "with only one analog stick and two other teammates to command it was obvious that an FPS was going to be challenging," so the team shifted to an overhead view that "allows for greater visibility of the area around you and makes commanding your officers a whole lot easier."[^ref-4] The action is simplified compared to the PC SWAT titles, and each of the two squadmates has a single button permanently assigned to him.[^ref-2] Kurt is moved with the analog stick; officers are commanded through context-sensitive button presses, or sent to a spot by holding the right shoulder button and moving a reticle.[^ref-7]

The game includes a unique first-person sniper view that activates at certain points, allowing players to pick off distant threats. However, critics noted that "given the overhead perspective's limited field of vision, it was disorienting to suddenly shift to a first-person view of something that had been offscreen a second ago."[^ref-2] Players can access a cheat code entry screen by pausing the game and holding L + R + Triangle + Left, which unlocks various gameplay modifications including invincibility and infinite ammunition.[^ref-32]

### Structure and Progression

Players take command of Officer Kurt Wolfe and can select two of four SWAT team members to accompany them on every mission.[^ref-4] Before each operation, players choose their squadmates from a roster of officers with distinct nicknames including "Hollywood," "Python," "Subway," and "Gramps," each possessing unique qualities and stats.[^ref-17] These team members can be assigned to "Blue" and "Red" teams for tactical purposes during missions.[^ref-12]

Each SWAT member is unique and has their own attributes and stats, which can be upgraded after every level.[^ref-9] The experience system rewards players for subduing enemies rather than killing them, encouraging tactical restraint over lethal force.[^ref-17] Completing missions by non-lethal means, without casualties or unnecessary kills, earns points that increase the team's stats and skills.[^ref-5]

GameSpot put the campaign at about five hours,[^ref-7] while IGN's game page lists HowLongToBeat estimates of 6 hours for the main story, 8 hours with side content, and 11 hours for everything.[^ref-19] Critics noted that "the game itself is not especially hard, so you'll find yourself breezing through most of the levels without even losing a guy."[^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay revolves around tactical room clearing and suspect management. "It's usually a good idea to at least try ordering the bad guys to surrender—sometimes they'll actually lay down their arms, enabling you to cuff them and pocket a nice bonus at mission's end for doing things by the book."[^ref-2] This mechanic represents the game's attempt to maintain SWAT's traditional emphasis on procedure over pure action.[^ref-4]

Door entry is perhaps the most developed mechanical system in the game. "Without question, opening doors with style is SWAT's forte," with players having access to multiple entry techniques including using mirrors to peek inside, deploying C2 explosives for breaching, and throwing flash grenades to disorient enemies.[^ref-20] Players must assess each situation and choose the appropriate method based on the threat level and objectives.[^ref-20]

Missions involve rescuing hostages, restraining or eliminating suspects, and disarming explosives.[^ref-10] The game features randomized AI placement, meaning enemy positions change between playthroughs to encourage tactical adaptation rather than memorization.[^ref-5] A random killhouse generator builds level geometry on the fly in small, medium or large sizes,[^ref-4] and players can create their own maps.[^ref-9]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

SWAT: Target Liberty received mixed to negative reviews upon release, with critics acknowledging its ambitions while criticizing its execution. GameSpot awarded the game 5.5/10, with Kevin VanOrd declaring it "too short, too tedious, and too absurd to take seriously" and calling it "a generic shooter that is neither tactical nor especially action-packed."[^ref-7] IGN's Sam Bishop gave it 4.5/10, lamenting that "I can't help but feel like there's a good game somewhere in Target Liberty, but there's so much that's working against the overall experience."[^ref-17]

Eurogamer was particularly harsh, scoring the game 4/10 and stating: "Boot up this game and you too can journey back, way back, to a time when it was okay for videogame characters to be implausibly stupid... Unless you live in 1992, this game is highly likely to disappoint."[^ref-22] GamePro's Spenser Hall noted that "though the tactical isometric shooting game, SWAT: Target Liberty shows a lot of promise, ultimately the allied AI and some control issues hold it back from truly shining," concluding that "Target Liberty will only serve to frustrate even the most die hard of tactical shooter fans."[^ref-23]

GameZone awarded 5.0/10, with Natalie Romano writing that "SWAT: Target Liberty for the PSP could have been a really great game since it does have all the right elements in place but with so many faults this is more an exercise in frustration."[^ref-10] Game Informer gave 5/10 and declared that "Target Liberty should receive a citation for roughing up the prestigious SWAT franchise."[^ref-24] VideoGamer's Will Freeman scored it 5/10, observing that "SWAT on the PSP is in fact a game of slow methodical progress and tentative gunplay" and that "while it is hard to hate the first outing for SWAT on the PSP, enthusiasm towards this mediocre release is hard to muster."[^ref-25]

Defunct Games gave 57%, with Cyril Lachel criticizing the game as "a messy game full of small graphics, a boring story and repetitive action" where "the pace of this game is unbearably slow."[^ref-11] GameSpy's Cameron Lewis was similarly disappointed, noting "nobody said being a cop was easy, but surely it should be more fun than a dreary hike through a glorified dungeon crawl."[^ref-26] Pocket Gamer UK suggested that "instead of setting its sights on liberty, the game should have perhaps concerned itself with offering a more engaging experience."[^ref-6] The lone positive review on Metacritic came from Official PlayStation 2 Magazine UK, which scored it 80: "With 45 hours of missions to do and its PSP-specific design, this is one of the best games on the handheld."[^ref-6]

### Modern Assessment

Some PSP players have since defended the game, with one Reddit post calling it "a hidden gem of a tactical shooter."[^ref-27] That remains a minority view against its 50/100 Metacritic average.[^ref-1]

## Development

### Origins

Development was handled by 3G Studios,[^ref-1] which a present-day GameCompanies listing describes as an independent developer for mobile, console and social games.[^ref-29] Sierra producer Paul Pawlicki said the game "is in no way a port. It was built from the ground up for the PSP," and that the biggest challenge was designing for the PSP audience: "Handheld games need to deliver a somewhat more arcade-like experience and also need to be playable in short spurts."[^ref-4] It was the first SWAT game on the PSP.[^ref-22]

The decision to abandon the series' traditional first-person perspective for an isometric view was deliberate; Pawlicki noted that SWAT 2 had used a similar angle.[^ref-4] He explained the design philosophy: "We tried to keep the core SWAT values intact, like Less-than-Lethal weapons and restraining suspects, while providing a storyline and settings that allow for a few more gunfights."[^ref-4] GamesRadar's preview described the result as taking "the Killzone: Liberation route."[^ref-2]

### Production

To ensure tactical authenticity, Sierra brought back Ken Thatcher, a 29-year veteran of the Los Angeles SWAT team, as consultant.[^ref-6] Thatcher trained the development team in proper procedure, such as how to open and clear a room, approach stairs, and judge what constitutes an imminent threat; over half an hour of footage of him training officers at the Blackwater facility was included as bonus video.[^ref-4] The cast of SWAT 4 returned to voice the officers, and the previously nameless leader, voiced by the same actor, was given a name and face as Kurt Wolfe.[^ref-4] The game carries licensed Benelli, FNH and SIG weapons alongside less-than-lethal options such as a pepperball gun.[^ref-4]

The storyline was written by Scott Rosenbaum, known for his work on the acclaimed television series "The Shield."[^ref-8][^ref-13] This collaboration aimed to bring gritty, procedural crime drama sensibilities to the game's narrative, though the final product received criticism for veering into implausible territory.[^ref-7] 3G Studios' team scouted Manhattan, and some levels are based on real locations including Central Park, the Ellis Island visitors center and Grand Central Station;[^ref-4] reviewers also noted a Coney Island level.[^ref-7]

### Technical Achievements

The game featured several technical innovations designed to enhance replayability on the portable platform. The randomized AI placement system meant that enemy positions would differ between playthroughs, preventing players from simply memorizing patrol routes.[^ref-5] A random killhouse generator created level geometry on the fly for every multiplayer mode,[^ref-4] and players could create their own maps.[^ref-9]

The ad-hoc multiplayer supported up to four players in three modes that Pawlicki said "are all competitive in nature": escorting civilians while rivals try to steal them, killing as many terrorists as possible in a time limit, and a VIP variant in which other players are terrorists hunting the protected person.[^ref-4] GameZone named them Football, Rodeo Round-Up and The Great Escape.[^ref-10] The game also included unlockable FMV clips from actual SWAT training documentaries, providing real-world context for the tactical procedures depicted in gameplay.[^ref-20]

### Technical Issues

Critics consistently cited problematic ally AI as the game's most significant technical shortcoming. IGN's Sam Bishop colorfully described the issue: "Hey kids, here's a fun fact for you: did you know that the Special Weapons and Tactics division of our boys in blue is composed of cross-eyed, paint-huffing, speed-walking half-wits that refuse to take orders?"[^ref-17] GamePro found squadmates often got "stuck on obstacles, doorways, or furniture,"[^ref-23] and IGN described men who stood in place being gunned down or ignored repeated requests to subdue a suspect.[^ref-17]

GamePro criticized targeting, noting that "targeting an enemy yourself ultimately takes far too long,"[^ref-23] and GamesRadar found the overhead view's limited field of vision made transitions to the first-person sniper view disorienting.[^ref-2] The slow movement speed of characters was another common complaint, with critics noting that "the pace of this game is unbearably slow" for a handheld action title.[^ref-11]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

SWAT: Target Liberty performed poorly in commercial terms, with VGChartz estimating total worldwide sales of about 0.04 million units, almost all of them in North America (0.03 million).[^ref-31]

The game was published during a transitional period for Sierra Entertainment, then operating under Vivendi Games, which merged with Activision in July 2008 to form Activision Blizzard.[^ref-34]

### Collections

VGChartz lists a PlayStation Network version alongside the UMD release.[^ref-31]

### Fan Projects

No significant fan remakes, modifications or homebrew projects for Target Liberty are documented.

### Critical Perspective

SWAT: Target Liberty represents an interesting but flawed experiment in adapting a PC-centric tactical franchise for handheld gaming. The game arrived during a period when developers were still learning how to translate complex genres to the PSP's unique constraints, and many of its shortcomings reflect the broader challenges of portable tactical gaming in that era. The decision to shift from first-person to isometric perspective was reasonable given the hardware limitations, but the execution failed to capture the tension and procedural satisfaction that defined the PC SWAT titles.

The game's legacy is largely one of missed potential. Critics consistently acknowledged that the foundation—tactical squad management, multiple entry techniques, less-than-lethal options—was sound, but poor ally AI, repetitive mission design, and pacing issues undermined the experience.[^ref-17] As a historical artifact, Target Liberty demonstrates both the ambition of late-era Sierra Entertainment projects and the challenges of maintaining franchise identity across dramatically different platforms. It remains a curiosity for SWAT franchise completionists and PSP collectors, though a few players still call it a hidden gem.[^ref-27]
