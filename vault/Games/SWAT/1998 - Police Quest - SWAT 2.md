# Police Quest: SWAT 2

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Police Quest: SWAT 2 is a real-time tactics game released on July 11, 1998, developed by Yosemite Entertainment and published by Sierra FX.[^ref-1][^ref-19] Representing a dramatic departure from its predecessor's full-motion video interactive movie format, the game adopted an isometric view with squad-level tactical gameplay, allowing players to command individual combatants during typical SWAT crisis situations.[^ref-2] The game was the sixth installment in the Police Quest series and the second entry in the SWAT subseries, though it would be the last title to carry the Police Quest name before the series continued simply as SWAT.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

The game distinguished itself by offering dual campaigns—players could take command of an LAPD SWAT team or, unusually for the genre, lead a left-wing domestic terrorist organization called Five Eyes.[^ref-1] Sierra's own 1996 preview billed this as a "major first," letting players "delve deep into the criminal mind,"[^ref-30] and one GOG.com user review later called SWAT 2 "the only game I'm aware of with a full terrorist campaign."[^ref-4] The title blended strategic planning with real-time action, letting players build teams from a pool of 100 SWAT officers or 100 terrorists.[^ref-5]

Created with the assistance of former Los Angeles Police Chief [[Daryl Gates|Daryl F. Gates]]—billed by Sierra's InterAction magazine as "the creator of the original SWAT team in the 1960's"—the game emphasized authentic police procedures and realistic tactical situations.[^ref-6][^ref-30] Many missions were based on actual LAPD cases, including scenarios inspired by the North Hollywood shootout of February 1997 and events paralleling the 1992 Los Angeles riots.[^ref-1] The game shipped with a 206-page manual containing an extensive database on SWAT tactics and procedures.[^ref-3]

## Story Summary

Set in the near future of 1999, Los Angeles faces a threat from Five Eyes, a left-wing domestic terrorist group led by the mysterious Basho and his right-hand man Dante.[^ref-7] The organization conducts increasingly bold criminal operations throughout the city, from robberies to hostage situations, challenging the LAPD's SWAT division. Players experience this conflict from either side of the law, with each campaign offering a distinct narrative perspective.[^ref-1][^ref-2]

In the SWAT campaign, players serve as commander of the tactical unit under Police Chief De Souza, working to neutralize threats while protecting civilian lives.[^ref-1] The scoring system penalizes players who allow innocents or police officers to be killed, while rescuing hostages, recovering evidence and arresting suspects earns public confidence and, ultimately, funding for SWAT.[^ref-32] The campaign progresses from training exercises to robberies, standoffs, riots and assassination attempts, culminating in confrontations with Five Eyes.[^ref-1][^ref-8]

The terrorist campaign follows Dante as he leads Five Eyes operations, recruiting members—one mission has the group storming college classrooms—and executing criminal schemes.[^ref-2][^ref-37] Late in the story, Basho returns and he and Dante vie for control of Five Eyes, leading to a three-way shootout with SWAT.[^ref-1] In the terrorist campaign's conclusion, Dante renounces Basho's criminal ways as unviable, disbands Five Eyes, and retires with his faction to "the nearest tropical island that doesn't have an extradition treaty with the U.S."[^ref-1]

The two campaigns cover the same conflict from opposite sides: Five Eyes causes incidents while SWAT responds to stop them.[^ref-1] Key characters include Sergeants Markossian and Alvarez on the SWAT side, and Basho and Dante on the terrorist side.[^ref-1] The storyline also features a corrupt Mayor Preston; some MobyGames contributors believe the character was inspired by Chief Gates's clashes with real LA Mayor Tom Bradley around the 1992 riots, while others think it is coincidence.[^ref-35]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

SWAT 2 employs a diagonal-down isometric perspective, presenting a tile-based world where players issue orders from a three-quarter overhead view.[^ref-9] The interface relies heavily on mouse control for selecting units and issuing commands, though the system received significant criticism for being clunky and poorly explained.[^ref-10] Players must manage multiple officers simultaneously, issuing movement commands, coordinating entries, and directing tactical responses in real time.[^ref-11]

The command system allows players to select individual units or groups and assign them actions such as Crouch, Challenge, Arrest, Rescue, Search, Disarm, Cover and Entry, with an Initiate button to execute several orders at once.[^ref-12] However, reviewers noted that officers rarely follow proper police procedure unless the player directs them, requiring constant micromanagement.[^ref-11] One MobyGames player review notes that SWAT officers, unlike terrorists, would not open a door on their own, and that selected officers who come under fire do not react without explicit orders.[^ref-36]

### Structure and Progression

The game features 30 scenarios total, divided equally between the SWAT and terrorist campaigns with 15 missions each.[^ref-11] The SWAT campaign opens with two training missions followed by 13 real assignments involving family disputes, riots, botched bank robberies, terrorist bombings and political assassinations:[^ref-12]

- **Training Missions:** Introduction to basic commands, movement, and engagement rules
- **Standard Callups:** Hostage situations, armed suspects, domestic disputes
- **Major Incidents:** Bank robberies, riots, coordinated terrorist attacks
- **Final Operations:** Large-scale confrontations with primary antagonists

Missions are relatively brief, with average scenarios taking 1-5 minutes, some extending to 8-9 minutes, and the entire game completable in under 3 hours.[^ref-11] A CheatCC strategy guide explains that it cannot give full mission walkthroughs because "no mission is alike and A.I changes in each mission, sometimes you may be able to resolve the mission peacefully whereas sometimes there may be a huge firefight with significant casualties."[^ref-8]

Between missions, players manage their roster by selecting team members from a pool of 100 characters per faction.[^ref-5] Budget management plays a role: overspending on elements and expensive gear can cost the player his job.[^ref-12] PC Gamer's reviewer found an exploit—each non-assigned officer carries a pistol that can be sold for $600, and stripping them all raised his budget "by nearly $50,000," effectively bypassing the budgeting system.[^ref-12]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The tactical gameplay emphasizes authentic SWAT procedures, including coordinated entries, use of flashbang grenades, establishing perimeters, and managing rules of engagement.[^ref-6][^ref-1] Computer Gaming World noted that "SWAT 2's cops place their emphasis on preserving life": even shooting a threatening suspect is a neutral event for scoring, while rescues, evidence and arrests are what count.[^ref-32]

Evidence collected in SWAT missions fills in the story; the more evidence gathered, the more complete the campaign's final briefing.[^ref-8] However, missions end automatically within seconds once all suspects and civilians are dealt with, sometimes before players can gather all evidence.[^ref-36] Equipment selection before missions proves critical, as some scenarios don't inform players of special equipment needed until the scenario starts.[^ref-11]

The SWAT campaign provides access to different equipment than the terrorist campaign, with SWAT teams using MP5 submachine guns as their primary weapon while terrorists employ LR 300 rifles.[^ref-14] Breaching tools, gas masks, grenades, bombs and body armor can be carried into missions.[^ref-1]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Police Quest: SWAT 2 received mixed reviews upon release, with critics praising its unique premise while criticizing interface and AI issues. The game earned a GameRankings aggregate score of 70%; Wikipedia describes the reviews as "average" and "much more positive than the previous two games."[^ref-1]

GameSpot awarded the game 5.7/10, with reviewer Scott Krol stating that "SWAT 2 could have been filled with nail-biting intensity and adrenaline-pumping action; instead, it's composed of a frustrating command system and messy gameplay."[^ref-11] He concluded that "the designers of SWAT 2 really missed the boat by choosing to use real time. As a turn-based game it could have been a winner."[^ref-11]

IGN's Jason Bates gave the game 7/10, offering a more balanced assessment: "At first glance, SWAT 2 looks like a pretty bad game. The graphics are dated, the characters on screen are miniscule and indistinct, and the control is clunky and poorly explained. [...] But if you take the time to learn the interface, look past the dated graphics and get into the design of the game itself, you'll be pleasantly surprised by what you find. SWAT 2 is a good little strategy game."[^ref-10]

Computer Gaming World's Dennis McCauley gave it 3.5 out of 5 stars in the November 1998 issue under the headline "Despite Flaws, SWAT 2 Is the Best Police Game in Years." He praised its "authentic police tactical flavor" but criticized veteran officers who "act like raw recruits," concluding: "Although its flaws are significant, SWAT 2 is the best law-enforcement sim yet."[^ref-32]

PC Gamer US was notably harsh, with Stephen Poole scoring the game at 52% in the November 1998 issue. He wrote that "SWAT 2's real-life setting and emphasis on saving lives is undeniably a breath of fresh air" but that "numerous flaws ultimately keep it from being the compelling experience gamers were hoping for."[^ref-12] He concluded: "With the excellent Rainbow Six now available, though, SWAT 2 is relegated to a second-place finish -- and not even a close one at that."[^ref-12]

Computer Games Strategy Plus's Ryan Brush (September 4, 1998) rated it 2.5 stars, writing that "SWAT 2 has some nifty features but they are offset by an ill-fitting real-time implementation."[^ref-33]

GameRevolution provided one of the most positive reviews, with Nebojsa Radakovic praising the atmosphere: "Yosemite Entertainment did a great job creating the proper feel and atmosphere for this game... I cannot rave about this game enough!"[^ref-15] The publication awarded an A- grade.[^ref-1]

Game Informer scored the game 6/10 in July 1998.[^ref-1] PC Gamer UK gave 68% in 1999.[^ref-1] Germany's PC Joker reviewed it in its 8/98 issue at 79%.[^ref-34] AllGame's Peter Suciu praised the realism, claiming "it is so realistic that the game may be used as part of future SWAT training!"[^ref-6]

### Modern Assessment

Modern assessments recognize SWAT 2 as an ambitious but flawed tactical game. MobyGames user Kasey Chang offered a retrospective view: "Too far ahead of its time to be fun, even with the simplified procedures... SWAT2 manage to wound itself with its sheer weight of SWAT authenticity. It is neither a game nor a simulation, but some sort of weird hybrid."[^ref-9]

The game maintains a following among tactical gaming enthusiasts. Steam reviews show 78% positive ratings from 113 reviews.[^ref-5] GOG.com users rate the SWAT 1+2 bundle 3.6/5 from 43 reviews, with some praising the terrorist campaign's uniqueness.[^ref-4]

Giant Bomb's wiki notes that "the game was overall better received than Police Quest: SWAT, offering a new take on the franchise, but fell due to clunky unit management, glitchy AI and too short campaign."[^ref-3]

## Development

### Origins

The previous game, Police Quest: SWAT (1995), had been a full-motion video interactive movie that sold close to one million units.[^ref-13] Sierra's InterAction magazine previewed the sequel in its Fall 1996 issue, pictured Gates with "SWAT Co-designer Susan Frischer," and promised 30 missions "taken from actual LAPD SWAT call-ups," AI "personalities" that differ every time, a game-speed slider and support for user-created scenarios.[^ref-30] Sierra then unveiled SWAT 2 as a real-time strategy game at E3 the following year, but it took more than a year to reach stores.[^ref-32]

The game was developed by Yosemite Entertainment, Sierra's studio in Oakhurst, California.[^ref-19] Its squad-level isometric design is often described as being "in the mold of the classic X-Com or Jagged Alliance games."[^ref-14]

### Production

Susan Frischer was the game's lead designer and is also credited with its story, dialogue and voice direction.[^ref-1][^ref-21] Sierra's official site noted her "personal rapport with Daryl Gates and several LAPD SWAT officers."[^ref-19] The credits thank members of the LAPD and Fresno SWAT teams, and LAPD personnel modeled for the officer photographs; Officer Bob Kain lent his likeness to the default sniper character Mark Emmet.[^ref-21][^ref-3] Kain would later become a runner-up on the Combat Missions reality television show.[^ref-3]

Former LAPD Chief Daryl F. Gates is credited as content consultant.[^ref-21] Sierra's 1996 preview promised "on-line hints from former LA Chief Daryl Gates himself," and MobyGames notes that, as in Police Quest: Open Season, Gates shows up from time to time to give in-game hints.[^ref-30][^ref-35] His association with the series had drawn criticism since Open Season's release in 1993, when the president of the Los Angeles Urban League said Gates "embodies all that is bad in law enforcement—the problems of the macho, racist, brutal police experience that we're working hard to put behind us."[^ref-20]

The game went gold on July 4, 1998, and shipped July 11, 1998, becoming the first title released under Sierra's new FX brand.[^ref-19] The demo logged more than 150,000 downloads in its first three weeks.[^ref-19]

Before release, Sierra's InterAction magazine had a group of police officers play the game—six members of the Fresno SWAT unit and one from LA SWAT. "This game makes you think, right down to the tactics that you're going to use in positioning your men," said Officer Richard Massa. "It's not a shoot-'em-up business, it's a life-saving business."[^ref-31]

### Technical Achievements

The game's AI was designed to vary encounters. InterAction noted that "the artificial intelligence behind SWAT 2 changes the parameters in each scenario you return to. What works once might not work again."[^ref-31] Computer Gaming World found replay value "good but not great": suspects may behave differently and appear in new locations, but the same number of opponents and obstacles return.[^ref-32]

The isometric engine portrays urban, suburban and rural terrain, with settings including convenience stores, warehouses, banks, office buildings, airports and parks.[^ref-32][^ref-6] Multiplayer supported up to four players over serial/null modem, modem, IPX or TCP/IP, with free Internet play through WON, and a terrain editor let players build their own multiplayer maps.[^ref-12][^ref-32]

### Cut Content

TV Tropes notes that some unused voice lines of the chief giving a debriefing contain sarcastic insults aimed at journalists, such as: "Today, an innocent died. We could have prevented that death. Remember, even a newsperson is worthy of our protection."[^ref-2] The finished game also takes shots at the news media, portraying them in one mission as anti-police and recklessly encouraging people to go to a riot.[^ref-2]

### Technical Issues

Reviewers reported a range of bugs and interface problems:

- Suspects or hostages who reach the edge of the map simply escape, and pursuing SWAT members cannot follow[^ref-32][^ref-33]
- There is no police perimeter, even though patrol cars are visible on the map[^ref-32]
- SWAT officers would not automatically open doors, unlike terrorist units[^ref-36]
- Selected officers failed to react when fired upon without explicit commands[^ref-36]
- Missions ended automatically upon objective completion, sometimes before evidence collection[^ref-36]
- Multi-story buildings could only have one floor occupied at a time[^ref-33]
- Terrorist escape vehicles are effectively invincible[^ref-33]
- Audio and text reports played simultaneously, making them nearly impossible to interpret[^ref-12]
- The final SWAT mission has an invisible exit from the building[^ref-37]

One Amazon customer review also complained of officers who "try to walk through a wall instead of around it."[^ref-29]

Compatibility issues on modern systems:[^ref-22]
- Very low FPS (game coded to run slowly)
- Stability issues with high resolutions
- LAN play uses deprecated serial modem connections only

Individual Amazon customer reviews also report no sound under Windows 7 and cut-scene videos that show only a colored box (with working audio) under Windows Vista.[^ref-29]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Sonny Bonds:** The protagonist of the original Police Quest series (1987-1991) appears as a selectable SWAT officer with excellent stats, providing a direct connection to the franchise's adventure game roots.[^ref-9][^ref-2]
- **Sierra Employee Photos:** The Five Eyes terrorist roster used photographs of actual Sierra employees, including Lori and Corey Cole, creators of the Quest for Glory series.[^ref-2]
- **SWAT Truck:** According to TV Tropes, the manual explains that the SWAT truck was bought from the Department of Energy for a single dollar and had a smiley face painted on its battering ram.[^ref-2]
- **Theme Song:** "Just Another Day in L.A." includes clips of actual cross talk and police dispatch recorded during the 1997 Hollywood bank robbery.[^ref-19]
- **Composer's Driving Record:** Chance Thomas reportedly received 13 traffic tickets in a single year while driving "a bright red, Mitsubishi 3000 GT VR-4 Twin Turbo."[^ref-19]
- **Vocalist's Day Job:** Lead vocalist Randall Porter worked as a Probation Officer for the State of Utah.[^ref-19]
- **Budget Exploit:** Non-assigned officers carry fancy pistols that can be sold for $600 each, allowing players to raise their budget by nearly $50,000.[^ref-12]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Police Quest: SWAT 2 was a commercial success.[^ref-1] During its debut week of July 12-18, 1998, Sierra reported that the game "shot pass [sic] titles such as GT Interactive's Unreal and Blizzard's Diablo to debut in the number 4 slot" on the Top PC Games Sales list, earning "the honor of highest-ranking newcomer" (PC Data figures).[^ref-19]

By late 1999, the game had surpassed 400,000 copies sold.[^ref-1][^ref-13] In a November 1999 interview, SWAT 3 producer Rod Fung cited that figure alongside the first game's close to one million units, saying the series "has been really good to Sierra in general" and that there were "huge expectations" for SWAT 3.[^ref-13] The franchise continued with SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle (1999) and SWAT 4 (2005).

### Critical Perspective

Police Quest: SWAT 2 occupies a curious position in gaming history. As a tactical game emphasizing realistic police procedures and the preservation of life, it offered a different perspective from most action-oriented titles of its era; PC Gamer called its "emphasis on saving lives" "undeniably a breath of fresh air."[^ref-12] The dual campaign structure—particularly the ability to play as terrorists—was remarkably bold for 1998. As one GOG reviewer noted, "SWAT 2 is notable for being the only game I'm aware of with a full terrorist campaign."[^ref-4]

Computer Gaming World saw the scoring system's emphasis on preserving life as "an excellent simulation of the practical political realities of modern law enforcement."[^ref-32] The same idea carried into the sequel: of SWAT 3, producer Rod Fung said "you're actually rewarded in the game for saving lives and not taking lives."[^ref-13]

However, SWAT 2's ambitious design was undermined by technical limitations and interface problems. The comparison to Rainbow Six, released the same year, was unflattering: PC Gamer concluded that with "the excellent Rainbow Six now available," SWAT 2 was "relegated to a second-place finish."[^ref-12]

The game's transition from the Police Quest adventure format to real-time tactics marked a turning point for the franchise. After SWAT 2, the series dropped the Police Quest name entirely, continuing as SWAT 3 and beyond.[^ref-3] SWAT 2 was released as Sierra, then part of Cendant Software, was reorganizing its titles into new brands; the Sierra FX label grouped the high-profile games developed by Oakhurst-based Yosemite Entertainment.[^ref-28][^ref-19]
