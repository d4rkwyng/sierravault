# Quest for Glory: Shadows of Darkness

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview
Quest for Glory: Shadows of Darkness is a 1993 adventure game/role-playing video game hybrid, the fourth installment in the Quest for Glory series by [[Sierra On-Line]][^ref-1][^ref-2].

Designed by [[Lori Ann Cole]] and [[Corey Cole]], it was the first and only game in the series to drop the roman numerals from the title[^ref-27].

Set in Mordavia, a world described as "a mix of Slavic folklore and Lovecraftian horror," the game features darker themes while maintaining the series' trademark humor[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-7].

Released in December 1993 on floppy discs and re-released on CD-ROM in September 1994, the CD version featured full voice acting including John Rhys-Davies as the Narrator[^ref-1][^ref-4].

According to Corey Cole, the game was developed with a budget of $750,000[^ref-1].

The floppy disc version was released with inadequate testing and is considered "almost unplayable," though the CD re-release addressed many issues[^ref-1].

## Story Summary
Shadows of Darkness follows directly from the events of Quest for Glory III: Wages of War[^ref-1]. Drawn without warning from victory in Fricana, the Hero arrives without equipment or explanation in the middle of the hazardous Dark One Caves in the distant land of Mordavia, a world full of undead that is "a mix of Slavic folklore and Lovecraftian horror"[^ref-1][^ref-3].

Upon escaping from the closing cave mouth, the Hero meets a mysterious young woman named Katrina who assists him several times in his journey[^ref-1]. The Hero helps the townspeople with their problems and encounters several old foes, including the not-quite-dead Ad Avis and the ogress Baba Yaga, while making several bizarre new allies[^ref-1].

The Hero is ultimately coerced into assisting Ad Avis' Dark Master in collecting the Dark Rituals that will allow Avoozl the Dark One (an obvious Cthulhu pastiche, and most likely a reference to the Slavic deity Chernobog) to manifest in Mordavia's world[^ref-1][^ref-3]. Naturally, the Hero escapes this control and thwarts their plan[^ref-1]. During the celebration of the Hero's somewhat pyrrhic victory, the wizard Erasmus appears, summoning the Hero to the land of Silmaria[^ref-1].

> [!warning]- Story Spoiler
> The climax sees Ad Avis destroyed in the final confrontation[^ref-1].

## Gameplay

The gameplay continued with Quest for Glory III's graphical, point-and-click interface[^ref-1]. The game introduced a new combat system with a sideways perspective of fights and allowed players to check an option to let the computer fight the battles automatically[^ref-1][^ref-3].

### Interface and Controls
- Point-and-click interface continuing from QFG III[^ref-1]
- New side-scrolling combat perspective[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- Option for automatic computer-controlled combat[^ref-1]
- Graphics: Wikipedia says the game "was developed with SVGA graphics"; PCGamingWiki and Sierra Fandom describe it as VGA[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-5]

### Structure and Progression
- Set in the distant land of Mordavia, plagued with undead, "a mix of Slavic folklore and Lovecraftian horror"[^ref-1][^ref-7]
- Day-night cycle with time-sensitive events[^ref-3]
- The Hero can be a fighter, a thief, a magic user or a paladin[^ref-19]
- Character import from previous Quest for Glory games[^ref-19]

### Puzzles and Mechanics
- Notable Tarot card sequence using imagery from the Russian tarot of St. Petersburg[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- Dark Rituals collection as main quest progression[^ref-1]
- Vampiric rabbits reminiscent of Monty Python and the Holy Grail[^ref-1]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews
A short product blurb in Computer Gaming World's "Taking A Peek" section (March 1994) said, "Offering a unique mix of dark mystery and light humor, Shadows of Darkness is another award winning adventure"[^ref-1][^ref-32]. The magazine's full review, by Scorpia in April 1994, was less positive: she liked the automatic combat option but found the puzzles ranged "from weak to obscure", and called the end game, with the Hero face-to-face with the villain, "a letdown"[^ref-1][^ref-33]. She especially criticized the bugs, saying the game "may well be the sloppiest product ever released by Sierra" and that Sierra had "tainted their near sterling reputation" by "releasing it prematurely for 'financial reasons'"[^ref-33]. Her verdict: "Overall, then, Shadows of Darkness was a disappointment"[^ref-33].

James V. Trunzo reviewed the game in White Wolf #43 (May 1994), giving it a "Very Good" evaluation and stating "If you simply want some challenging fun presented in a gothic setting, try the newest Quest for Glory"[^ref-1]. The game was rated K-A (Kids to Adults) by the ESRB[^ref-19].

### Modern Assessment
Rowan Kaiser of Engadget and Ryan Stevens of GameTrailers consider it the best entry of the entire series; Kaiser called it "the clear best of the bunch"[^ref-1][^ref-7]. Michael Baker for RPGamer considers it "worth the money even twenty years on," scoring it 4 out of 5 stars[^ref-1][^ref-8]. Adam Rosenberg of G4TV considers Shadows of Darkness "the most elaborate and well-designed" entry in the series[^ref-1]. PC Gamer's Richard Cobbett considers the game "absolutely wonderful"[^ref-1][^ref-9].

In 2011, Adventure Gamers named Shadows of Darkness the 23rd-best adventure game ever released[^ref-1]. IMDb users rate it 8.6/10 (233 votes, as of October 2026)[^ref-4]. MobyGames critics average 76% (16 reviews)[^ref-19]. The CRPG Addict gave it a final rating of 47, ranking it in the top 8% of the games he had rated at the time[^ref-20]. HowLongToBeat reports the main story takes approximately 13½ hours to complete, with completionist runs taking about 22½ hours (as of October 2026)[^ref-10]. On Speedrun.com the fastest Any% run is about 15 minutes, and Any% No Major Skips about 22 minutes[^ref-21].

## Development

### Production
Quest for Glory IV features darker themes while maintaining the humor of previous games through methods like incorporating Boris Karloff and Peter Lorre parodies[^ref-1]. The game was inspired by gothic fiction, "old horror movies and books about vampires and werewolves"[^ref-1]. Development ran late, forcing Sierra to ship the floppy version with inadequate testing[^ref-1]. The CD version released the following year was much improved[^ref-1]. The GOG digital re-release has since resolved many remaining bugs[^ref-1].

The CD-ROM version was the first game in the series to feature voice actors, including John Rhys-Davies as the Narrator (whose recording took more than three weeks, leading him to call the game the "CD-ROM from Hell"), Jennifer Hale as Katrina (her first video game voice role), and Bill Farmer as Leshy[^ref-1][^ref-4].

### Technical Achievements
- First Quest for Glory game with full voice acting (CD version)[^ref-1]
- Graphics: Wikipedia says SVGA; PCGamingWiki and Sierra Fandom describe VGA[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-5]
- New side-scrolling combat system[^ref-1]
- Development budget of about $750,000, not counting about $2 million Sierra spent developing its SCI engine[^ref-1][^ref-29]
- Original soundtrack by Aubrey Hodges with reprises of Hero's Theme and Grieg's "Anitra's Dance"[^ref-1]
- Platform releases: December 1993 (floppy DOS), September 1994 (CD DOS/Windows)[^ref-1]

### Slavic Folklore and Lovecraftian Influences

The game draws heavily from Eastern European mythology and cosmic horror[^ref-23]:
- Mordavia is inspired by Slavic folklore, Gothic fiction, and "old horror movies and books about vampires and werewolves"[^ref-1]
- The Dark One Avoozl is described as a Cthulhu pastiche, likely referencing the Slavic deity Chernobog[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- Baba Yaga, a recurring series villain, is based on the Slavic witch from folklore[^ref-1]
- The game incorporates Boris Karloff and Peter Lorre parodies while maintaining humor[^ref-1]
- Tarot card sequences use imagery from the Russian tarot of St. Petersburg[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- Vampiric rabbits reminiscent of Monty Python and the Holy Grail appear[^ref-1]

## Trivia

- This was the only game in the Quest for Glory series to drop the Roman numerals from its title[^ref-27]
- The Dark One Avoozl is an obvious Cthulhu pastiche, likely referencing the Slavic deity Chernobog[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- The game's vampiric rabbits are reminiscent of Monty Python and the Holy Grail[^ref-1]
- The game incorporates Boris Karloff and Peter Lorre parodies to maintain humor despite its dark themes[^ref-1]
- Tarot card sequences in the game use imagery from the Russian tarot of St. Petersburg[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- The floppy disc version was so buggy that Scorpia of Computer Gaming World called it perhaps "the sloppiest product ever released by Sierra"[^ref-1][^ref-33]
- Voice actor John Rhys-Davies (known for Indiana Jones and Lord of the Rings) took more than three weeks to record his narration and called the game the "CD-ROM from Hell"[^ref-1][^ref-34]
- Jennifer Hale (Mass Effect's Commander Shepard, among many other roles) voiced the character Katrina; it was the first video game in which she voiced dialogue[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-35]
- The development budget was about $750,000, the same as Quest for Glory III's[^ref-29]
- The Paladin is a bonus character type available only to players who have completed one of the first three Quest for Glory games[^ref-30]
- Katrina, a mysterious young woman who repeatedly helps the Hero, is a central figure of the story[^ref-1]
- Bill Farmer, best known as the voice of Goofy, voiced the character Leshy[^ref-1][^ref-4][^ref-36]
- Jim Cummings (Winnie the Pooh, Darkwing Duck) voices Boris Stovich[^ref-4][^ref-28][^ref-37]
- Quest for Glory II ends with an advertisement for the "upcoming" game under the title Quest for Glory III: Shadows of Darkness[^ref-1][^ref-27]
- The game's atmosphere was directly inspired by gothic fiction and "old horror movies and books about vampires and werewolves"[^ref-1]

## Legacy

Several modern critics, including Engadget's Rowan Kaiser and GameTrailers' Ryan Stevens, consider Shadows of Darkness the best entry in the series[^ref-1][^ref-7], and Adventure Gamers ranked it the 23rd-best adventure game of all time in 2011[^ref-1]. The game's blend of Slavic folklore and Lovecraftian horror gave it a much darker tone than earlier entries in the series[^ref-1][^ref-7]. The title change (dropping the "IV") was the only time in the series this occurred[^ref-27].

The troubled floppy release, which Scorpia called perhaps "the sloppiest product ever released by Sierra", was followed a year later by a much-improved CD version[^ref-1][^ref-33].
