# Lords of Magic: Special Edition

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 29, 2026</small>

## Overview

Lords of Magic: Special Edition is a fantasy strategy game that combines turn-based exploration with real-time combat and resource management,[^ref-1] set in the world of Urak.[^ref-3] Released in September 1998 as an enhanced version of the original Lords of Magic (December 1997), the Special Edition addresses numerous bugs and shortcomings while adding substantial new content including the Legends of Urak Quest Pack, a lord editor, a map editor,[^ref-6] 8 new buildings, 17 new monsters, and 5 new storylines based on legendary characters.[^ref-2] The game was developed by Impressions Games and published by Sierra On-Line, and GOG's store page describes it as "a fine mix of the best qualities from Heroes of Might and Magic and Lords of the Realm."[^ref-3]

The game tasks players with defeating Balkoth, Lord of Death, who has unleashed a nightmare of war and terrorism across Urak after a thousand years of peace.[^ref-3] Players can choose from eight different faiths—Life, Death, Order, Chaos, Air, Earth, Fire, and Water—each with distinct strengths, weaknesses, and over 160 different spells and 90 artifacts to discover.[^ref-5] Chris Beatrice, general manager of Impressions, declared that "Lords of Magic Special Edition is the game we always wanted to make. The game ties together all of the rich diverse elements that make up classic high fantasy and weaves from them a wonderful, different story every time you play."[^ref-6]

## Story Summary

In the fantasy world known as Urak, supporters of eight different religions once coexisted in relative peace for a thousand years.[^ref-5] This era of tranquility was shattered when forces of darkness, led by the evil sorcerer Balkoth, unleashed a devastating campaign of war and terrorism that destroyed the people and laid waste to the land.[^ref-3] Once magnificent cities now lie in ruin, and the Great Temples devoted to learning have been overrun by Balkoth's worshippers.[^ref-8]

The people of Urak cry out for a savior—a champion to drive away the forces of evil and restore peace to their shattered world.[^ref-9] Players assume this role, choosing a character and a religion, training heroes, and building armies to crush Balkoth and his Death cult while managing the cities and resources that support their forces in the field.[^ref-2] The main goal of the game is to defeat Balkoth, Lord of Death, by any means necessary, whether through military conquest, diplomatic maneuvering, or strategic alliance.[^ref-5]

After completing the game once by defeating Balkoth, players unlock a new campaign option allowing them to begin a new game as Balkoth himself, with the goal of conquering all of Urak.[^ref-5] The Legends of Urak Quest Pack included in the Special Edition offers five additional storylines based on legendary characters including Merlin, Beowulf, and Siegfried, expanding the narrative possibilities of the base game.[^ref-8]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Lords of Magic: Special Edition is described as a "hybrid RPG/Strategy game" that combines multiple gameplay systems.[^ref-10] The game operates on two distinct levels: a turn-based strategic map where players maneuver armies and manage resources, and real-time tactical combat when forces engage the enemy.[^ref-1] The interface requires significant learning investment—as one review noted, "Lords of Magic is complex, and attempting to play without reading the 100 pages of instructions will result in a big headache."[^ref-11]

The game controls primarily via mouse and keyboard, with the strategic layer featuring a bird's-eye and isometric perspective.[^ref-12] Combat transitions to real-time battles where players can control individual units, though PC Gamer's review of the original called the combat system "awkward",[^ref-18] and GameSpot's Special Edition review found it still "extremely difficult to target the enemy units" at close quarters.[^ref-13] One notable technical limitation is that the game cannot run in any resolution other than 640x480, nor can it be windowed.[^ref-7]

### Structure and Progression

Players begin by selecting one of eight faiths (Air, Earth, Fire, Water, Life, Death, Order, or Chaos) and choosing their Lord type from three classes: Warrior, Mage, or Thief.[^ref-14] Each faith has distinct characteristics, with unique military units, magical creatures, spells, and strategic advantages that fundamentally alter the gameplay experience.[^ref-5]

The game features extensive character progression with different maximum experience levels for different unit types:[^ref-10]
- **Military Units:** Maximum level 5
- **Knights:** Maximum level 6
- **Champions:** Maximum level 10
- **Lords:** Maximum level 12
- **Magical Creatures:** Maximum level 15

Armies consist of three groups, with each group limited to one Champion and three Units.[^ref-10] Legendary Creatures represent the most powerful summons but can only be called forth once per game (except Water's Giant Spiders, which can be summoned in any number and come in groups of two), adding strategic weight to their deployment.[^ref-10]

### The Legends of Urak Quests

The Special Edition includes five Legends of Urak quests: Scepter of the Lich, Dragon's Hoard, The Eternal Flame, The Holy Grail, and Siegfried and Brunhilde.[^ref-14] GameSpot's review describes one in which the player, as a Death Mage, must "find and recover a sacred scepter for your lord, the King of Darkness," and another based on Arthurian legends.[^ref-13]

A GameFAQs walkthrough reproduces the difficulty and time estimates from Sierra's official game website, listing the quests by faith:[^ref-15]

| Quest | Difficulty | Estimated Time |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| Fire | Medium | 4–7 hours |
| Siegfried | Medium-Hard | 5–9 hours |
| Death | Hard | 5–9 hours |
| Order | Hard-Expert | 30–50+ hours |
| Earth | Expert Only | 30+ hours |

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Resource management forms a core gameplay pillar, with players balancing gold, ale, and crystals to maintain their empire and armies.[^ref-13] The diplomacy system allows players to forge alliances, negotiate with other faiths, or pursue conquest—"You can be a peacemaker, warmonger, backstabber, or whatever you like to be called."[^ref-17] 

Building management occurs across 8 distinct capitals that players vie to control through conquest or allegiance.[^ref-8] Up to 80 different kinds of units can be raised, providing substantial variety in army composition and tactical options.[^ref-11] The game features over 160 different spells and 90 different artifacts to discover and utilize.[^ref-5]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Lords of Magic and its Special Edition received mixed to positive reviews, with critics acknowledging the game's ambitious scope while noting persistent technical issues. Most surviving contemporary reviews cover the original 1997 release rather than the Special Edition. In its March 1998 review of the original, PC Gamer awarded 82%, with reviewer Keith Sullivan listing as highs "Great strategic depth; well-designed units; strong AI; epic scope" and as lows "Combat system is awkward; the interface has a few problems; initial release buggy."[^ref-18] Sullivan concluded that "Lords of Magic is a deep, fun game; it just needs some tweaking to be really great."[^ref-18]

GameSpot gave the Special Edition 7/10, with Michael E. Ryan noting that "The Special Edition corrects a number of flaws and adds several new features but somehow ends up being only a tiny bit more appealing than its predecessor."[^ref-13] The original release had received a lower 6.3/10 from GameSpot, with the reviewer lamenting that "Lords of Magic seems like the perfect strategy game... Unfortunately, LOM falls far short of its potential."[^ref-19]

Reviewing the original, PC Gamer UK was notably less impressed, awarding only 56% and declaring it "the most clumsy and out-dated game I've seen for years."[^ref-20] The reviewer criticized the design philosophy: "you do not make a good game by pulling a hundred half-baked ideas out of the unoriginality hat and then bouncing them all off some mad kind of wall."[^ref-20]

GameCenter's February 1998 review of the original delivered a harsh 4/10, with Hugh Falk noting that "Lords of Magic is riddled with bugs, and the real tragedy is that my hypothetical box cover would have suited its contents more accurately."[^ref-21] Computer Games Strategy Plus's Tim Royal offered a more nuanced take on the original, describing the experience poetically: "Lords of Magic smells, acts, and cries like a muconium drenched infant, but its baby blue eyes put a tractor beam lock onto your soul, screaming 'Keep me!'"[^ref-22]

For the Special Edition, GameRankings lists Computer Games Magazine at 4/5, Electric Playground at 6.5/10, and Games First! at 4/5.[^ref-23] Game Revolution's January 1998 review of the original assigned a B+ grade under the headline "It's like a Warcraft and Ultima fruit-roll-up!"[^ref-11]

### Modern Assessment

The Home of the Underdogs write-up reproduced on My Abandonware calls it "one of the best strategy games ever made" and argues that it, "much more so than G.O.D.'s overhyped Age of Wonder, deserves to be considered the true spiritual sequel to SimTex's acclaimed Master of Magic."[^ref-24] The same write-up quotes Games Domain's review of the Special Edition, which praised its atmosphere: "LOM:SE is lush with atmosphere. The strategic component, where you will maneuver all your armies and characters in a turn-based environment, is beautiful." That review concluded the game was "the best computer implementation I've yet seen of pencil-and-paper fantasy campaigning for one, with all the trimmings."[^ref-24]

Steam user reviews are "Very Positive" at 92% approval.[^ref-9] GOG.com users rate it 4.2/5 based on 118 reviews.[^ref-3] However, some modern players find it dated—one Steam reviewer noted: "It might be a great game in 1997 but it is at best mediocre in 2025."[^ref-25] Another defended it: "OG Sierra development at it's finest. Just remember the game is almost 30 yrs old, so it's not a modern interface, and there is no hand-holding."[^ref-25]

**Aggregate Scores:**

## Development

### Origins

The design of Lords of Magic drew inspiration from classic fantasy literature and successful strategy games. According to the Designer's Notes in the game manual, "When we first started planning a fantasy strategy game, our initial investigations led back to Tolkien's Lord Of The Rings trilogy."[^ref-20] According to Wikipedia, the game was intended to combine elements of Heroes of Might and Magic II and Lords of the Realm II,[^ref-5] the latter one of Impressions' own strategy titles.[^ref-18]

Impressions Games, described as "a small part of the Sierra family," developed the game under Chris Beatrice, who is listed as director on Wikipedia and as lead designer in MobyGames' Special Edition credits.[^ref-18][^ref-5][^ref-27] The team aimed to create an experience that blended classic fantasy role-playing with real-time strategy combat, offering players the ability to develop characters as warriors, mages, or thieves while managing an empire.[^ref-8]

### Production

The original Lords of Magic shipped in December 1997; the Home of the Underdogs write-up describes "a wonderful concept bogged down by a frustrating amount of bugs and unfulfilled promises."[^ref-24] Computer Games Strategy Plus's review noted the game "shows signs of being delivered into the cuddling arms of gamers well short of its due date," describing numerous technical issues including disappearing hired champions, crashes from the high-level zoom feature, and units getting caught in "endless decision loops."[^ref-22]

Sierra responded with multiple patches, culminating in the Special Edition release in September 1998 which incorporated all updates and patches along with substantial new content.[^ref-6] The Special Edition was announced with an estimated retail price of $39.95, with existing Lords of Magic owners eligible for a $20 rebate.[^ref-6] New features included the Legends of Urak Quest Pack, improved AI, a map editor, custom maps, eight new magical creatures, 17 new wandering monsters, and new buildings, artifacts, and spells.[^ref-6]

### Technical Achievements

The game engine, called GameScript, is based upon PostScript language level 3—"the language used by Adobe Inc. for their .ps files. It is a stack-oriented language."[^ref-7] Community investigation revealed the engine contains 1802 hard-coded procedures, and patch author ascension64 noted that it uses Blizzard's dynamic link libraries (DLLs), with references to battle.net.[^ref-7] Computer Games Strategy Plus's review of the original lists multiplayer for "2-8 players, Internet, LAN, modem, serial",[^ref-22] and PCGamingWiki notes that "The official WON multiplayer service has been shut down."[^ref-12]

The modding community discovered "there is enough customization out there to make total conversions out of Lords of Magic," though special building effects remain hard-coded in the executable and are not modifiable through scripts.[^ref-7] ascension64's unofficial 3.02 patch adds a script console, opened with the tilde (~) key, that exposes the game's underlying script commands.[^ref-7]

### Technical Issues

The original release was notoriously buggy. Documented issues included:[^ref-22]
- Hired champions disappearing on occasion
- High-level zoom feature crashing consistently
- Lengthy loading times between turns (several seconds to several minutes)
- Highlighted creatures failing to un-highlight
- Units getting caught in endless decision loops

The Special Edition addressed many issues but introduced new problems:[^ref-13]
- Marauding parties appearing at alarming rates
- Units standing idle after killing initial targets in real-time combat
- Troops getting hung up on scenery
- Difficulty targeting enemy units in close quarters
- Long recovery times for wounded troops
- AI opponents beginning with superior armies, gold, crystals, and ale
- When defeating an enemy lord, vast armies pouring into player's lands

The official 3.01 patch for the Special Edition has a bug that causes some low-level dungeons and marauder parties to spawn with Pegasus Riders, a high-level unit; the unofficial 3.02 patch fixes it.[^ref-12]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Specific sales figures are not available in the research data. The game was released at an estimated retail price of $39.95, with existing Lords of Magic owners eligible for a $20 rebate when purchasing the Special Edition.[^ref-6] The game has remained commercially available through digital distribution, first on GOG.com (July 23, 2009) and later on Steam (December 3, 2015), currently priced at €4.99.[^ref-34]

### Collections

The Special Edition itself functions as a compilation, bundling:[^ref-2][^ref-6]
- The original Lords of Magic game
- The Legends of Urak Quest Pack expansion
- Lord Editor
- Map Editor
- Additional content (8 new buildings, 17 new monsters, 5 new storylines)

The US retail release included a Caesar III Demo disc.[^ref-35]

### Fan Projects

The modding community has remained active despite the game's age. Notable fan projects include:[^ref-7]
- **Unofficial 3.02 Patch** (Build 0014.3) by ascension64 – Comprehensive community patch fixing numerous bugs and adding features
- **LoMSE Update Mod** by Meogron (2024) – "Fixes lot of errors with artifacts, spell sounds and makes some unit, artifact and minor spell balances"[^ref-36]

A dedicated subreddit (r/LoMSE) maintains an active community with discussions of game mechanics, modding, and ongoing player engagement.[^ref-37]

### Related Publications

- **Game Manual:** 100+ pages of instructions, essential for learning the game's complex mechanics[^ref-11]
- **Quick Reference Card:** Included with retail copies[^ref-35]

### Critical Perspective

Lords of Magic: Special Edition occupies a curious position in strategy gaming history. Despite its troubled launch and mixed critical reception, it has admirers: the Home of the Underdogs write-up calls it "a true underdog worthy of every strategy gamer's library."[^ref-24] Not everyone agrees; a 2004 MobyGames user review noted that, "As with LOTR II, it's a hybrid with everything turn-based except the battles which are real-time," but found that "the different aspects of the game seem disjointed."[^ref-27]

The Home of the Underdogs write-up compares it to SimTex's Master of Magic, calling it that game's "true spiritual sequel."[^ref-24] PC Gamer's 1998 review of the original concluded that "Lords of Magic has exceptional play value for gamers willing to dig into it and work past some the game's quirks."[^ref-18]

The game remains on sale through modern digital platforms, and its modding community is still active. In a Steam discussion thread about a possible sequel, one player wrote, "The world is too rich not to revisit," and another said a worthy successor would be welcome, "But it would be very important to me that a sequal would not cut out complexity nor style."[^ref-38]
