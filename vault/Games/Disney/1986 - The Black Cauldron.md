# The Black Cauldron

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Black Cauldron is a 1986 adventure game developed by Sierra On-Line in collaboration with Disney[^ref-1]. It was created by Al Lowe, who later became known for the Leisure Suit Larry series[^ref-2]. According to Adventure Classic Gaming, it was only the second adventure game Sierra developed from a movie license, after The Dark Crystal[^ref-18]. It is based on Disney's 1985 animated film of the same name, itself adapted from Lloyd Alexander's Chronicles of Prydain novels, and it replaced the text parser with a simplified function-key interface intended to make the game more accessible to children[^ref-3].

Al Lowe describes the film as "the last of the pre-Michael Eisner Disney animated feature-length movies," and recalls that "Disney had no software developers back then and had seen my very early game, 'Troll's Tale.' They asked me to do a movie spinoff using its simplified Spacebar & Enter key-only interface"[^ref-4].

## Story Summary

Players take control of Taran, the assistant pig-keeper at Caer Dallben, who must prevent the evil Horned King from obtaining the magical Black Cauldron[^ref-6]. The Black Cauldron has the power to "change skeletons into deathless, man-like, emotionless warriors" which would give the Horned King "a huge army to take over Prydain"[^ref-6]. When Taran's oracular pig Hen Wen is captured by the Horned King's forces, "Taran must now embark on a mission to rescue Hen Wen and defeat the Evil Horned King before he can discover the Cauldron's location"[^ref-5].

The game follows the basic plot structure of the Disney film but allows for significant player choice in how events unfold. As Al Lowe explained, "The story echoes the movie, but anywhere you could do something different from the action in the movie, you received more points for doing so. There are at least six different variations of the ending, based on decisions made throughout game play"[^ref-7].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The Black Cauldron used an innovative interface that, according to Wikipedia, would not reappear in the genre for about ten years[^ref-3]. To make the game more accessible to children, Sierra removed the text parser and used function keys instead[^ref-3]. Players moved Taran with the arrow keys and interacted with the world through specific function keys: F3 to choose inventory items, F4 to use inventory items, F6 to use items near the character's location, and F8 to look at objects[^ref-8].

Not every player was won over by the simplified controls. A 2017 HonestGamers community review, which scored the game 2/10, joked that "Disney felt that the best way to recoup the losses of their crappy movie was to produce an even crappier video game"[^ref-9]. A joystick was optional on the PC[^ref-18], and the Atari ST version could also be controlled with the mouse[^ref-11].

### Structure and Progression

The game featured "seventy three-dimensional screens more detailed than any seen before, in up to 16 colors" with characters that could "pass in front of trees, behind rocks, and around other characters"[^ref-10]. It was built on Sierra's AGI (Adventure Game Interpreter) engine, which, like Sierra's earlier Hi-Res Adventures, drew its scenes with vector graphics: polygons drawn on screen and then colored[^ref-28].

Players could explore the world of Prydain, collecting items, solving puzzles, and interacting with characters from the Disney film. The game included survival elements, with messages like "Your throat is dry" appearing to remind players to find food and water[^ref-9]. The scoring system encouraged creative problem-solving and deviation from the film's plot, with "multiple solutions and variable scoring" allowing players to "follow the movie's storyline, or use your imagination to maximize your score"[^ref-10].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzles were designed to be more straightforward than typical Sierra adventures of the era, reflecting the game's target audience of children[^ref-12]. The game included both inventory-based puzzles and simple arcade sequences, though "even the arcade segments are pretty simple"[^ref-13]. Players could save their progress using 12 save slots[^ref-8]. Antic's reviewer noted that on the Atari ST, "although not mentioned in the documentation, the game speed may be adjusted by typing 'slow,' 'fast' or 'normal'"[^ref-11].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been mixed. Adventure Classic Gaming's Davide Tomei gave the original game 3/5 (Good) in 2008, concluding that "even for today, The Black Cauldron is a decent adventure game. The dated graphics and the clumsy interface can make for some frustrating gameplay, but they are endurable flaws"[^ref-18]. Alex Bevilacqua was less kind, giving it just 23% in 2023[^ref-19].

User reviews on aggregate sites show more positive reception, with MyAbandonware users rating it 4.27/5[^ref-20] and DOSGames.com giving it 4/5[^ref-2]. MobyGames shows a critic aggregate score of 67% with players rating it 3.5/5[^ref-21]. Hardcore Gaming 101 argues that "the simpler interface, slightly more structured approach, and generally lowered difficulty actually make it more approachable for the modern gamer than most other games of the time"[^ref-13].

## Development

### Origins

Disney had no in-house software developers at the time[^ref-4]. Al Lowe has given two accounts of why Disney came to him. On his own website he says Disney "had seen my very early game, 'Troll's Tale'"[^ref-4]. In a 2010 interview excerpted by Game Developer, he said, "They liked what I did on Winnie the Pooh, and they wanted someone to make a game from their new film"[^ref-22]. He also recalled that Disney's side "didn't really understand video games at that time; the job was assigned to the same women who were in charge of educational film strips for the schools"[^ref-22].

### Production

Disney provided Sierra with unusual access to the film's original materials. Al Lowe described how "They gave me complete access to the original hand-painted backgrounds, the original Elmer Bernstein score, even the original animation cells, which were still literally lying in heaps, before being sent off to the dump!"[^ref-4] This collaboration allowed the game to maintain visual and audio consistency with the Disney film.

MobyGames credits the game design to Al Lowe and Roberta Williams. The programming credits go to Sol Ackerman, Chris Iden, Al Lowe, Scott Murphy, Jeff Stephenson and Ken Williams, and the graphics credits to Mark Crowe, Gerald Moore, Karen Nugent and Terry Pierce[^ref-31]. In a Spring 1989 Sierra Newsletter interview, Crowe said he "did some animation for Black Cauldron which was the first project Scott and I worked on together"[^ref-30]. Murphy described spending "nights out at his house debugging Black Cauldron. And then I ended up being the only one working on it. After a while Ken and Al Lowe bailed out on me. So I got Black Cauldron shipping"[^ref-30]. While finishing The Black Cauldron, Crowe and Murphy began planning the game that became Space Quest[^ref-23]. The game was built using Sierra's AGI engine[^ref-24].

### Technical Achievements

Reviewing the Atari ST version, Antic's Matthew Loveless found that, although the pictures were "in a chunky low-resolution mode", "the images are slickly drawn with accurate perspective and subtle shading", with Taran "a lifelike animated character"[^ref-11]. The DOS version supported CGA, MCGA, EGA, Hercules, Tandy/PCjr and VGA video modes[^ref-8]. The Apple IIgs version featured enhanced audio capabilities using the ES5503 sound chip[^ref-25].

The game required only 256K of memory and ran on systems as basic as the Intel 8088[^ref-8]. There were performance issues, though. On the Atari ST, Antic noted that "the adjacent screen is loaded in slowly" whenever Taran walked off the edge of a screen[^ref-11].

## Legacy

The Black Cauldron's function-key interface, which replaced typed commands, was an approach Wikipedia says did not reappear in the genre for about ten years[^ref-3].

### Preservation and Fan Community

The original game is no longer sold, and Al Lowe "generously released this game as freeware"[^ref-7]. On his website he writes that "these games are no longer of any interest to my friends at Disney or Sierra, so feel free to copy them and share them with your friends"[^ref-4].

In 2004 the fan developer DreamMaster released Black Cauldron Remake, a freeware remake made with Adventure Game Studio. The AGS Panel rated it 3 cups in 2015: "Some annoying bugs aside, this is a solid translation of the original Black Cauldron pc game to AGS"[^ref-29]. Hardcore Gaming 101 suggests the original may "actually best the King's Quest games" in approachability, "even though it's not quite as groundbreaking"[^ref-13].

### Animated Adventure Aesthetics

Disney's original backgrounds and animation cells were used to model the game's "over 1,020 cells of animations that made up the 30 animated characters", and its 8 musical tunes were based on the film's score[^ref-18]. Adventure Classic Gaming found that "despite the limited graphics, the game is able to capture the spooky ambience of the movie on which it is based"[^ref-18].
