# J.R.R. Tolkien's War in Middle Earth

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

*J.R.R. Tolkien's War in Middle Earth* is a real-time strategy and adventure adaptation of *The Lord of the Rings*. Its 16-bit versions for MS-DOS, Amiga, Atari ST and Apple IIGS were developed by [[Synergistic Software]] and published by Melbourne House.[^ref-1][^ref-2] Computer Gaming World's May 1989 review credited the design to Alan Clark and [[Robert Clardy]] and gave Melbourne House of Costa Mesa, California as the publisher.[^ref-3] A separate, simpler 8-bit game with the same title was made for the Commodore 64, ZX Spectrum, Amstrad CPC and MSX by Mike Singleton's Maelstrom Games. Melbourne House published that one too.[^ref-4][^ref-5] This page covers the Synergistic version.

War in Middle Earth was the first game built on Synergistic's "World Builder" engine. The studio went on to use that engine for *Spirit of Excalibur*, *Vengeance of Excalibur* and *Conan: The Cimmerian*.[^ref-5][^ref-6][^ref-7] Sierra On-Line did not publish War in Middle Earth. The connection to SierraVault is that Sierra acquired Synergistic in 1996, and the studio stayed a development group within Sierra until it closed on February 22, 1999.[^ref-8][^ref-9]

## Story Summary

The game follows the War of the Ring. It opens with Frodo Baggins leaving Hobbiton with Sam and Pippin after a letter from Gandalf warns him to make for Rivendell, while the Nine Riders search the Shire.[^ref-3][^ref-2] Once the hobbits reach Rivendell, the Fellowship of the Ring forms and the main quest begins: the Ring has to be unmade in the Cracks of Mount Doom, and the armies of elves, men and dwarves have to be mustered against Saruman and Sauron.[^ref-3]

The leaders of the Free Peoples will not commit their forces until the player presents each of them with a token proving the "truth of your embassy." These artifacts are scattered across the map, and characters met along the way give clues to where they are.[^ref-3] Dragon's reviewers gave two examples: King Théoden of Rohan has to be shown the Red Arrow, and the Gondorians mobilise when presented with the Sceptre of Annúminas.[^ref-11] The player loses if the Ring is captured and carried to Saruman or Sauron, or if the forces of evil overrun enough of the strongholds of the West.[^ref-3][^ref-2]

The story does not have to follow the books. MobyGames notes that the player is "free to decide how heroes and armies progress," and that if the Ring-bearer dies, a surviving companion can take up the Ring.[^ref-2]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The game runs in real time on three levels of detail:[^ref-3][^ref-11]

- **Full map level.** All of Middle-earth on one screen, with forces shown as coloured dots: blue for allies, red for enemies and green for neutrals.[^ref-2]
- **Campaign level.** A 36-screen scrolling map where orders are given and most of the game is played. Characters appear as figures and armies as heraldic badges.[^ref-3][^ref-12]
- **Animation level.** A close-up of one location with fully animated characters set against painted backgrounds. Conversations, item handling and small fights happen here.[^ref-3][^ref-13]

Commands are icons with keyboard equivalents. The magnifying glass zooms in, the hourglass changes game speed, the scroll saves and loads, and the eye shows status by gazing into the Mirror of Galadriel.[^ref-11][^ref-13] The game can be controlled by mouse, joystick or keyboard.[^ref-13] An anonymous text-file review of the IBM version, archived on textfiles.com, called the interface "easy and intuitive." It also said the reviewer had to slow a fast PC down to 6 MHz because the game ran too fast even at its slowest speed.[^ref-13]

### Structure and Progression

Play is open-ended and simultaneous. Army battles are resolved at the campaign level with four orders (charge, engage, withdraw and retreat), and battles can be watched or skipped.[^ref-2][^ref-12] Wikipedia notes that individual characters can be seen inside the larger battles, and that a battle of fewer than about 100 units can be watched at ground level while larger ones are only reported as numbers.[^ref-1] Computer Gaming World's reviewer found that the tokens were not strictly necessary. Once Sauron's forces were on the move, he could command every allied army anyway.[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The adventure layer centres on finding objects, such as the tokens and equipment, and on talking to non-player characters such as Tom Bombadil and Radagast.[^ref-1][^ref-3] The package included a large parchment map of Middle-earth. Partway into the game, the player is asked to enter the map coordinates of a location from it. The text-file review describes this as the game's only copy check.[^ref-13]

| Dragon #147 (Jul 1989) | Amiga, MS-DOS | 3 of 5 stars[^ref-1][^ref-11] |
| ST-Log #36 (Oct 1989) | Atari ST | Positive review, no score[^ref-12] |
| Info #29 (Nov 1989) | Amiga | 4.5/5[^ref-10] |

Chris Lombardi's Computer Gaming World review praised how faithful the game was to Tolkien: "The exquisite detail and accuracy of the game make this the definitive Tolkien computer game." He also warned that "the strategy element is, at best, beginner level and replayability is questionable."[^ref-3] Hartley, Patricia and Kirk Lesser wrote in Dragon that the game was "a lion-hearted attempt to bring one of the finest fantasy adventures to microcomputers." They complained that the interface made them lose encounters and that it was hard to react in time during PC combat. They added that "Of the versions we have played, we preferred the Commodore Amiga format."[^ref-11]

Frank Eva of ST-Log disliked the Atari ST animation and backgrounds and found that "just about every move requires a disk swap" on a single-drive machine. Even so, he concluded that "If the potential buyer can look beyond the somewhat chintzy graphics, War in Middle Earth is a fantasy-lover's ultimate contest—one that he will come back to again and again."[^ref-12] The textfiles.com reviewer opened with "This is the first Tolkien-based game I've ever liked; the first one to really capture the feel of the LORD OF THE RINGS books themselves."[^ref-13]

### Modern Assessment

Modern coverage is thin. On MobyGames the 16-bit entry has a Moby Score of 7.2.[^ref-2] The CRPG Addict blog has not given it a full review, but it treats the game as the origin of the engine and interface used in Synergistic's later Excalibur and Conan games.[^ref-6][^ref-7]

## Development

### Origins

Two different games were sold under the War in Middle Earth name in 1989:

- **16-bit (Synergistic).** Lemon Amiga credits the Amiga version's concept to Mike Singleton, design to Alan B. Clark and Robert Clardy, production to Ron Harris and direction to Clardy. It also lists "production assistance" by Graeme Devine and documentation by Marcus Streets.[^ref-10] The Internet Archive's Apple IIGS release gives a similar credit list, including Ann Dickens Clardy.[^ref-14]
- **8-bit (Maelstrom).** Spectrum Computing credits the Spectrum version's design to Mike Singleton of Maelstrom Games, together with Alan B. Clark and Robert Clardy of Synergistic. It gives Chris Pink for code and Paul Robotham for graphics.[^ref-5] Wikipedia's article on Singleton says the game was developed by Maelstrom Games, the company Singleton had founded to run a play-by-mail version of *Dark Sceptre*.[^ref-15]

The textfiles.com review describes the 8-bit Commodore version as "substantially different" from the Amiga game. It was "redesigned to focus almost exclusively on its wargame aspects": it starts in Rivendell, drops the object-hunting, and adds unit statistics and 15 difficulty levels.[^ref-13] MobyGames likewise calls the 8-bit game "a simpler version of the 16-bit game" and "a pure strategy game."[^ref-4]

The Dragon reviewers reported that the game took "five years to create."[^ref-11] That claim does not appear in any other source consulted. ST-Log said the game was "approved by the Tolkien estate itself."[^ref-12]

### Publishing

Each version should be credited separately:

- MobyGames lists Melbourne House as publisher of every 16-bit release: the U.S. MS-DOS and Apple IIGS editions and the UK Amiga and Atari ST editions.[^ref-2]
- Wikipedia says the game came out from Virgin Mastertronic "on the Melbourne House label".[^ref-1] The textfiles.com review describes it as "published by the Virgin Mastertronic Group and distributed by Melbourne House."[^ref-13]
- The 8-bit ZX Spectrum game was published by Melbourne House, and Dro Soft reissued it in Spain.[^ref-5]

### Technical Achievements

War in Middle Earth was the first game on Synergistic's World Builder engine.[^ref-5] The CRPG Addict wrote that *Spirit of Excalibur*'s interface "evolved" from War in Middle Earth.[^ref-6] Later he noted that Synergistic reused the engine for multiple characters in *Warriors of Legend* (1993) and for *The Beverly Hillbillies* (1993).[^ref-7] MobyGames lists the MS-DOS version's minimum requirements as an 8088 CPU and 256 KB of RAM, with CGA, EGA, MCGA and Tandy graphics and PC speaker or Tandy sound.[^ref-2] The Apple IIGS version needs 768 KB.[^ref-2][^ref-14]

## Legacy

War in Middle Earth established the template that Synergistic reused for the rest of its World Builder games. Gameplay moves between a real-time strategic map and side-on scenes of individual characters.[^ref-6][^ref-7] Virgin's *Spirit of Excalibur* (1990) was described by MobyGames as "a spiritual descendant of War in Middle Earth" that "uses the same engine."[^ref-18] It was followed by *Vengeance of Excalibur* (1991) and *Conan: The Cimmerian* (1991).[^ref-9]

Synergistic later worked with Sierra as a subcontractor. In 1996 Ken Williams proposed "taking us over entirely," as Bob Clardy recalled to Polygon.[^ref-8] Under Sierra, the studio made *Birthright: The Gorgon's Alliance* and *Diablo: Hellfire*.[^ref-8][^ref-9]
