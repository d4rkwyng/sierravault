# B.C. II: Grog's Revenge

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

B.C. II: Grog's Revenge is a 1984 action game developed by Sydney Development Corp. and published by Sierra On-Line, released for the Commodore 64, ColecoVision, Coleco Adam and MSX[^ref-1][^ref-6]. The game serves as the sequel to B.C.'s Quest for Tires (1983), which had won several awards, including *Electronic Games*' Arkie for Most Humorous Video/Computer Game and *Billboard*'s award for best use of graphics and sound[^ref-26]. Both games are based on Johnny Hart's popular newspaper comic strip *B.C.*, which first appeared in the 1950s and features prehistoric characters in humorous situations[^ref-3].

The game features the character Thor, who rides a stone unicycle through a maze of mountains in search of the "Meaning of Life"[^ref-4]. Unlike its predecessor, which featured a more linear, Moon Patrol-style gameplay[^ref-10], Grog's Revenge offers players greater freedom of movement across interconnected mountain paths[^ref-5]. *Zzap!64* rated it 75% in June 1985, calling it an "Amusing new scenario on the collect and dodge theme"[^ref-24], and *Compute!'s Gazette* called it "an equally good sequel"[^ref-16]. Some players have found it repetitive: one C64-Wiki user review faults "its monotony", saying "You do not have to do more than collecting clams and avoiding obstacles"[^ref-3].

The game was released on multiple platforms through various international publishers, including U.S. Gold in Europe, Comptiq in Japan, and Erbe Software in Spain[^ref-1]. Ports advertised for the Atari 8-bit computers, ZX Spectrum, BBC Micro, and Amstrad CPC were never released[^ref-6].

## Story Summary

Thor has completed his Quest for Tires in the previous adventure. Now he must discover the Meaning of Life[^ref-4]. But this philosophical journey won't be easy—the Meaning of Life is hidden away somewhere in a long maze of mountains[^ref-7]. Thor must navigate treacherous paths on his trusty stone unicycle while avoiding the menacing Grog, who stalks him throughout his journey[^ref-5].

The only way to reach Peter and the toll bridge that leads to enlightenment is through the "real scary caves"[^ref-7]. Each mountain is covered with clams, the currency of the prehistoric world as established in Hart's comic strip[^ref-6]. Thor needs to gather enough clams to pay the toll and advance to the next mountain[^ref-6]. Peter, a character from the original comics[^ref-10], serves as the toll collector who guards passage between mountain regions[^ref-9].

The game culminates in a quest across multiple mountain sets, with Thor dodging obstacles and outwitting Grog to reach the ultimate prize. However, the meaning of life itself proves to be "unpleasant" according to player accounts[^ref-2]. When players finally reach the last mountain, a huge sign tells Thor that the Meaning of Life is to the right of him, but Grog is already waiting—forcing players into a cave to the left in search of an alternate route[^ref-10].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

B.C. II: Grog's Revenge is controlled via joystick (Port 2 on the Commodore 64)[^ref-11]. The game features tight, responsive controls that give players full responsibility for their actions—"you only have yourself to thank in the event of another lost life"[^ref-12]. Unlike the predecessor which was essentially "on rails," this sequel features 4-directional movement allowing Thor to explore the mountain paths more freely[^ref-5].

The stone unicycle's physics create a distinctive feel, with "the toddling unicycle, the waving head of hair, the charming grin and the funny crashes" making Thor "one of the most likeable characters of all times"[^ref-3]. The game supports both single-player and two-player modes, with players taking turns[^ref-11].

### Structure and Progression

The game structure varies significantly between platform versions. The Commodore 64 and Coleco ADAM versions feature the most content with 15 stages organized into three sets of 5 mountains each[^ref-6]. The MSX and ColecoVision versions contain only 5 stages total[^ref-6].

Players can choose from three mountains of increasing difficulty at the start[^ref-10]. Each mountain features:

- **Outdoor paths:** Winding roads around mountainsides where Thor collects clams
- **Cave sections:** Dark interiors where Thor's unicycle inexplicably features headlights[^ref-5]
- **Toll bridges:** Checkpoints guarded by Peter, who must be paid in clams for passage[^ref-6]
- **Warp tunnels:** Hidden shortcuts with number codes in each mountain level[^ref-3]

Levels are lettered. On the Commodore 64 the mountains of each chain are marked A to E, with the Meaning of Life on the peak of mountain E; on the easy chain the warp codes are 2 pressed twice (A to B), 3 twice (B to C) and 4 twice (C to D)[^ref-3].

The MSX version runs to a Level F, with these warp tunnel codes documented by players:
- Level A: Code 22 (to Level B)
- Level B: Code 23 (to Level C)
- Level C: Code 44 (to Level D)
- Level D: Code 45 (to Level E)
- Level E: Code 62 (to Level F)[^ref-13]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay loop involves collecting clams while avoiding hazards and the pursuing Grog[^ref-3]. As one Lemon64 commenter put it: "Collecting clams is okay. But what raises the game a couple of notches is how you have to develop a strategy to avoid Grog!"[^ref-11]. Players must balance efficient clam collection with safe navigation around obstacles.

The game lacks continues and has no pause feature, creating significant challenge[^ref-10]. Death comes through various means including hitting walls, falling off edges, and encounters with Grog. Notably, "the strength and appeal of Grog's Revenge is also its weakness, because it's much more fun getting killed off than collecting clams and freewheeling round the mountain"[^ref-14]—the cartoon death animations proving particularly entertaining to players[^ref-15].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The game received generally positive reviews from the gaming press of the mid-1980s, with particular praise for its graphics and animation.

*Zzap!64* reviewed the game in its June 1985 issue (#2), with Gary Penn, Julian Rignall and Bob Wade on the panel[^ref-11]. One reviewer's comment read: "This is the first game where I have laughed every time I died," calling it "an addictive and amusing game"[^ref-24]. The issue mistakenly printed *Shadowfire*'s ratings box (91%, "Stunningly impressive program") beside the review; the same issue's "Apology Corner" admitted the swap and reprinted Grog's Revenge's real box, which gave 75% for value for money, with the verdicts "Amusing new scenario on the collect and dodge theme" and "Wonderful sense of humour keeps you laughing and playing"[^ref-24][^ref-3]. Some secondary sources, including Wikipedia, still repeat the 91% figure[^ref-6].

*Computer and Video Games*' 1989 *Complete Guide to Consoles* rated the ColecoVision version 87%: "Hilarious graphical touches and jolly gameplay makes this a game that's both addictive and highly amusing"[^ref-25].

*Commodore User* provided multiple scores: 4/5 in June 1985 from reviewer Mike Pattenden, and a later 8/10 in June 1988[^ref-11]. The original review stated: "The joy about this game is the graphics which are marvellous. It's like being in a cartoon, because the whole thing is superbly animated"[^ref-14]. The review concluded that "that would have made it a classic instead of just plain great"[^ref-14].

*Commodore Format* gave the game 70% in March 1994, calling it "definitely a game of the old school" and "a fun little diversion"[^ref-6].

Additional reviews included:
- *Commodore Horizons*: 6/10 (June 1985)[^ref-11]
- *Home Computing Weekly*: 3/5 (May 1985)[^ref-11]
- *Your 64*: 2/5 (July 1985)[^ref-11]
- *C&VG*: 6/10 (July 1985)[^ref-11]

*Compute!'s Gazette* reviewed the disk version (US$34.95) in its September 1985 issue (#27). James Trunzo wrote that "on rare occasions, a truly good arcade game is succeeded by an equally good sequel," concluding that the game "leaves the gamer hoping for a sequel to the sequel"[^ref-16].

Modern retrospectives have maintained generally favorable opinions of the game, with community sites celebrating its charming presentation while acknowledging gameplay limitations.

Another player noted: "BC II - Grog's Revenge is an easy game to pick up and play"[^ref-12], while acknowledging that modern standards may find it lacking compared to contemporary expectations[^ref-10].

## Development

### Origins

B.C. II: Grog's Revenge emerged as a direct sequel to the successful B.C.'s Quest for Tires, released in 1983[^ref-2]. The original game had won several awards[^ref-26], and the sequel kept its connection to Johnny Hart's comic strip[^ref-3].

The development was handled by the Canadian developer Sydney Development Corp.[^ref-1] The team opted to move away from the Moon Patrol-style side-scrolling of the original, instead creating a more open exploration-based experience that gave players greater agency in how they navigated the prehistoric landscape[^ref-10].

### Production

Development was led by designers Rick Banks and Michael Bates[^ref-6]. The game maintained the comic aesthetic of its predecessor but employed "more restrained colors" in its visual presentation, primarily featuring grey and blue tones rather than the brighter palette of the first game[^ref-3].

The programming team included J. Stuart Easterbrook, P.C. Kelly, and Tris Orendorff as coders, while J. Meinert and Scott Fiander handled the graphics[^ref-11]. The result preserved the cartoon-like quality that made the original notable while adapting it to the new gameplay structure.

**Development Credits:**[^ref-11]
- **Designers:** Rick Banks, Michael Bates[^ref-6]
- **Programmers:** J. Stuart Easterbrook, P.C. Kelly, Tris Orendorff
- **Graphics:** J. Meinert, Scott Fiander
- **Composer:** Paul Butler

### Cancelled ZX Spectrum Version

A ZX Spectrum port was in development at U.S. Gold, being worked on by programmer Dougie Burns[^ref-17]. The version was reportedly progressing well before being cancelled. As Burns later reflected: "I'll always regret not finishing Grog's Revenge for US Gold, because it was looking good"[^ref-2]. The game was advertised in UK magazines during 1985 with a planned price of £7.95[^ref-17], but Burns was ultimately switched onto other projects before completion[^ref-2]. The Spectrum version was never released[^ref-18].

### Technical Achievements

The game's animation system was particularly celebrated, continuing the visual excellence established in the original. The cartoon-style graphics impressed contemporary reviewers—"It's like being in a cartoon, because the whole thing is superbly animated"[^ref-14]. The death animations proved especially memorable, with players recalling decades later how they enjoyed "the cartoony style of death animations (hitting the wall & falling off the edges)"[^ref-15].

The MSX version was notable for being "one of the few games that is not a direct Spectrum conversion and takes good advantage of MSX1 capabilities"[^ref-19].

### Cut Content

The most significant cut content relates to the cancelled platform versions. Despite being advertised in gaming magazines, ports for the following systems were never released[^ref-6]:
- Atari 8-bit computers
- ZX Spectrum (partially developed by Doug Burns)[^ref-17]
- BBC Micro
- Amstrad CPC

Additionally, the US version of the ColecoVision release removed the staff credits screen that appeared in the Canadian version. "The US version replaced the blue title screen with a more eye-pleasing style. Unfortunately, it also removed the screen that listed the game's staff"[^ref-21]. An additional 1K of data was changed between the two regional versions[^ref-21].

### Technical Issues

The game is infamous for containing critical bugs that prevented completion on certain platforms.

**MSX Version Bug:**
All MSX releases contain a programming error that makes the game impossible to complete[^ref-6]. "There's a spot in the final level (Level F) where the road corner doesn't take you to the spot where it should, but instead takes you back to the very beginning of the level, therefore making it impossible to make any more progress/beat the game"[^ref-22]. The bug was documented by Generation MSX: "The MSX version has a bug which makes it impossible to finish level F"[^ref-23].

Technical analysis revealed the bug was caused by incorrect byte values in the phase connection lists. The Phase F connection list contained byte value 00h instead of 04h, causing an inaccessible area needed to progress. Additionally, Phase B contained a bug (04h instead of 14h) causing delayed cave exits and VRAM corruption[^ref-19].

**ColecoVision Version Bug:**
The ColecoVision version suffered from the same issue, "although it was fixed for the Coleco Canada release"[^ref-6]. The Canadian version remains the only bug-free ColecoVision release.

**Community Fix:**
After 34 years, Spanish programmer Félix Espina finally fixed the MSX version in 2019[^ref-6]. As celebrated by The MSX Channel: "34 years later, the MSX version of the game is *finally* fixed"[^ref-13]. The patch corrected the connection list errors, finally allowing MSX players to complete the game[^ref-19].

**Other Issues:**
- Controls can be "finicky with wall/fall detection" according to some players[^ref-12]
- "There are not enough lives for the length of the game. Its easy to die, there are no continues, and there is no pause feature"[^ref-10]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Warp Tunnel Codes:** Each mountain level contains hidden warp tunnels with specific number codes that allow players to skip ahead[^ref-3]
- **Comptiq Easter Egg:** The MSX version contains a hidden easter egg message in the "Comptiq presents" screen when pressing F and H keys simultaneously[^ref-13]
- **Headlight Anachronism:** "It always bugged me how the caveman had headlights on his unicycle, I mean really?"[^ref-5]—players have long questioned the technological inconsistency of a prehistoric character having electric lighting
- **Comic Strip Currency:** The use of clams as currency is directly lifted from the B.C. comic strip, where clams served as the prehistoric economy's standard monetary unit[^ref-6]
- **Regional Title Variations:** The game is also known as "BC2" and "Grog's Revenge" in various markets, and as "グロッグス・リベンジ" in Japan[^ref-15]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures have been found. The game was released on four platforms through several international publishers[^ref-1], and The Power House re-released it on a budget label in 1988[^ref-11].

The predecessor B.C.'s Quest for Tires had won multiple awards[^ref-26]. Reviews of the sequel were mixed but mostly positive: *Compute!'s Gazette* judged it "an equally good sequel"[^ref-16], *Zzap!64* gave 75%[^ref-24], and MobyGames' critic average is 70%[^ref-2]. One MobyGames user review found the gameplay thin next to its graphics, noting that "great graphics does not always equal a great game"[^ref-10].

### Collections

The game was distributed through various publishers and budget lines:
- Original Sierra On-Line release (1984)[^ref-1]
- U.S. Gold European distribution (1984-1985)[^ref-11]
- The Power House budget re-release (1988)[^ref-11]
- Comptiq Japanese release (1985)[^ref-2]
- Erbe Software Spanish release[^ref-2]

### Fan Projects

The most significant fan contribution came from Félix Espina, who in 2019 created a patch fixing the long-standing bug in the MSX version[^ref-6][^ref-19]. This community effort finally allowed MSX players to complete the game as originally intended[^ref-19].

The game remains available through various abandonware sites and emulation platforms, with ClassicReload.com offering browser-based Commodore 64 emulation[^ref-4].

### Critical Perspective

B.C. II: Grog's Revenge represents an interesting case study in licensed game development during the early home computer era. The game translated the visual charm of Johnny Hart's comic strip into interactive form, and reviewers singled out its animation: "It's like being in a cartoon, because the whole thing is superbly animated"[^ref-14]. One player review cautions, however, that "great graphics does not always equal a great game"[^ref-10].

The game's legacy also includes its bugs. The US ColecoVision release and every MSX release cannot be completed, though Coleco Canada's ColecoVision release fixed the problem[^ref-6]. The MSX bug stayed unpatched for 34 years until Félix Espina's 2019 fix[^ref-13][^ref-19].

C64-Wiki user Robotron2084 was blunter: "In comparison to its forerunner Grog's Revenge distinguishs itself in my opinion by its monotony of the course of the game. You do not have to do more than collecting clams and avoiding obstacles"[^ref-3]. This assessment reflects a common trajectory for sequels attempting to expand upon simpler predecessors—sometimes additional complexity doesn't translate to additional engagement.
