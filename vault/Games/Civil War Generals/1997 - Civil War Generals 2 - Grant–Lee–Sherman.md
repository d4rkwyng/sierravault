# Civil War Generals 2: Grant, Lee, Sherman

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

Civil War Generals 2: Grant, Lee, Sherman is a turn-based wargame of the American Civil War, developed by Impressions Games and published by Sierra On-Line for Windows in October 1997[^ref-62]. It is the sequel to Robert E. Lee: Civil War General (1996)[^ref-62], and it was designed by Douglas Gonya and produced by Stephen Grammont, building on Jeffrey Fiske's design for the first game[^ref-63]. Where its predecessor covered only Lee's Confederate war in the East[^ref-40], the sequel lets the player take either side in the Eastern or Western theater[^ref-13].

Battles are fought on a hex map[^ref-62]. The game offers single battles, mini-campaigns and the whole war, plus a terrain editor and Internet, modem and network play[^ref-62]. GameSpot counted 17 campaigns and over 200 predesigned scenarios[^ref-14]. Its producer later said it "wound up selling a lot. A lot more than they thought it would"[^ref-56]. Critics were split: PC Gamer gave it 86%[^ref-66], while Computer Gaming World called its combat system "completely broken"[^ref-67]. One fan's later verdict was that "Civil War Generals 2 has been the best Civil War game I could fine [sic] as most focus on WWII or other wars"[^ref-4].

## Story Summary

There is no story in the adventure-game sense. The game recreates the battles and campaigns of the American Civil War. Sierra's release announcement promised command "as General Grant, Lee, Sherman or a choice of several other great leaders" and said the game covered "more than 40 battles and 160 variations" in the Eastern and Western Theaters[^ref-70]. GameSpot counted 46 historical scenarios, including Fredericksburg, Gettysburg, Vicksburg and Chickamauga, and 17 campaigns, one of which plays out the entire war in both theaters[^ref-14]. Sierra split those 17 into "three monster, eight major and six smaller campaigns"[^ref-70]. Computer Gaming World counted 45 engagements linked across the campaigns[^ref-67].

History can change as the campaign goes on. A "vast throng of alternative scenarios" comes into play whenever the player changes the course of history[^ref-14], and depending on how well a battle went, the next fight may be an alternative to the real one[^ref-67]. Before each battle the game gives a historical overview of it, names the historical winner, and offers advice to each side[^ref-3].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The battlefield is shown from above on a hex grid[^ref-62][^ref-13], played with keyboard and mouse[^ref-62]. PC Gamer described battles fought "in the classic hex-based pattern derived from generations of paper-and-pencil wargames". Each unit has numbers for health, organization, firepower and morale, and the reviewer found these "easily accessible and readable"[^ref-66]. Computer Gaming World gave the scale as thirty minutes per turn at 200 yards per hex[^ref-67]. Generals command units at brigade and regiment level[^ref-68].

Compared with the first game, the interface added an undo feature for movement and formation changes, plus a zoom option on the battle map[^ref-68]. GameSpot found the terrain art decent but the unit art weak. In particular, units cannot change facing, so Union and Confederate forces often meet "with their backs turned"[^ref-13].

### Structure and Progression

Players can fight one battle, a mini-campaign or the entire war[^ref-62]. In a campaign, units carry their losses and experience from battle to battle. After each fight, a casualty screen grades the result from major defeat to major victory. A hospital screen then shows how wounded officers are doing, and a weapons screen spends captured supply on better rifles, sabers and cannon[^ref-3]. GameSpot compared these between-battle upgrades to Panzer General's: "You can't purchase more troops, but you can load up on Napoleons and Parrott guns"[^ref-14]. Leaders who perform badly can be dismissed[^ref-67].

Victory depends on morale, casualties and objectives. Victory hexes now appear on the map[^ref-68]. PC Gamer noted that hotly contested ground gains "an ever-increasing number of victory points"[^ref-66]. Units also carry two morale ratings, their own and the army's collective morale, and the army's morale carries over between campaign battles[^ref-66].

Multiplayer supports two players over modem, serial and IPX links. Sierra's free SIGS service hosted Internet play, and hot-seat and play-by-e-mail were also available[^ref-23]. Computer Games Strategy Plus reported that play-by-e-mail was missing from the initial release, with a patch promised[^ref-68].

### Combat and Mechanics

Sierra's announcement listed 13 unit types, among them headquarters, gunboats, siege mortars and engineers[^ref-70]. Engineers could build bridges or destroy forts, gunboats patrolled the waterways, and horse artillery joined the field armies. Forts, swamps and oceans were added to the terrain[^ref-68]. The sequel also added zones of control, to stop units slipping away from nearby enemies, and indirect fire for certain artillery[^ref-68]. Wikipedia adds that battles can be played at brigade or regimental level[^ref-65].

Computer Gaming World described each attack as a sequence: attacker fire, defender morale check, defender fire, attacker morale check, then close combat. Infantry has no ranged fire, so it must close to an adjacent hex to attack. Stacking is limited, and only field artillery can share a hex with other unit types[^ref-67]. Leaders have ratings for health, initiative, organization and ability to inspire loyalty, and senior commanders can raise or lower their subordinates' ratings[^ref-67].

The AI "ranges from decent to devious and can be quite challenging on the hardest level," according to GameSpot[^ref-13]. PC Gamer disagreed: its reviewer "had no trouble leading the South to victory on the hardest difficulty level"[^ref-66].

A scenario editor lets players change existing battles or build a battlefield from scratch, placing troops, terrain and resupply points[^ref-14]. Sierra said it was the same editor the designers had used to build every battle in the game[^ref-70]. The game also includes a multimedia history of the war[^ref-67].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| PC Gamer US (Feb 1998) | 86% | Keith Sullivan: "a good, competent offering" for casual war and strategy gamers[^ref-66] |
| GameSpot | 6.7/10 | Michael E.: "a good game, but not a great one"[^ref-30] |
| Computer Gaming World (via GameSpot, Dec 19, 1997) | 2/5 | Jim Cobb: "anything but an improvement"[^ref-67] |
| Computer Games Strategy Plus | — | Jim Pedicord: "the game the original should have been"[^ref-68] |

Keith Sullivan of PC Gamer praised a "historical scope that is simply breathtaking", the morale system and the historical module. He found the graphics "merely adequate", the AI "barely adequate", and the music repetitive. He concluded the game was "not the be all and end all of Civil War games for the hard-core wargamer" but "a good, competent offering" for casual players[^ref-66]. GameSpot called it "a solid, enjoyable, and challenging turn-based wargame" without true innovations. Its reviewer noted that "the initial release of CWG2 was plagued by bugs and crashes" but that the 1.0.1 patch fixed them[^ref-13]. He concluded: "If you're a wargame or Civil War enthusiast looking for an extensive list of scenarios to keep you occupied for a while, this is definitely the game for you"[^ref-34].

Jim Cobb of Computer Gaming World liked the branching campaigns and the scenario editor. But he wrote that the combat model "makes it impossible to win the game using historically authentic tactics". He found the elements "wasted on a completely broken combat system and a dated game engine"[^ref-67]. Jim Pedicord of Computer Games Strategy Plus was warmer. He praised the new units, zones of control, victory locations and improved AI, and judged that "though still a little on the weak side for a master tactician," the game "should appeal to most wargamers"[^ref-68].

### Modern Assessment

As of October 2025, MobyGames listed a critic average of 68% from 13 ratings and a player average of 3.5 out of 5 from 12 ratings[^ref-81]. A MyAbandonware commenter recalled online play: "Was very good at it; … very seldom lost." He also wrote: "The AI was terrible. When I wasn't playing online I would play against myself," and remembered building fictional battles with the editor for "quite an online community"[^ref-36]. A fan guide on HubPages documents getting the game running on Windows XP and later. It notes that Sierra's original 1.04 patch was 16-bit, and that a repackaged version exists for 64-bit Windows 7 and 8[^ref-3].

## Development

### Origins

The predecessor, Robert E. Lee: Civil War General, was developed by Impressions Games and published by Sierra On-Line[^ref-40]. Sierra's announcement of the sequel called it "the best selling Civil War game of 1996"[^ref-70]. According to Wikipedia, Sierra's in-house *InterAction* magazine went further and called it the best-selling wargame of 1996[^ref-39]. NPD data for September 1996 placed it at #8 on the reference software chart[^ref-58]. Its designer, Jeffrey Fiske, had aimed to "create a product that Civil War enthusiasts could play, regardless of prior gaming experience"[^ref-41], and Panzer General was a reference point for that game's design and title[^ref-47].

In 1997, Computer Games Strategy Plus reported the sequel under the working title "Civil War Generals". The team under Steve Grammont was "taking the wishes and complaints gamers had with R.E. Lee and Impressions' own internal ideas" and working them into "a vastly expanded product". Plans then included Eastern and Western campaigns playable from either side, around 40 scenarios, new units such as mortars and gunboats, two zoom levels, and an integrated scenario editor, with a fall release[^ref-69]. The report added that all of Impressions' 1997 titles would stress multiplayer play on the Sierra Internet Gaming System (SIGS), and Impressions general manager Peter Haffenreffer promised that single-player gaming would not suffer[^ref-69]. In Computer Games Strategy Plus's E3 coverage that year, the follow-up was described as focusing "on multiple campaigns covering the entire war," with an editor included[^ref-80].

### Production

MobyGames credits Douglas Gonya as game designer and Stephen Grammont as producer, with Peter Haffenreffer as executive producer and Chris Beatrice as creative director. Dean Lawson did the programming, Andrea Muzeroll and Peter Crafts the art, and Keith Zizza the music. The game is credited as "based on the original R.E.Lee: Civil War General game design of Jeffrey Fiske"[^ref-63]. Mort Künstler supplied Civil War paintings, and Jay Wertz & Associates the multimedia history and film footage[^ref-63].

Grammont had joined Impressions, Sierra's division in Cambridge, Massachusetts, as a QA manager. When the first game sold well, "to his surprise Grammont was tapped to produce a sequel, Civil War Generals 2"[^ref-56]. Late in production, Sierra founder [[Ken Williams]] "rolled into Cambridge" to push the team toward real-time strategy, since "Real-time strategy games had taken off"[^ref-56]. Grammont recalled: "So we fought him. I told the guy who founded Sierra, at a meeting … I told him he was wrong. ... They backed off. They let us do what we wanted to do."[^ref-56]

Sierra announced the game as a release of Impressions Software, "a division of Sierra On-line". It carried a street price of $49.00 and needed at least a 486/66 with Windows 95 or Windows 3.1 and 16MB RAM[^ref-70]. The same release noted that Sierra had recently merged with CUC International[^ref-70].

### Technical Specifications

Computer Gaming World listed the requirements as a 486-66 (Pentium recommended), 16MB RAM, 52MB of hard-drive space, Windows 3.1 or better, SVGA graphics and a 2x CD-ROM (4x recommended), with support for Sound Blaster-compatible sound cards[^ref-67]. MobyGames rates the game "Kids to Adults" under the old ESRB scheme and lists releases for both 32-bit and 16-bit Windows[^ref-62].

## Legacy

### Sales

Grammont said the game "wound up selling a lot. A lot more than they thought it would. The games that [Sierra] had made for real-time strategy all bombed."[^ref-56] It was still selling in late 1998. PC Data charts reported by IGN put Civil War Generals 2 at #4 for the week of September 27 to October 3, 1998[^ref-71], #5 for October 18–24[^ref-72] and #6 for October 25–31[^ref-73]. For October 1998 as a whole it ranked #12, at an average price of $29[^ref-74]. In NPD's July 1998 reference software chart, the Ultimate Civil War Collection rose from #58 to #7[^ref-76]. In January 1999, Sierra said the Civil War Generals series had sold "over 700,000 units worldwide to date"[^ref-75].

### Civil War Generals 3

Sierra and Impressions announced Civil War Generals 3 in January 1999, alongside [[1999 - Pharaoh|Pharaoh]], for a fall release[^ref-77]. Its official site credited Jeffrey Fiske and Steve Serafino as designers. It described turn-based management of the wartime economy, with combat in real time or resolved by a "quick battle" option[^ref-79]. In April 2000 Computer Games Strategy Plus reported that the game had been cancelled, mainly because projected sales would "barely" cover development costs[^ref-78].

### The Producer's Next Step

Soon after the fight with Sierra over real-time strategy, Grammont quit Sierra[^ref-56]. He and Charles Moylan went on to found Battlefront.com, the company behind the Combat Mission series[^ref-56].
