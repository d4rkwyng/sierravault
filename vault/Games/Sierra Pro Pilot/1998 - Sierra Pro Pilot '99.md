# Sierra Pro Pilot '99

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 29, 2026</small>

## Overview

Pro Pilot '99: The Complete Flight Simulator is a civilian flight simulator developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] for Windows 95 and Windows 98 in 1998[^ref-1][^ref-6]. Dynamix announced it on July 30, 1998 as "the second title in the company's general aviation simulation product line," following Sierra Pro Pilot 98, at a suggested retail price of $49.99[^ref-6]. IGN listed its release date as October 15, 1998, and Computer Games Magazine reported on November 19, 1998 that it was "now available"[^ref-8][^ref-9].

The game was mostly a corrective upgrade to Pro Pilot 98. Its main additions were 3D acceleration for 3Dfx graphics cards, a pop-up Operator's Handbook, flight tutorials developed with the National Association of Flight Instructors (NAFI), a sixth aircraft (the 1997 Cessna 172R) and scenery for Western Europe alongside North America[^ref-6][^ref-7][^ref-14]. IGN called it "The industry's latest boxed patch"[^ref-9]. GameSpot called it "a dramatic improvement over that first release"[^ref-10].

Critics praised its flight instruction, manuals and cloud rendering, but criticised a flight model that could not handle loops or spins and graphics that were only accelerated on 3Dfx hardware[^ref-9][^ref-10][^ref-12]. MobyGames averages its critic scores at 69% across 16 ratings[^ref-5].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Each of the six aircraft has its own detailed cockpit panel. GameSpot found them "significantly better than the panels in Flight Simulator 98," adding that "All the switches are there, from fuel tank selection to working landing lights"[^ref-10]. Computer Gaming World noted that the panels could be "easily operated with the mouse or the keyboard"[^ref-12]. AllGame lists flight yoke, joystick/gamepad, keyboard and mouse as supported controls[^ref-16]. Keys could be remapped only by editing files outside the game[^ref-14].

The headline interface addition was the Pop-Up Operator's Handbook, which can be viewed on screen while flying, even in 3D-accelerated mode[^ref-6]. It holds checklists, performance data, flight tutorials, scenic flights, challenge flights, custom tabs and personal notes[^ref-17]. GameSpot noted its "step-by-step checklists for procedures such as starting the aircraft, takeoff, climb, descent, and landing"[^ref-10].

Navigation aids include VORs, NDBs and ILS. A Global Positioning System display is tied to the autopilot[^ref-10][^ref-17]. Air traffic control is audio[^ref-17]. Players can choose to hear "yourself" reading back the instructions, in Games Domain's words, or let a talking copilot handle radio calls[^ref-12][^ref-14]. In the Cessna, an instructor is also available to give advice[^ref-14].

The game runs only at 640×480, in both 2D and 3D modes[^ref-10]. Games Domain noted that the Windows interface screens were also fixed at 640×480, which left the flight-planning maps "way too small"[^ref-14].

### Structure and Progression

At startup the player chooses a US flight or a European flight. The two scenery areas are separate, so a transatlantic flight isn't possible[^ref-14]. Training is layered:

- **Ground lessons:** animated video tutorials. GameSpot counts 30 AVI demonstration videos[^ref-10]. Computer Gaming World counts 31 "Ground Lessons" carried over from the previous version, averaging about two minutes each[^ref-12].
- **Tutorial flights:** 48 step-by-step lessons developed with NAFI and reached through the Operator's Handbook[^ref-6][^ref-12]. Players can also write their own tutorials and share them[^ref-12].
- **Flight assignments:** the manual describes twenty flight assignments, all in US airspace. One is a marginal-VFR flight that forces a diversion to an alternate airport[^ref-14].
- **Free flight:** the Flight Planning Wizard can generate routes and random weather. On a filed flight plan, ATC gives clearance to each waypoint. Without one, ATC instructions are available near airports[^ref-12][^ref-14][^ref-17].

### Aircraft and Scenery

Pro Pilot '99 includes six aircraft[^ref-6][^ref-17]:

- **Cessna C172R Skyhawk (1997)**, new in this release
- **Cessna C172P Skyhawk**
- **Beechcraft Bonanza V35**
- **Beechcraft Baron B58**
- **Beechcraft King Air B200** (the official site calls it the Super King Air B200)
- **Cessna CitationJet 525**

The 172R is the only new plane over Pro Pilot 98's five. IGN put it bluntly: it is "just a more recent version of the original game's Cessna 172"[^ref-7][^ref-9].

The scenery covers the United States, Western Europe and parts of Canada (Montreal and Toronto), built from more than 38 million elevation points. Dynamix said 35 metropolitan areas were highly detailed[^ref-6][^ref-16][^ref-17]. Airport counts differ by source. The July 1998 press release and GameSpot say "over 3,500," while the archived official site and AllGame say over 4,300[^ref-6][^ref-10][^ref-16][^ref-17].

Reviewers found the terrain uneven. GameSpot called the height modelling "very well done" but the textures "very muddy looking," noting that desert Sedona was rendered "in a lush green"[^ref-10]. Computer Gaming World wrote that "high-detail" in the 35 cities was "in the eye of the beholder"[^ref-12]. Games Domain found the US scenery more detailed than Europe's, with Glasgow Airport reduced to "just two runways and a control tower"[^ref-14].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

**GameSpot** (Denny Atkin, posted November 16, 1998) gave it 7.5. The review called it "the program that the first version should have been"[^ref-10][^ref-11]. Atkin praised the cockpits, tutorials and clouds, but found that in "unusual attitudes," the flight modelling "falls apart." A Bonanza's nose freezes when it is "perpendicular to the ground" in a loop, and spins aren't handled properly[^ref-10].

**IGN** (Luke Shannon, October 23, 1998) gave it 5.8. Its summary read: "The industry's latest boxed patch makes a handful of improvements -- but it's too little, too late." Shannon reported that loops were impossible because no plane could pitch past vertical. The game crashed or locked up in most of his first sessions until he did a full installation. He concluded that "it's hard to imagine why anyone would buy Pro Pilot '99"[^ref-9].

**Computer Gaming World** (John Nolan, January 1999, "Flight Instructor in a Box") rated it three and a half stars, describing "an average, three-star civilian flight simulation" whose "outstanding, comprehensive flight instruction earns the extra half star." Nolan wrote that "PRO PILOT '99 should have been the first release of PRO PILOT." He faulted the missing torque effects, the mild stalls and the impossibility of spins, calling the result "negative training"[^ref-12].

**Computer Games Magazine** (Greg Ingram, January 6, 1999) headlined its review "Not the plane of choice for pros." Ingram called it "a definite improvement over the previous release" but said the flight modelling was "so bad it is almost laughable." He noted a King Air could take off in 200 feet without flaps. He praised the ATC and called the Flight Companion manual "a superb work of documentation"[^ref-13].

**Games Domain** (Martin Smith) called it "a pretty good game that I've enjoyed playing." He added that "there is really nothing new on offer," and listed the UK retail price as £39.99[^ref-14].

**Jeuxvideo.com** reviewed the English-language version, which it listed with a May 1999 release date, and scored it 9/20. It said the release felt more like an add-on than a full game, and that the plane refused to pass the vertical in a loop[^ref-15].

The game won an AVSIM Gold Medal for Excellence in Flight Sim Design[^ref-8]. The official site quoted the AVSIM score as "95 out of a possible 100 points" and also listed a FlightSim.com Award of Excellence[^ref-18].

### Modern Assessment

Little retrospective coverage of Pro Pilot '99 could be found. On MobyGames, its 69% critic average spans a wide range, from Power Play (89%) and Gamezilla (87%) down to PC Jeux (49%) and Jeuxvideo.com (45%). Its player average is 1.8 out of 5, from three ratings[^ref-5].

## Development

### Origins

*Pro Pilot 98 context:* Sierra's first Pro Pilot went on sale without any 3D accelerator support[^ref-9]. GameSpot's February 9, 1998 review of Pro Pilot 98 scored it 6.1. That review said the game showed "definite signs of having been rushed to market for the holidays," with "over 30 bugs fixed in the first patch"[^ref-21]. Pro Pilot 98 nonetheless sold over 275,000 units, according to Sierra's October 1, 1998 press release for the budget Pro Pilot USA[^ref-20].

Dynamix announced Pro Pilot '99 at Oshkosh, Wisconsin, on July 30, 1998. Its press release billed the game as the first PC flight simulator developed with NAFI's cooperation. NAFI worked on 48 text tutorials based on the Federal Aviation Regulations and "assisted Dynamix with the accuracy of the simulator's aircraft models"[^ref-6][^ref-7]. Dynamix also partnered with the Experimental Aircraft Association's Flying Start program, whose information was to be included in the packaging[^ref-6].

Director and designer Mark Pechnick said: "With the release of Pro Pilot '99 and the strategic support of NAFI, we give both aspiring pilots as well as armchair pilots an intense, personalized, realistic flying experience from the safety of home"[^ref-6]. NAFI executive director Sean E. Elliott called it "a excellent way to advance a student's development of aviation procedural skills"[^ref-6].

### Production

MobyGames credits 56 people on the Windows version[^ref-2]. The key roles were:

**Development Credits:**[^ref-2]
- **Director:** Mark R. Pechnick
- **Assistant Director:** Brian Gallardo
- **Producer:** Graeme Bayless
- **Lead Programming:** Caedmon Irias, Alan C. Zander
- **Art Director:** Brian Broderick
- **Scenery Development Leader:** Scott Huette
- **Sound:** Charles Barth, Ken Rogers
- **Quality Assurance Manager:** Lloyd Madden

According to Computer Gaming World, the engine and aircraft sounds were "recorded from the actual specific aircraft you're flying"[^ref-12]. IGN noted "Actual recordings of each plane's engines and digitized air traffic controller voices"[^ref-9].

The box was documentation-heavy. AllGame lists a 270-page Flight Companion, a 64-page "Getting Started" booklet and six fold-out quick reference cards[^ref-16]. The official site advertised a "285 page Flight Guidance book"[^ref-17]. The training videos sat on the second CD[^ref-14].

### Technical Achievements

With a 3Dfx Voodoo, Voodoo2 or Banshee card, the game added bilinear filtering, transparency, MIP-mapping and improved haze. GameSpot called the result "the best clouds we've seen in any flight sim"[^ref-10]. Computer Gaming World agreed: "No other sim can match the cloud quality in PRO PILOT '99"[^ref-12]. The press release also listed glowing runway lights, sun glare, cloud layering, fog, wind gusts and support for flight in instrument meteorological conditions[^ref-6]. With any other 3D card, though, GameSpot found the graphics "little-improved from the original release"[^ref-10].

### Technical Issues

- **Flight model:** loops were impossible in the shipped game, which wouldn't let any plane pitch past vertical, and spins weren't simulated[^ref-9][^ref-10][^ref-12]. Computer Gaming World found no torque effect on takeoff and only a gentle drift after an engine failure in the twins[^ref-12].
- **Takeoff performance:** Games Domain reported that the unpatched Cessna took off "like there was a rocket lit under it"[^ref-14]. Computer Games Magazine took off in a King Air in 200 feet without flaps[^ref-13].
- **Stability:** IGN's copy crashed or locked up in most of its first sessions. The problem seemed to disappear after a full installation[^ref-9].
- **Hardware:** acceleration worked only on 3Dfx cards[^ref-9][^ref-10].
- **Expandability:** the game had no third-party scenery support and no multiplayer[^ref-13][^ref-14].

## Legacy

Dynamix offered a $20 rebate to owners of Pro Pilot 98 or Pro Pilot USA until March 31, 1999[^ref-8]. MobyGames lists Pro Pilot '99 in the German compilation *Sierra's Fliegende Asse* (2001)[^ref-1].

The series continued with Pro Pilot 2000. The official Pro Pilot site announced it for the third quarter of 1999, with an all-new ATC system, improved flight modelling and the Hawaiian Islands[^ref-19].
