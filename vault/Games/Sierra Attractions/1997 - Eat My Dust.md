# Eat My Dust

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

**Eat My Dust** is a 3D racing game developed by Davidson & Associates, its Funnybone Interactive studio, and Van Duyne Engineering, and published by Sierra On-Line for Windows 95[^ref-1][^ref-13]. The manual calls it "the wacky 3D road race" and Sierra's Winter 1997 Buyer's Guide "The Zany 3-D Road Race", both with cars rather than karts[^ref-10][^ref-27]; MobyGames describes it as a go-kart racer[^ref-1]. The contemporary Games Domain review compared it to Nintendo's Mario Kart: players race to checkpoints against the clock and computer opponents while flinging chickens, golf balls and swarms of bees at their rivals[^ref-15]. It was aimed at players aged eight and up[^ref-15][^ref-11][^ref-27], and received mixed reviews[^ref-9].

Funnybone Interactive was a children's entertainment and educational software developer in Canton, Connecticut, which Davidson & Associates acquired in February 1995[^ref-22]. The game's credits list "Eat My Dust" and "FUNNYBONE Interactive" as trademarks of Davidson & Associates, with the Catalina 3D system supplied by Van Duyne Engineering[^ref-23]. The game offers four racing environments (Alien Asteroid, Bleach Bone Gulch, Super Speedway and Backroad Rally), each with three levels of difficulty[^ref-8][^ref-13][^ref-17][^ref-27]. Davidson's own pre-release web page of February 1997 names the fourth environment "Country Challenge" instead of Backroad Rally[^ref-26].

> [!note] Release date
> Sources disagree on the release year. Sierra Chest gives a first release of 1996-11-16, Wikipedia gives 1996, and the credits' copyright line reads © 1996[^ref-4][^ref-9][^ref-23]. NeverDie's product listing gives "Released: 1996"[^ref-8]. MobyGames, PCGamingWiki and Home of the Underdogs list 1997[^ref-1][^ref-6][^ref-3]. Games Domain dates the release to February 1997, and Electric Playground's review is dated February 25, 1997[^ref-15][^ref-13].
>
> The game was on sale earlier than those review dates. Davidson's official site, as archived on December 19, 1996, announced the game as "now available everywhere" and offered the full version "with easy online ordering in time for Christmas" alongside a free demo[^ref-25]. A Sierra advertisement in the December 1996 issue of *Disney Adventures* promoted the game and its free internet play[^ref-28], and a Sierra advertisement with the same copy ran in the January 1997 issue[^ref-31]. By February 3, 1997 the site said it was "now available in stores", with a web price of $29.95[^ref-26]. The Winter 1997 Sierra Buyer's Guide lists it at the same price[^ref-27]. This page keeps 1997 as the headline year, following MobyGames.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls
Players race cars with the aim of reaching checkpoints within the allotted time and finishing first, while avoiding obstacles and computer-controlled competitors[^ref-2][^ref-15]. Before a race, players pick one of six drivers and one of five vehicles; the manual's wording is "Pick from 5 tricked-out cars"[^ref-26][^ref-27][^ref-10]. Each vehicle has its own top speed, acceleration and maneuverability[^ref-4][^ref-8][^ref-15]. Vehicles range from a sporty buggy to the huge "groundbreaker"[^ref-15]. MobyGames' description adds that the vehicle styles and sizes all handle differently[^ref-1]. The game supports keyboard, joystick and steering wheel control, and offers detail levels that trade picture quality for speed[^ref-4][^ref-11].

### Structure and Progression
Players choose one of four terrains and an easy, medium or hard track layout[^ref-4]. Electric Playground described the three layouts as "oval, squiggly oval and super squiggly oval"[^ref-13]. Three difficulty settings (Novice, Advanced and Pro) control car speed and opponent aggression[^ref-4]. There are three modes:
- **Time Trial:** a solo run.
- **Head to Head:** two players with no computer opponents.
- **Road Rally:** a five-car race[^ref-4].

An optional "Road Hog" character in a clownish car interferes with all racers[^ref-4][^ref-11]. Two players can race on one computer in split-screen mode[^ref-4][^ref-15]. Two-player races over the internet were also supported, free of charge apart from the player's own internet access fees, but that service is no longer active[^ref-4][^ref-13][^ref-26][^ref-28]. Custom tracks required the Kid's Track Editor, which was not on the game CD but was "available Jan. '97 on the Eat My Dust Web site", according to the manual[^ref-10][^ref-4][^ref-15]. Davidson's site in February 1997 offered the track editor as a download for owners of the game, and Sierra's Winter 1997 Buyer's Guide advertised the same download[^ref-26][^ref-27].

### Puzzles and Mechanics
Each car carries unusual weapons that hinder opponents, and more ammunition can be picked up during a race[^ref-2][^ref-15]. Electric Playground listed rubber chickens, golf balls, slime balls and bees[^ref-13]. Gamer's Zone noted rubber chickens, beehives and golf bags lying on the road to be collected[^ref-11]. Drivers shout catchphrases during races, which can be switched off[^ref-15][^ref-4]. Players can also send "taunts" to internet opponents[^ref-11].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| Games Domain | — | No numeric score; "Seen it done better before"[^ref-15] |
| Gamer's Zone | 3 globes | "A nice little offering"; overall score shown as three globe images, which Wikipedia gives as 3 out of 5[^ref-11][^ref-9] |
| Electric Playground | 3/10 | "Just not very much fun"[^ref-13] |

### Critical Analysis

Games Domain called the game "an attempt at recreating a Mario Kart style game on the PC"[^ref-15]. The review said the graphics "need a powerful PC to run smoothly on". Even then, a slow frame rate gave "absolutely no sensation of speed whatsoever"[^ref-15]. It also criticized tracks "too narrow for the width of the cars", occasional freezes mid-race, and cars getting stuck in scenery[^ref-15]. It praised the user-friendly option screens, split-screen and internet play, but concluded that "Eat My Dust offers nothing new to the racing genre"[^ref-15].

Electric Playground found the graphics and sound "passable", with "some shimmering and popping in and out of scenery"[^ref-13]. The reviewer called the controls "horrendous" and found the in-car perspective nauseating[^ref-13]. They liked the character design and the rubber-chicken weapons, but concluded that "the whole game is just not very much fun" and scored it 3 out of 10[^ref-13].

Gamer's Zone was kinder. Its reviewer noted "quite a bit of herky-jerkiness" on a Pentium 90 but called the game "a nice little offering" that promised "anyone aged eight and up a lot of entertainment"[^ref-11].

### Later Assessment

Home of the Underdogs described the game as "a decent racing game for kids that in many ways merits its obscurity". After quoting the 1997 Games Domain review, the site added that "even that younger audience is well advised to stick with Mario Kart on the console systems rather than play this inferior copy-cat"[^ref-3]. MyAbandonware republishes the same write-up, and its users rate the game 4.45/5 from 11 votes (checked October 29, 2026)[^ref-14].

## Development

### Origins
The game came together during a period of corporate consolidation. On February 20, 1996, CUC International announced it would acquire both Davidson & Associates and Sierra On-Line[^ref-24], and the Davidson acquisition closed in July 1996[^ref-22]. Davidson had acquired Funnybone Interactive in February 1995[^ref-22]. Funnybone had previously worked with Sierra on *[[1996 - Stay Tooned!|Stay Tooned!]]*, and one of Eat My Dust's drivers returns from that game[^ref-13][^ref-17].

### Production
MobyGames credits the following team[^ref-23]:
- **Producer:** Shannon Sullivan
- **Executive producer:** Michael Albanese
- **Catalina 3D engine and game programming:** Phil Van Duyne
- **Race world editor and internet programming:** Eddie Rohwedder
- **Lead artist and race world designer:** Ray Cooper
- **Original music:** David Luis Ortega, Rich Seitz and Tom Zehnder
- **Car designs:** Dan Marseno and Michael Carangelo
- **Writers:** Seth Feinberg, Bill Gusky, John Piel, Mark Schwarz and Shannon Sullivan

Funnybone Interactive handled character and interface design, with Joel Fried as executive producer, Susan Swanson-Decker as creative director and John Siciliano as director of animation[^ref-23]. The voice cast includes Michael Gough and Edie Mirman. The credits also thank Sierra On-Line and Synergistic Software[^ref-23]. Sierra's Winter 1997 Buyer's Guide states that "Eat My Dust is developed by Van Dyne Engineering and co-developed by Funnybone", spelling the engineering firm's name "Dyne" where MobyGames credits "Phil Van Duyne"[^ref-27][^ref-23]. The credits carry a © 1996 Davidson & Associates copyright, with "Catalina 3D System courtesy of Van Duyne Engineering"[^ref-23].

### Technical Specifications

**Minimum Requirements:**[^ref-11][^ref-6][^ref-10][^ref-26][^ref-27]
- **Operating System:** Windows 95
- **Processor:** Intel Pentium 90
- **RAM:** 8 MB (16 MB recommended)
- **Graphics:** 256-color graphics card (local bus or PCI video)
- **Sound:** Windows 95-compatible sound card
- **Media:** Double-speed CD-ROM drive
- **Optional:** Joystick or steering wheel

### Technical Issues
Games Domain reported that the game needed a powerful PC to run smoothly and still suffered from a slow frame rate. It also reported mid-race freezes and collisions that left vehicles stuck in scenery[^ref-15]. Gamer's Zone found the game jerky at every detail setting on its Pentium 90 test machine[^ref-11]. Electric Playground noted scenery pop-in and said lowering the detail level traded picture quality for speed[^ref-13].

## Legacy

### Reputation
Home of the Underdogs calls Eat My Dust "one of the most little-known games published by Sierra"[^ref-3]. Its online multiplayer server is no longer active[^ref-4].

### Preservation
The game is available on abandonware and preservation sites, including MyAbandonware and the Internet Archive, which also hosts the manual[^ref-14][^ref-2][^ref-10]. PCGamingWiki notes the following[^ref-6]:
- The game uses a 16-bit installer, and it gives a manual-installation workaround for 64-bit Windows.
- Widescreen resolution support is listed as "640x480 only".
- Three demos exist[^ref-6]. Davidson's site in February 1997 offered them in three sizes (4.3, 7 and 11 MB), each limited to one track, one player character and one car[^ref-26].

The Funnybone Interactive Wiki notes compatibility issues when running the game in Windows 95/98 virtual machines[^ref-17].

The game also circulated on magazine cover discs. The Brazilian *Revista do CD-ROM* no. 21 (April 1997) and the Portuguese edition, *Mundo do CD-ROM* no. 07 (May 1997), both list it among the games on their cover CD-ROMs[^ref-29][^ref-30]. The Brazilian magazine's feature on the game (pp. 15–16) tells readers to "select Single Player (the option available in this version)", so the cover-CD copy offered Single Player only[^ref-32].
