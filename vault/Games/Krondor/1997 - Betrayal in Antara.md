# Betrayal in Antara

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Betrayal in Antara is a 1997 role-playing game developed and published by Sierra On-Line as a follow-up to the acclaimed 1993 title Betrayal at Krondor[^ref-1][^ref-6]. While utilizing an updated version of Betrayal at Krondor's game engine, the game takes place in an entirely new fantasy world called Ramar rather than Raymond E. Feist's Midkemia universe[^ref-7]. As author Raymond E. Feist himself clarified, "Betrayal in Antara by Sierra is a 'sequel' to BaK only insofar as it's using the same general game engine and is created by the company that owned Dynamix. Other than that it has nothing to do with my work"[^ref-3].

The game emerged from Sierra's need to create a new RPG after losing the rights to produce another game based on Raymond Feist's The Riftwar Cycle[^ref-1]. Designer Peter Sarrett became involved with the project in an unusual way: during one of the long lunch breaks he spent playing Betrayal at Krondor, CEO Ken Williams stopped by and the two talked about the game and computer RPGs; later Sarrett wondered aloud to Williams why Sierra had not followed up on its success, and soon afterwards received an email from Ken saying simply "Come see me in my office", where he was asked to design Sierra's next role-playing game[^ref-5]. Sierra positioned the game as their return to role-playing games, with PC Gamer reporting on June 30, 1997 that "Sierra On-Line has begun shipping Betrayal in Antara, a follow-up to their award-winning roleplaying game Betrayal at Krondor"[^ref-6].

## Story Summary

Set in the Empire of Antara in the fantasy world of Ramar, players take on the role of Aren and William as they become embroiled in a conspiracy involving a dying man named Gregor who presses a medallion into William's hand while speaking his last word: "Consort"[^ref-8]. Near Panizo the pair rescue Kaelyn Usher from bandits and she joins them[^ref-1], making up the three-character party the player navigates[^ref-7]. The story unfolds across nine chapters[^ref-8] as players work to "save the empire of Antara from corruption and war"[^ref-9]. Unlike its predecessor, the game does not continue Raymond E. Feist's Riftwar saga but instead starts a new tale in a different fantasy world[^ref-7].

The narrative explores themes of political intrigue and betrayal as the protagonists navigate the crumbling Antaran Empire. The game features 34 interactive characters throughout the adventure[^ref-10], with players seeking "power, fortune and glory" while uncovering the larger conspiracy threatening the realm[^ref-10].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Designer Peter Sarrett made a conscious decision to maintain familiarity with the predecessor, stating "I decided early on not to fix what wasn't broken, and BAK players will find the interface and combat system of Betrayal in Antara familiar"[^ref-5]. The game requires a 2-button PC compatible mouse for navigation[^ref-5] and features both first-person exploration and third-person turn-based combat on a hexagonal grid[^ref-5][^ref-8].

Unlike Betrayal at Krondor, most towns in Antara can be physically explored rather than accessed through menu screens[^ref-7]. The game also offers an optional full-screen view that was not available in its predecessor[^ref-7]. The world has day/night cycles with simulated light levels[^ref-8], and the party needs torches to see at night or underground[^ref-5].

### Structure and Progression

The game employs a skill-based progression system without traditional experience points or levels[^ref-8][^ref-12]. Spells are cast using health and stamina points instead of a separate magic point system[^ref-8]. Aren is the only party member capable of casting magic, and players can increase his magic stat by sleeping at his home in Briara[^ref-13].

Gameplay tips from community guides suggest that "Scouting is most important for the other guy and gals...combine its with stealth...it will virtually negate any ambush by enemy"[^ref-13]. The party must carry food, and characters who go without it begin to starve and lose stamina; the world also contains chests sealed with unusual puzzle locks[^ref-5][^ref-8].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The game includes various cheat codes. Players can enter "Ask a glass of water" to teleport to the start of a chapter, "Why am I so dull" to learn skills faster, or "Some call me tim" to kill all opponents in combat[^ref-14]. A user comment on the Easter egg database ties "Ask a glass of water" to Ford Prefect's line in Douglas Adams' Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy and suggests "Why am I so dull" refers to Marvin the robot[^ref-14].

Combat maintains the turn-based tactical approach of its predecessor, with battles playing out on hexagonal grids. The game features both battle themes and location-specific music, including tavern songs and "The Ballad Of The Cyrilan" musical sequence[^ref-15].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Betrayal in Antara received mixed reviews from critics, with many praising the gameplay while criticizing technical aspects. The game achieved a Metacritic user score of 7.4/10 based on 7 user ratings[^ref-16].

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| GameSpot | 6.6/10 (Fair) | Criticized graphics quality and linearity[^ref-17] |
| Next Generation (Oct 1997) | 3 stars | "Antara succeeds it in the fashion of Doom II"[^ref-32] |
| MobyGames Critics | 69% average (21 ratings) | Mixed professional reception[^ref-18] |
| IMDb | 6.9/10 (14 ratings) | User rating[^ref-19] |

GameSpot's reviewer Desslock noted that "For gamers willing to tolerate substandard graphics and linear gameplay, Betrayal in Antara succeeds in providing an enjoyable role-playing experience"[^ref-17]. However, he criticized the visuals severely, stating the graphics are "frankly, as ugly as an orc" and describing the color palette as "muddy Monet"[^ref-17].

### Modern Assessment

Player reviews on MobyGames and other platforms reflect a divided opinion. One reviewer noted that "The game doesn't make a good impression graphically from the word go" and criticized the voice acting as "Very bad"[^ref-18]. However, another player defended the game, writing "This is a great game and deserves great reviews. It's not fair to mark it down for not playing on newer systems"[^ref-10].

On the RPG Codex forum, one poster summed it up: "Game's not as good as BaK but worth playing"[^ref-21]. Technical issues have been a persistent complaint: GameSpot's reviewer hit a fatal error at the beginning of chapter 9 in the initial release, along with crashes when acting outside the linear plot[^ref-17], and forum users later reported major issues running the game on Windows XP[^ref-21].

## Development

### Origins

The development of Betrayal in Antara arose from Sierra's desire to capitalize on the success of Betrayal at Krondor while navigating licensing constraints. As gaming historian Jimmy Maher noted, the game "copied some of the interface elements and gameplay approaches of its predecessor, but moved the action to a generic fantasy world, to less satisfying effect"[^ref-4]. Sierra had lost the rights to produce another game based on Raymond Feist's Midkemia, requiring them to create the new world of Ramar[^ref-1].

Peter Sarrett's involvement as designer grew out of his own enthusiasm for Betrayal at Krondor after he joined Sierra in 1994. He recalled: "Loading it up at lunch time, I'd wind up playing well into the afternoon before I realized the time had passed. Many evenings I stayed at work past midnight hacking my way through just one more group of enemies"[^ref-5]. A conversation with Ken Williams during one of those lunch breaks, and Sarrett's later question about why Sierra had not followed up on the game, led to Williams asking him to design Sierra's next RPG[^ref-5].

### Production

The development team included lead programmer Rikki Cleland-Hura, who helped flesh out world details, while the art team created the visual look for the Antaran Empire setting[^ref-5]. The score was composed by David Henry, who also handled dialogue and sound effects[^ref-5][^ref-23]; the soundtrack archived at KHInsider runs to 72 files totaling 1 hour and 25 minutes[^ref-15].

Sierra released the game on multiple CD-ROMs, installed to the hard drive from the first disc[^ref-5]. The minimum requirements were Windows 3.1 or Windows 95, an 80486DX2 66 MHz processor, 16 MB RAM, 20 MB of hard drive space, 256-color video and a 4X CD-ROM drive[^ref-11], plus a standard 2-button mouse[^ref-5].

### Technical Achievements

GameSpot's Desslock noted that "Even though Antara is one of the first RPGs to utilize high-resolution SVGA graphics, the graphics are, frankly, as ugly as an orc"[^ref-17]; a MobyGames player review likewise points out that the game is "limited to 640x480 resolution in 256 colors, outdated at the time of release"[^ref-18]. To create the backgrounds, the development team took photos of different landscapes and touched them up with 3D tools to make them look like paintings[^ref-1].

A significant patch (version 1.10) was released to address "the problem some users have had when loading chapters, as well as a few other issues"[^ref-24]. The patch is preserved in patch archives[^ref-25]. GameSpot reported that Sierra had released a beta patch "that should squash most of these bugs (including the chapter 9 bug)"[^ref-17]. Full walkthroughs document the game's content[^ref-31].

## Legacy

Betrayal in Antara represents Sierra's attempt to create a successor without the licensed intellectual property that made the original successful. While it maintained the gameplay mechanics that players enjoyed, the generic fantasy setting and technical issues prevented it from achieving the same critical acclaim as Betrayal at Krondor.

The game is sold digitally on GOG.com and Steam in a pack bundled with Betrayal at Krondor[^ref-33][^ref-34]. Gaming wikis provide technical compatibility information for modern systems[^ref-11], and community forums continue to offer technical fixes for Windows XP and later operating systems[^ref-21].

Despite mixed reception, the game provided "over 100 hours of entertaining storytelling"[^ref-17] according to contemporary reviews. Sarrett concluded in the game manual: "We hope this is but the first of many visits you'll be making to Ramar, just as this is Sierra's first step in our return to role playing games"[^ref-5].
