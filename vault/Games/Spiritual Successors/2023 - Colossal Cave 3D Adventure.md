# Colossal Cave 3D Adventure

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>


## Overview

Colossal Cave 3D Adventure (sold as *Colossal Cave*) is a 3D and VR reimagining of the seminal text adventure created by Will Crowther in 1976 and expanded by Don Woods in 1977[^ref-6]. Developed by Cygnus Entertainment, the company founded by Sierra On-Line founders [[Ken Williams]] and [[Roberta Williams]], with Roberta as creative director[^ref-15] and Ken as lead engineer (MobyGames credits him under "Lead Engineers")[^ref-23], the game marked their first new game in some 25 years[^ref-2][^ref-19]. The project transforms the original text-based cave exploration experience into a first-person point-and-click adventure that Ken Williams described as "intended for the whole family"[^ref-3][^ref-11].

The game challenges players to explore a vast underground cave, collecting treasures while solving puzzles and avoiding hazards including hostile dwarves and a wandering pirate[^ref-5][^ref-9]. Don Woods's 1977 version of the original, on which the remake is based, had fifteen treasures and 140 map locations[^ref-4]. As in the original, players aim for a perfect score of 350 points[^ref-2][^ref-9]. The original's famous codewords "Xyzzy" and "Plugh" are part of the game[^ref-5]; in the text original, "Xyzzy" teleports the player between the building and the Debris Room[^ref-4].

Colossal Cave was released on January 19, 2023 for PC, Mac, Linux, Steam Deck, PlayStation 5, Nintendo Switch and Meta Quest 2, with further console and VR versions following later in 2023[^ref-6]. The original Colossal Cave Adventure established conventions that became standard in the adventure game genre and inspired Roberta Williams to create Mystery House in 1980, the game that launched Sierra On-Line[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

Like the original text game, Colossal Cave offers little story: the player knows only that the cave holds treasure and magic, and that many who entered never came back[^ref-9]. The remake opens with the original game's text, now spoken by a narrator, as the player approaches a lone cabin by a stream; a key found in the cabin unlocks the door into the cave[^ref-9]. Inside are diverse rooms and caverns, with the sound of roaring underground rivers[^ref-9].

Treasures found in the cave are returned to the cabin for points[^ref-9]. The cave is home to dwarves (seven, of course), who appear at random and throw knives, and a pirate who steals the player's treasure and keeps it in his own secret room[^ref-5][^ref-9].

The player encounters various obstacles, including a giant snake that only a small bird will scare away, a troll at the troll bridge, and a bear[^ref-5][^ref-9]. The original's "maze of twisty little passages, all alike" became a byword in hacker culture[^ref-4], and the remake will, as Eurogamer put it, "trap you in a maze and offer no explanation of what you have to do to get out"[^ref-11]. A dragon also appears[^ref-9].

The goal is to reach the maximum score of 350 points by finding all the treasures and leaving the cave safely[^ref-4][^ref-9]. The ending passes through the Witt's End room into a large warehouse, where an office of bluish-green elves congratulates the player and shows the final score[^ref-9].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Colossal Cave 3D Adventure presents the classic cave exploration experience from a first-person perspective with point-and-click style interaction: there is an inventory, and "you use things on other things"[^ref-11]. The game supports both standard monitors and virtual reality headsets, with the VR version offering two movement options, Comfort Locomotion and Classic Locomotion[^ref-9].

Players cycle through collected objects in the inventory to use them on puzzles[^ref-9]. Mega Visions found that in VR, aiming was "rather difficult" and that the inventory filling the whole view was more distracting than on a flat screen[^ref-9]. A charming narrator reads out the original text descriptions of each location[^ref-9].

### Structure and Progression

The game faithfully recreates the original's structure of interconnected cave chambers and passages[^ref-2], which the official fact sheet describes as 14 distinct regions[^ref-6]. Players are free to explore in any direction, though certain areas require specific items or puzzle solutions to access. Areas include:

- **Surface Area:** The cabin by the stream, where treasures are returned for points[^ref-9]
- **Upper Cave:** Early passages such as the Debris Room and the bird and snake puzzle[^ref-4][^ref-9]
- **The Maze:** Multiple sections of confusingly similar passages that challenge players' mapping skills[^ref-4]
- **Named rooms from the original:** Including the Hall of the Mountain King, the Orient loop and Witt's End[^ref-9]

Progress is tracked through a scoring system that awards points for collecting treasures, discovering locations, and solving puzzles[^ref-2]. Original maps from the 1970s and 1980s remain compatible with the 3D remake, allowing veteran players to use their old notes[^ref-7].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzle design remains faithful to the 1976 original, maintaining what some modern reviewers consider dated mechanics[^ref-2]. Players must experiment with items and their environment, often without explicit guidance on what to do next[^ref-9]. Key mechanics include:

- **Treasure Collection:** Finding treasures and returning them to the cabin for points[^ref-9]
- **Magic Words:** The original's codewords "Xyzzy" and "Plugh" return[^ref-5]
- **Item Manipulation:** Using inventory items on other objects to progress[^ref-11]
- **Environmental Hazards:** Keeping an eye on the score before the lamp goes out, surviving dwarf attacks, and preventing the pirate from stealing treasures[^ref-7][^ref-9]

The game does not hold the player's hand, requiring careful observation and experimentation[^ref-9]. The narrator will eventually offer hints, but accepting one costs points[^ref-9]. A classic telephone hint line is also available at (206) 207-9017[^ref-6][^ref-9].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Colossal Cave 3D Adventure received mixed reviews upon release, with critics divided between appreciation for its historical preservation and criticism of its dated design. Worth Playing awarded the game 80/100, stating "Colossal Cave is a good adventure game that will appeal more to those with some nostalgia for classics and classic sensibilities"[^ref-12]. GamingTrend also scored it 80/100, praising how "this modern remake brings the wonder of a text adventure to life in full 3D"[^ref-12].

God is a Geek gave a more measured 60/100, noting "Colossal Cave is a love letter to the 1976 original which fans will enjoy, but newcomers will likely be confused, intimidated, and frustrated by its design"[^ref-12]. Adventure Gamers awarded 50/100 (2.5/5 stars), characterizing the game as "a near-equal balance of good and bad" and observing that "despite a lovingly crafted 3D reimagining, Colossal Cave recreates the original too closely to appeal to much more than game history buffs and nostalgia seekers – or very patient players who don't mind repetitive, arduous gameplay"[^ref-2].

More critical assessments came from Metro GameCentral (40/100), calling it "a misguided attempt to recreate one of gaming's oldest and most influential classics, that's let down by outdated visuals and prehistoric gameplay elements"[^ref-12]. Slant Magazine (40/100) noted the game "can hardly be called a modernization, because it would have felt antiquated even if it came out 20 years ago"[^ref-12]. Movies Games and Tech also awarded 40/100[^ref-12].

### Modern Assessment

User reception has been considerably more positive than critical reviews. Steam rates the game "Very Positive", with 90% of 87 user reviews positive as of October 2026[^ref-13]. The Meta Quest store shows a user rating of 3.8/5 from 86 ratings[^ref-14]. Game industry figures have expressed enthusiasm, with George Broussard, co-creator of Duke Nukem, stating "Exploring Colossal Cave in first person was fun! It's fast, immersive, and just a joy to explore"[^ref-5].

Mega Visions summarized the divide in a review rating the game as "GOOD," explaining: "Essentially, your enjoyment of Colossal Cave completely depends on your connection to the original 1976 text adventure. For fans of the original game, Roberta Williams' redesign will feel like a welcome update that provides visuals without tampering with core gameplay functions"[^ref-9]. Eurogamer's Robert Purchese offered a thoughtful perspective on launch day: "I also find it fascinating, because which other game today will trap you in a maze and offer no explanation of what you have to do to get out? ... once you settle into that rhythm, there is a nostalgic pleasure in it"[^ref-11].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **Metacritic:** 53/100 (14 critic reviews), 8.2/10 (24 user ratings)[^ref-12]
- **Steam:** Very Positive, 90% of 87 reviews[^ref-13]
- **Meta Quest Store:** 3.8/5 (86 ratings)[^ref-14]

## Development

### Origins

The project originated during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown when Ken Williams, after decades away from game development, began experimenting with the Unity game engine[^ref-10]. During this period, he had written a book about the old Sierra days, whose success surprised him and brought back memories of their time making games[^ref-10]. When Ken started coding for fun, Roberta Williams suggested adapting Colossal Cave to 3D[^ref-3].

Roberta Williams explained the significance of the choice: "Colossal Cave is the game that started Sierra On-Line. It started my career"[^ref-15]. The original Colossal Cave Adventure had directly inspired her to create Mystery House in 1980, which became the first graphical adventure game and launched what would become Sierra On-Line[^ref-7]. Ken Williams noted: "As we dug deeper and deeper into the game we discovered layers of complexity that explain why the game became such an industry phenomena. It has action elements, humor, a scoring system, adventure elements, interesting characters, a huge world to explore and more"[^ref-3].

The project was initially referred to as "The Secret" before its official announcement[^ref-3]. Roberta Williams reflected on her return to game development: "I hadn't really wanted to get back in the industry, but I just suddenly felt this urge, almost kind of like when I sat down and did Mystery House"[^ref-15]. The Williams approached the project with low stakes; Ken told Ars Technica, "For us, it's not really about the money. If the game sinks or swims, it ain't gonna change our life at all"[^ref-19].

### Production

Cygnus Entertainment was named after the Williamses' boat, *Cygnus*[^ref-16][^ref-18]. By March 2022 the team had grown to 15 people, Ken wrote on the studio blog ("We now have 15 people working on the game!")[^ref-22]; Engadget gave 16 later that month, and the team worked remotely throughout the pandemic[^ref-15][^ref-19]. The development team included professionals with experience in 3D graphics and programming, with Roberta serving as creative director[^ref-15] and Ken as lead engineer[^ref-23]. Emily Compton, a team member, noted that "this game is the genesis of their fame and also a nostalgic property to revisit late in their careers, functioning like bookends"[^ref-16].

The team had access to the original game's source code and comments, which Roberta combed through to make design documents[^ref-16]. Development required balancing art against performance for the VR version on the Meta Quest 2, where art had to start from basic geometry with polygon counts raised slowly[^ref-16]; Ken Williams wrote that the Quest 2 required a minimum of 72 frames per second and that "Adding VR to the project doubled the cost"[^ref-18]. The team's artists used Substance 3D tools for texturing, along with Blender, Maya, and ZBrush for 3D modeling[^ref-16]. PBR (Physically Based Rendering) textures were made in Substance Painter for integration into Unity[^ref-16].

Marcus Maximus Mera, the first artist to work on the game, came from a background in high-end jewelry and apparel design[^ref-1][^ref-16][^ref-19]. Amber Johnson, the team's level designer, revealed her personal connection to the project: "Playing Kings Quest games as a kid is one of the reasons I decided to go into game development"[^ref-16]. Mera recalled telling Ken: "If Sierra was here today this game would be in virtual reality"[^ref-16].

**Development Credits:**[^ref-6][^ref-15]
- **Executive Producer:** Ken Williams
- **Creative Director:** Roberta Williams
- **Lead Engineer:** Ken Williams[^ref-23]
- **Original Design:** Will Crowther, Don Woods
- **Team Size:** 16 people (March 2022)

Crowther and Woods never copyrighted their original game. When the Williamses called Woods to ask permission anyway, Engadget reported, his one request was that they never try to copyright it and keep the franchise open and free for anyone to play with; they agreed[^ref-15].

### Technical Achievements

The game was built in Unity[^ref-3], and Ken Williams counted approximately 20 different versions across PC, console and VR platforms, noting that Unity's multiplatform support "only gets you most of the way to the finish line"[^ref-18]. According to Ken Williams, the game was designed as "a fully immersive 3D and VR environment for a realistic cave exploration experience"[^ref-3].

Multiple versions were developed to accommodate different hardware capabilities. The VR version targeting Meta Quest 2 required optimization balancing performance against art fidelity[^ref-16]. In September 2022 the team was working on a higher-resolution PC version with better textures and more decorative objects[^ref-16]. Steam lists the game as Steam Deck Verified[^ref-13].

### Cut Content

No cut content has been documented. Ken Williams wrote that the team released the game "with the puzzles exactly as they were in the original text-based game, warts and all", then patched in extra hints after reviews called it faithful "to a fault"[^ref-18].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

In his March 2023 "Making of" article, Ken Williams called the launch price of $39.99 "the biggest single mistake we made", cutting it to $24.99 after customer complaints and negative reviews triggered by the price; he wrote that major releases for PS4, PSVR2, Xbox One, iOS, Android "and more" were still ahead[^ref-18]. On the Interactive Fiction Community Forum, Andrew Plotkin summarized the article as discussing "some of the reasons why he thinks it didn't sell well"[^ref-17]. The project represents the first game from Ken and Roberta Williams in some 25 years, making it a significant event in adventure gaming history regardless of commercial performance[^ref-2][^ref-19]. The Williams were candid about their expectations, telling Ars Technica that the project was "not really about the money"[^ref-19].

### Awards

- **Day of the Devs:** Official Selection, "Selected as one of the top 10 new indie releases!" (as listed on the Steam store page)[^ref-13]
- **World Video Game Hall of Fame:** The original 1976 Colossal Cave Adventure was inducted in 2019[^ref-4][^ref-6]

### Collections

A Boxed Collector's Edition was released on September 20, 2023[^ref-6].

### Fan Projects

The 3D remake itself serves as a tribute to the original, which has been ported and adapted many times since 1976. Among them, Eric S. Raymond created Open Adventure in 2017, a port of Don Woods's 1995 version for modern computers[^ref-4].

### Related Publications

- **Original Source Code:** William Crowther's original 1976 version comprised 700 lines of code plus 700 lines of data; Don Woods's 1977 expansion grew to 3,000 lines of code and 1,800 lines of data[^ref-4]
- **Ken Williams' Memoir:** Ken wrote a book about the Sierra days during the pandemic, which contributed to his return to game development[^ref-10]
- **"The Making of Colossal Cave":** Ken Williams' March 2023 post-mortem of the project on the official site[^ref-18]

### Critical Perspective

The original 1976 text adventure is considered one of the most influential games ever created, establishing conventions that became standard in the adventure game genre and directly inspiring the creation of Sierra On-Line[^ref-7]. According to Graham Nelson, creator of the Inform interactive fiction language, "for the five years to 1982 almost every game created was another 'Advent'"[^ref-4].

Critics noted that the game's refusal to modernize its mechanics, keeping puzzles that can trap players without explanation, would put off many contemporary players[^ref-11].

This fidelity to the source material was intentional. In 2022, Ars Technica reported that Roberta and Ken "made clear that the original script and puzzles are as untouched as possible", with mild tweaks to turn text puzzles into readable 3D scenes[^ref-19]. A player testimonial quoted on the official site put it this way: "This is a Classic Car of gaming. It's for the collector. It's for the old-schooler…"[^ref-5].
