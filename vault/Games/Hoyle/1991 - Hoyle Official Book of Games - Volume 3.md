# Hoyle Official Book of Games Volume 3

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Hoyle Official Book of Games Volume 3, subtitled "Great Board Games," is the third installment in Sierra On-Line's Hoyle series, released for DOS in 1991 and ported to the Amiga in 1992[^ref-28][^ref-31][^ref-33]. Where the first two volumes had featured card games, Volume 3 moved the series to board games. Sierra's own InterAction magazine (Spring 1993) called it "the first Hoyle game to be rendered in VGA," with "hand-painted 256-color art"[^ref-52], and Wikipedia likewise notes it was built with Sierra's newer VGA engine[^ref-4]. Its six games are Checkers, Backgammon, Dominoes, Yacht, Snakes and Ladders and Pachisi[^ref-28][^ref-29].

The series takes its name from Edmond Hoyle, the 18th-century card-game authority[^ref-4]. As in the first volume, players face computer opponents drawn from Sierra's own adventure games: eighteen characters, split into "Good Guys" and "Bad Guys"[^ref-28][^ref-29]. Sierra's InterAction magazine promoted a multiplayer option as "new for this game," letting family and friends play against each other as well as against the computer[^ref-30].

## Story Summary

Hoyle Official Book of Games Volume 3 is a board game collection and has no story. Its personality comes from its opponents. The instruction booklet describes eighteen Sierra characters on two Choose Opponents screens, with a button that toggles between "Good Guys" and "Bad Guys"[^ref-28]. According to Wikipedia, the good guys are Mother Goose, King Graham, Rosella, Larry Laffer, Passionate Patti, Jones, Sonny Bonds, Roger Wilco and Laura Bow. The bad guys are Ad Avis, Baba Yaga, Lillian, Arnoid, Mordack, Mr. Big, Lolotte, Vohaul and the Sheriff of Nottingham[^ref-4].

Compute! reported that the heroes are "arranged in order of their difficulty as opponents, from Mother Goose, whose digitized voice prompts you when you make a mistake, to Sonny Bonds," with the villains ordered by playing strength in the same way[^ref-29]. Wikipedia says that, unlike the first volume, the characters do not hold conversations with each other. A few have themed remarks instead, such as Baba Yaga calling her opponents "dearie"[^ref-4]. Compute! adds that players can talk with the computer opponents to learn about each game's history[^ref-29].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Play is point-and-click. Checkers pieces are dragged to the target square, Backgammon dice are rolled by clicking the dice cup, and Pachisi pawns are dragged around the board[^ref-28]. The icon bar holds CONTROL (Games, Quit, Replay, About, Help), OPTIONS, SCORE, RULES and HELP, and each game's rules can be read on screen[^ref-28]. Compute! listed a mouse as "recommended"[^ref-29], and MobyGames lists the mouse as the input device and an analog joystick as a supported controller type, read via the MobyGames API[^ref-31]. PC Joker found mouse control flawless but called joystick control "die reinste Qual" ("sheer torture")[^ref-34].

### Structure and Progression

The Select Game Screen offers six games[^ref-28]:

- **Checkers:** traditional red-and-black pieces or an animated "Frog Checkers" set, with an optional must-jump rule[^ref-28]
- **Backgammon:** optional doubling cube, an Acey Ducey rules option (against human opponents only) and two board designs[^ref-28][^ref-29]
- **Dominoes:** two players, with options covering must-play, highest doublet, boneyard rules, hand size and target score. Compute! counted "six different sets of rules"[^ref-28][^ref-29]
- **Yacht:** a dice game for building poker hands, with a color-cycling background[^ref-28][^ref-29]
- **Snakes and Ladders:** up to four players, shown as animated children who climb the ladders and slide down the snakes[^ref-28][^ref-29]
- **Pachisi:** up to four players, with cowrie shells as an alternative to dice and a choice of two boards[^ref-28][^ref-30]

On the Player Type Screen (used for every game except Snakes and Ladders), each seat is set to human or computer. That lets the player face one, two or three opponents, depending on the game[^ref-28]. Snakes and Ladders has its own setup screens instead ("How Many Real Kids?" and "How Many Computer Kids?") for up to four players[^ref-28]. There is no campaign or progression system.

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Each opponent has a skill level of Beginner, Average or Expert, shown on the Choose Opponents screen[^ref-28]. Compute! described the Checkers frogs: an illegal move sends the frog leaping, even "to the top of your opponent's head," before it hops back while the opponent points out the mistake. Captured frogs "dive deep into a pond"[^ref-29]. For young players, Snakes and Ladders has a "Star On" option that marks the square where a move should end[^ref-28].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Alfred C. Giovetti reviewed the game for *Compute!* in August 1992 and was largely positive. He called Checkers "by far the cutesiest game in the box" and praised the Snakes and Ladders animation as "fluid, well done, and fascinating to watch." He concluded that "Hoyle's 3 is a great game for adults and children alike" that "will probably be a permanent addition to my hard drive"[^ref-29]. He also noted reports of voice bugs, "such as conflicts with the mouse and partial rather than complete phrases and words"[^ref-29].

*PC Games* (August/September 1992) gave Eric Grevstad's review no numeric score and a mixed verdict. He wrote that anyone wanting "fast-paced action" might react with "nice animation and graphics. Hmmm, there's not much to them, is there?" He said the game "really requires 256-color VGA and a mouse," found the Sierra characters' "repartee is limited and repetitive," and thought that "all but the most placid children may find Snakes and Ladders numbingly dull." His conclusion was that "board gamers who are looking for wholesome family entertainment will like Hoyle 3"[^ref-45]. In *Game Player's PC Entertainment* (Vol. 5 No. 2, 1992), Lance Elko wrote that "the graphics shine, the sound and music are good, the interface and menu system are elegant," but also that "Volume 3 is less engaging than either of the first two volumes," because in four of the games "strategy takes a back seat to the luck of the roll." He added that "286 owners can expect a long wait" as the computer opponent ponders a move or the game screens are redrawn[^ref-46].

*Computer Game Review* (issue 8, March 1992) ran Hoyle's 3 in its "Best of the Rest" capsules. The notice said "Graphics and sound effects are well done, as in the rolling dice or the slap of bones on the domino board," and the three staff reviewers each added a comment. Dave wrote "Great fun, especially with a lot of friends. Backgammon and Dominoes alone are worth the price"; Mike, "Addicting little games, great for the younger audience or laptop owners looking to kill some time"; and Steve, "Not something I'd play incessantly, Hoyle 3 is the kind of game to boot up every now and again." The page prints 81, 81 and 80 over the three comments; the magazine states that its scores use a 1-100 scale[^ref-48]. *Computer Gaming World* #92 (March 1992) listed the game in its new-product notices, saying it "adds multi-human player options" and eighteen computer opponents, including "a Terminator clone named 'Arnoid'"[^ref-49].

The Amiga port was reviewed in *Amazing Computing* (September 1992) by Jeff James, who liked the animated-frog Checkers but found that "in the area of graphics, Hoyle3 is a little disappointing": "Converted from the 256-color MS-DOS version, Hoyle3 suffers somewhat from grainy game screens and a muddy color palette." He reported that the game needs 1MB of RAM and Kickstart 1.2 or later, ships on two unprotected disks, and ran at "slow and slower" speeds even on an A3000; a Sierra spokesman told him a patch disk fixes most of the speed problems[^ref-47]. *Computer Gaming World* #96 (July 1992) listed the Amiga version among its "Conversions Received"[^ref-50].

The German magazine *PC Joker* (February 1992) scored the DOS version 55%. It praised the graphics and the animated opponent portraits but found the computer's playing strength "leider nicht weltbewegend" ("unfortunately not earth-shattering")[^ref-34]. *Alle PC Spiele* (1993) similarly summed up the games as having good graphics but weak playing strength[^ref-34].

Sierra's own InterAction magazine (Summer 1992) reported that Sierra "wound up with five nominations" at the Software Publishers Association awards for several titles, including *The Official Hoyle Book of Games Vol. 3*. The magazine does not say which category, so this is a nomination only[^ref-44].

### Modern Assessment

Home of the Underdogs called the collection "a mixed bag." It judged that Snakes and Ladders and Checkers "are downright boring, while backgammon and pachisi are a lot of fun," and found the character chatter less frequent and less funny than in the first volume[^ref-38]. Its review also says there are "fewer opponents to choose from," but that is HotU's claim alone: the instruction booklet, Sierra's InterAction (Spring 1993) and Compute! all give 18 opponents, and Compute! notes that Volume 3 has, "as in the first Hoyle's, nine heroes and nine villains"[^ref-28][^ref-52][^ref-29].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames (critics):** 55% (1 rating)[^ref-34]
- **MobyGames (players):** 4.0/5 (11 ratings)[^ref-34]

## Development

### Origins

Compute! described Volume 3 as Sierra returning "once again to the tried-and-true games format used in its two earlier versions," this time with board games rather than cards[^ref-29]. Volume 3 brought back the Sierra-character opponents that Volume 2 (a solitaire collection) had dropped[^ref-4][^ref-29].

### Production

Sources disagree on who designed the game. MobyGames credits Marti McKenna and Bridget McKenna as game designers, Bridget McKenna for writing, and [[Warren Schwader]] as lead programmer[^ref-32]. The Sierra Wiki also names both McKennas as designers[^ref-39], and Home of the Underdogs names Bridget McKenna[^ref-38]. Wikipedia, however, says Schwader "continued as the Lead Programmer and Game Designer for the next two games in the series" after Volume 1[^ref-4]. *Computer Game Review* (March 1992) printed "Designed by: [[Bill Davis]], [[Stuart Moulder]]" in its capsule[^ref-48]. Those two names match the booklet's Creative Director and Director/Producer rather than its game designers. The instruction booklet is the game's own credit and is the most direct evidence for this volume: its inside-front-cover credits list "Game Designers: Marti McKenna, Bridget McKenna" and "Lead Programmer: Warren Schwader", and a later credits page names Bridget McKenna as manual writer[^ref-28].

### Technical Achievements

Compute! reported that the backgrounds, opponents and playing pieces are animated, "with digitized voices and a varied musical score accompanying play"[^ref-29]. The booklet notes that each game has its own musical theme, reflecting its history[^ref-28].

### Technical Specifications

**Disk Version:**
- **Media:** 3.5" and 5.25" floppy disks[^ref-29][^ref-31]
- **Graphics:** 256-color VGA and 16-color EGA/Tandy[^ref-29][^ref-28][^ref-31]

### Technical Issues

Compute! noted reported voice bugs, including mouse conflicts and clipped phrases[^ref-29]. The Sierra Wiki lists no official patch, only a fan patch by NewRisingSun that "fixes some animations"[^ref-39]. ScummVM supports the game and rates it "Excellent"[^ref-36].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- Sierra's InterAction (Spring 1992) ran a "Strategy and Tactics" column on the game in which Sierra characters such as King Graham and Rosella give Checkers advice. Its writer described being beaten at Checkers by "that holographic arch-villain Vohaul"[^ref-43]
- Compute! enjoyed the cross-franchise matchups: "You can now pit your Pachisi prowess against Passionate Patty" and "overhaul Vohaul's Yacht"[^ref-29]
- The Sheriff of Nottingham may refer to himself as the "Great Nottingham"[^ref-4]
- In Snakes and Ladders, the Sierra characters are replaced by "game child" pieces[^ref-28]
- The Pachisi option to roll cowrie shells recreates the game's ancient form[^ref-29]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Sierra's own InterAction magazine ranked the game in its published top-ten lists of Sierra titles: number 8 in the Spring 1992 issue ("Sierra's Top Ten," p. 34) and number 10 in the Fall 1992 issue ("Top Ten Games," p. 82)[^ref-43][^ref-51]. These are Sierra's own lists; they give rankings, not unit sales, and the Spring list does not state the period it covers. Sierra bundled it with *Conquests of the Longbow* and *Gobliiins* in its *Family Fun Pack* compilation, which MobyGames says also included the Windows 3.x screen saver *Screen Antics: Johnny Castaway*[^ref-40]. Sierra's own InterAction magazine (Summer 1992) listed Hoyle 3 among five CD-ROM titles Sierra had scheduled for the following year; that was a plan, and this page has not verified a CD release of Volume 3[^ref-44]. MobyGames lists only 3.5" and 5.25" floppy media for the DOS version. Its DOS patch listing for the game includes a Sierra sound-driver file (486SBDRV.EXE) described as "to be found on late re-releases on CD"; the note does not say which titles' CD editions it means[^ref-31]. Compute! noted that players who wanted to face human opponents by modem could play Hoyle's 3 through The Sierra Network, Sierra's online service[^ref-29].

### Collections

- **Family Fun Pack** (1992 Windows, 1993 DOS): with *Conquests of the Longbow* and *Gobliiins*[^ref-40]
- **Hoyle Classic Board Games** (SierraOriginals CD): ScummVM notes that the Volume 3 floppy data was also found on a SierraOriginals CD under this title (1996)[^ref-35]. This is a re-release of Volume 3, not the separate Windows game [[1997 - Hoyle Classic Board Games]]
- **Crazy Nick's Software Picks: King Graham's Board Game Challenge** (1992): a budget pack containing Backgammon and Checkers, which the Sierra Chest says were originally included in Volume 3[^ref-53]. See [[1992 - Crazy Nick's Software Picks - King Graham's Board Game Challenge]]

### Fan Projects

The game runs in ScummVM[^ref-36], and the Sierra Help Pages offer a fan-made DOSBox installer, which requires the original disks, and an animation-fix patch[^ref-39]. The Internet Archive preserves a self-running (non-interactive) demo and scans of the boxed paper materials[^ref-37][^ref-28].

### Related Publications

- **Instruction booklet:** Covers rules and options for all six games, plus the full opponent roster[^ref-28]
- **Sierra Game Manual:** A generic Sierra manual, included in the box, covering installation[^ref-28]

### Critical Perspective

Volume 3 has had little critical attention, but contemporary and later reviewers agree on its strengths. Compute! and Home of the Underdogs both singled out the animated characters and boards, while PC Joker found the computer opponents weak players and Home of the Underdogs found the chatter less frequent and less funny than in the first volume[^ref-29][^ref-34][^ref-38]. Game Player's PC Entertainment and PC Games were cooler on depth, calling it "less engaging" than the earlier volumes and the characters' repartee "limited and repetitive"[^ref-46][^ref-45]. A Sierra Gamers forum member recalled "the first three in the series which I played a lot. Definitely Sierra classics"[^ref-26]. Fan-maintained Sierra release lists place "Hoyle 3" among Sierra's 1991 games[^ref-3], and GameFAQs lists it in the Hoyle franchise for PC and Amiga[^ref-2].
