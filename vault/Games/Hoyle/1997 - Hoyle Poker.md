# Hoyle Poker

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 23, 2026</small>

## Overview

**Hoyle Poker**, released in 1997 for Windows 95 and Windows 3.1, was Sierra's first stand-alone poker game; poker had already been part of 1996's *Hoyle Casino*, where it was played strictly by casino rules with fixed bets.[^ref-25][^ref-22] Rather than a casino, it recreates a neighborhood Friday-night home game in which the dealer changes with each hand and picks the variant to be played.[^ref-5] It was published under the Hoyle name as part of Sierra's series of card games,[^ref-1][^ref-5] and was later repackaged as **Hoyle Classic Poker**.[^ref-1][^ref-21] Sierra sold it for $24.95 under order number 83741.[^ref-24][^ref-27]

GameSpot's Walter Morbeck wrote that Sierra "has done an admirable job of creating a sharp and faithful rendition of poker."[^ref-4] The game pairs real poker rules with Sierra's humour: a table of animated opponents, each with their own personality, playing style and skill level, who toss out a steady stream of one-liners.[^ref-1][^ref-4]

## Story Summary

Hoyle Poker has no narrative. The player sits down at a friendly home game with a bankroll of $1,000 in cash, plus another $9,000 in a bank account that can be reached through an ATM, which, like the other characters, has a personality of its own.[^ref-1][^ref-4] The "story" is the banter of the opponents around the table.[^ref-4][^ref-5]

## Gameplay

### Variants

Sources differ on the count of poker variants. Sierra's own InterAction magazine and *Computer Games* both said "over 20", CGW counted "two dozen", MobyGames lists 20, the GamesDomain review says 24, and GameSpot and *PC Gamer* both said "more than a dozen".[^ref-24][^ref-28][^ref-22][^ref-1][^ref-5][^ref-4][^ref-23] MobyGames names, among others:[^ref-1]

- Texas Hold 'Em
- Omaha and Omaha Hi/Lo
- High Chicago
- Cincinnati
- Baseball
- 5 Card Stud, 7 Card Stud and 7 Card Stud Hi/Lo
- Jacks or Better
- Criss Cross
- Dealer's Choice
- 5 Card Draw and 5 Card Draw Lo-ball

Sierra's preview also named the home-game variants Pass the Trash, Murder and The Bitch,[^ref-24] and *PC Gamer* singled out Baseball ("all 3s and 9s are wild"), Murder and The Bitch as the "fun and wacky games" that "add spice to the mix."[^ref-23]

The rules can be switched from one variant to another between hands, and the Help menu gives a short set of rules for each variant at any time during a game.[^ref-4] Players can also choose table backgrounds (GameSpot's favourite was a kitchen table with a pizza box and Chinese food) and card backs, and can play a tournament or simply play until the money runs out.[^ref-4][^ref-5] *PC Gamer* called the Dealer's Choice option, in which the computer opponents pick games at random, "one of the best features".[^ref-23] A tutorial mode let players practise and learn their opponents' betting strategies.[^ref-24]

### Opponents and the Attitude Meter

In single-player mode the player can choose up to seven opponents, each with their own personality, playing style and skill level.[^ref-1] AllGame's reviewer described a cowboy, a bartender and "a James Dean guy" among the cast, picked at random for each table.[^ref-29] CGW's tips page listed the computer players as Anna, Bart, Elayne, Kathryn, Mrs. O'Shea, Rick, Sterling and T-Bone the dog.[^ref-22] GameSpot named several of them: James, Elayne (with her New Jersey accent), the James Bond-like Sterling, the motherly Mrs. O'Shea, Anna, and a bulldog named T-Bone.[^ref-4] Sierra's own preview instead introduced Elayne as a New Yorker and Manhattan advertising executive.[^ref-24] The GamesDomain reviewer described the cast as "spirited stereotypes all with their own corny jokes, egos and style of play", including a college professor and a snobby rich guy.[^ref-5]

Studying the characters can reveal each player's *tell*.[^ref-1] GamesDomain found that some physical tells exist but that most are "hidden within the phrases spoken by the other players."[^ref-5] The **Attitude Meter** sets the pace: higher settings bring more animation and comments from the AI players, lower ones keep them quiet for a faster game.[^ref-1] Sierra marketed the game as "Poker with an attitude!"[^ref-28] *PC Gamer* quoted quips such as "I've got more guts than a meat packing plant" and "Kiss my call", and noted the chatter could be turned off altogether.[^ref-23]

### Bluffing

CGW's tips page offered per-opponent advice, such as "Elayne: When she winks at you, she's nervous; bluff her."[^ref-22] The GamesDomain review advised that with a good hand "you need to resist the temptation to raise right away", since raising early drives most opponents to fold; the pot grows much bigger if you lure some of them along.[^ref-5]

### Multiplayer

MobyGames lists Internet and same-screen multiplayer, with 1–8 players offline and 2–8 online.[^ref-1] Online play ran through the Sierra Internet Gaming System (SIGS), which in 1997 also hosted *Hoyle Blackjack*, *Hoyle Casino*, *Power Chess* and *Shivers 2*, among others.[^ref-26] *PC Gamer* described SIGS as "a free web site where you can play real people, though 5 Card Draw is the only game currently provided."[^ref-23] Sierra also promoted Internet tournaments.[^ref-24] An on-line play mode let players test their skills against real players from around the world;[^ref-1] the GamesDomain reviewer noted it was for play with friends out of town, adding, "Please, no exchange of money, this is a friendly game of poker."[^ref-5]

## Reception

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| GameSpot | 8/10 | "Sharp and faithful rendition of poker"; 8 in all five subscores[^ref-4] |
| All Game Guide | 80/100 (MobyRank) | "This is a haven for poker lovers"[^ref-30][^ref-29] |
| Computer Gaming World | 60/100 (MobyRank) | Sep 1997; "Annoying AI; unreliable Internet competition"[^ref-30][^ref-22] |
| Computer Games Magazine | 60/100 (MobyRank) | "Poker with an attitude!"[^ref-30][^ref-28] |
| PC Gamer | not legible in scan | "Fun poker simulation"; attitude "gets tiresome real fast"[^ref-23] |
| MobyGames critic average | 72% | Based on 5 critic ratings[^ref-1] |

Morbeck opened his GameSpot review by observing that "what makes poker enjoyable are the strictly human elements", which seemed to make a computer version farfetched; he concluded that the game could serve "either as a temporary surrogate or as a replacement for the real thing."[^ref-4] He praised the cast's diversity and fast pacing, but found the bundled rulebook less useful for the computer game than one written for it would have been.[^ref-4] The review was first posted on 4 June 1997;[^ref-4] MobyGames dates it June 1998.[^ref-30]

Charles Ardai's CGW review, "Ante Matter", opened: "After Hoyle Casino and Hoyle Blackjack, it was inevitable that Sierra would further milk the venerable Hoyle name with Hoyle Poker." He praised the game's "trademark slickness" but found the computer players "extremely annoying" for their "Central Casting appearance" and "puerile comments", and complained that they could not easily be replaced with human opponents "given Sierra's buggy link." He recommended it to "Poker enthusiasts who found Hoyle Casino tame."[^ref-22]

*PC Gamer*'s Joseph Novicki judged that Sierra "seems to really have its act together with this edition" and that the many variants and adjustable rules made it "much better than your average poker sim."[^ref-23] AllGame's Mark Kanarick called it "one of the most fun computer games I have played in a long time"; his one complaint was the documentation, with rules found only through the help index.[^ref-29] Doug Radcliffe in *Computer Games* found the opponents' one-liners "somewhat annoying" at high attitude settings.[^ref-28]

The GamesDomain review, reproduced on MyAbandonware, called it "a great game for all skill levels" and good for learning the basics of poker.[^ref-5] Comparing it with *Golden Nugget*, whose opponents were "basically like playing against robots", the reviewer judged Hoyle Poker better for "its fully developed characters and their bluffing and attitudes," and concluded that it was "a decent enough game for the price."[^ref-5]

### Running It Today

A 2024 user comment on MyAbandonware reports that, from a mounted ISO, the Windows version randomly stalls while a character speaks until the player clicks to continue; the commenter knew of no fix.[^ref-5]

## Development

### Origins

Producer Steve Van Horn, who from early 1995 had led the team reworking Sierra's *Hoyle Classic Games*, explained in late 1996 why Sierra sold Poker and Blackjack as separate games alongside *Hoyle Casino*: "because they are not the same games." The stand-alone titles offered tutorials and wild-card variants, whereas in *Hoyle Casino* "Poker is played strictly according to casino rules with fixed bets." He added that Sierra had "Beta tested Poker and Blackjack on the Net. One regular player was from Scotland."[^ref-25]

In Sierra's spring 1997 preview, Van Horn said: "Hoyle Poker has plenty of attitude, multiple game variations and a depth of game play unmatched by any other card games." The article said Sierra had researched what players wanted and heard that they wanted "Friday-night games" rather than only casino-style ones.[^ref-24] In the same issue, Ken Williams wrote that he played *Hoyle Blackjack* over the Internet and would "be standing in line to buy one of the first copies of our Hoyle Poker when it comes out."[^ref-24]

GamesDomain framed the game as part of Sierra's case for card games: not every computer gamer has the reflexes for action games or wants "the emotional drain of a complex adventure game", and some players have older computers.[^ref-5] Hoyle went on to be counted among Sierra's popular software titles,[^ref-3] and in 1998 Sierra grouped the Hoyle license under its new Sierra Attractions brand for family and board-style games. GameSpot's archive dates that report to April 2000, but its release slate (*Half-Life* in summer, *King's Quest: Mask of Eternity* in fall, *Babylon 5* in early 1999) places it in 1998.[^ref-15]

### Credits

MobyGames credits 39 people.[^ref-1] Jim Love designed the game, and Stephen H. Van Horn produced it. Earl Malmrose was lead engineer, with Frank T. Hollander as senior software engineer. Character animation was by Rabih AbouJaoudé, Heather Ivy and Gayle Rowbotham, and Julian Love did the intro animation. Evan Schiller composed the music, with additional music and sound by Jim Love. The voice actors were Pat Cashman, Amy De Bourget, Jeff Hoyt, Linda Mathews and Kate Myre.[^ref-1]

### Package and Requirements

The box included Stewart Wolpin's 358-page book *The Rules of Neighborhood Poker According to Hoyle*, which covers all of the game's variants plus others not in the game.[^ref-4][^ref-23] The game runs on Windows 95 or Windows 3.1 with very low requirements: a 486 with 4 MB of RAM, a 2× CD-ROM drive, and 640×480 graphics.[^ref-1][^ref-5] *PC Gamer* gave the requirement as a 486/33 and recommended a Pentium 90 with 8 MB of RAM.[^ref-23] Sierra's spring 1997 InterAction feature listed it as "Available in March".[^ref-24] The Sierra Chest records a first release date of 1 March 1997, while MobyGames lists Windows and Windows 16-bit releases in the United States and Canada on 30 April 1997.[^ref-21][^ref-31] The Sierra Chest also lists a 1998 re-release and a 1999 *Classic Poker* re-release (UPC 0-20626-83741-5).[^ref-21] MobyGames instead describes *Hoyle Classic Poker* as a "1998 repackaging"; the sources do not settle which year the Classic edition first appeared.[^ref-1] MobyGames also lists it as part of the 1997 *On-Line Games Collection Series*.[^ref-1]

## Legacy

MobyGames notes that elements of the gameplay of the 2005 *[[2005 - Hoyle Poker Series|Hoyle Poker Series]]* can be traced back to this game.[^ref-20]
