# Hoyle Casino Empire

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Hoyle Casino Empire, released in Europe simply as Casino Empire, is a business simulation game for Windows in which the player runs a casino and tries to turn a profit, attract customers and earn good ratings[^ref-17]. It was developed by an in-house team at [[Sierra On-Line|Sierra Entertainment]] and published by the company, and Wikipedia places it in Sierra's Hoyle Casino series[^ref-17]. Sierra announced the game on February 19, 2002, presenting it as an expansion of its "best-selling Hoyle games" line[^ref-28], and it shipped in North America on September 23, 2002 and in Europe on October 25, 2002[^ref-17].

Unlike the card-and-table Hoyle Casino editions, Casino Empire is a building and management game: GameSpot described it as "the first management strategy game from Sierra Attractions, the developer of the popular Hoyle card, board, and casino games," and compared it to RollerCoaster Tycoon[^ref-23]. GameSpy likewise called it "the first building sim-type game with the Hoyle name"[^ref-22].

Critics were divided. Wikipedia sums up the reception as "a number of mixed reviews"[^ref-17]; MobyGames averages 14 critic scores at 65%, ranging from GameZone's 84% down to 40%[^ref-21].

## Story Summary

The campaign has a light comic frame. The player's cousin Buddy owes money to mob boss Al Guccini, and his only asset is a run-down casino; the player is brought in to make it profitable, with a first target of $25,000 profit and Level II status within two years[^ref-24]. GameSpy describes this opening as a training mode in which "your idiot cousin can't seem to run a simple casino"[^ref-22].

From Buddy's small casino the player moves on to progressively larger properties, each run by a new boss who hires the player at the start of the mission[^ref-23]. The casinos parody real Las Vegas resorts: IGN identified knock-offs of the Luxor, Excalibur, Hard Rock and Bellagio[^ref-25], and Gaming Nexus noted versions of Excalibur, Luxor and the Stratosphere[^ref-27]. The second casino, The Egyptian, asks the player to earn $200,000, reach Level III and achieve a 250 casino rating[^ref-24]. Along the way the player meets celebrity parodies; GameSpot heard spoofs of Bill Cosby, Siegfried & Roy and Regis Philbin[^ref-23], and Gaming Nexus mentioned takes on Michael Jackson and Arnold Schwarzenegger[^ref-27].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The casino is shown from a fixed isometric viewpoint[^ref-18][^ref-27]. Objects are placed by drag and drop and can be rotated before placement, while wall-mounted facilities such as the cashier's booth and security office need a free section of wall; an object's icon turns red when it cannot go where the player wants it[^ref-24]. An event ticker at the top of the screen flags important issues, and icons let the player zoom in and out[^ref-23].

Reviewers consistently criticised what the interface left out. The map cannot be rotated[^ref-25][^ref-27], there is no minimap for jumping around large casinos[^ref-22][^ref-23], and the camera cannot be locked onto a patron, an employee or a ticker event, so troublemakers have to be found by scrolling[^ref-23][^ref-27]. IGN also noted the absence of a game-speed slider[^ref-25].

### Structure and Progression

The game has two modes. Empire Mode is the campaign, in which the player manages eight casinos in turn, from a small one up to the largest; each has objectives to meet within a time limit, and failing them means restarting from the last casino reached[^ref-17]. Sandbox Mode removes the time limit and objectives; it unlocks after the first Empire casino, and further campaign progress unlocks more casinos for it[^ref-17].

Each casino has a Level Status that controls which games and services can be built. Once the casino has enough customers and a high enough rating, the player can pay to raise it, up to the fourth level[^ref-17]. Typical goals combine a cash target, a casino rating and a build level[^ref-23][^ref-27].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The player fills the floor with games such as blackjack, poker, craps, roulette, baccarat, Pai Gow poker, slot machines and video poker, and adds facilities including cashier's booths, restrooms, bars, lounges, bingo, keno and gift shops; hotels can offer in-room keno, video games and Internet access[^ref-18]. Decorations such as statues, fountains and plants draw customers toward nearby games[^ref-18][^ref-22]. MobyGames' description lists four kinds of patron: Tourists (60% of the clientele), Gamblers, High Rollers and celebrity VIP High Rollers[^ref-18].

Customers need cash desks or ATMs, restrooms, food and drink, and hotel rooms; neglecting these makes them leave and give bad reviews[^ref-17]. Staff include machine attendants, custodians, waitresses and security guards[^ref-17], and Gaming Nexus noted that showgirls can be hired and that each worker can be assigned a route[^ref-27]. Rival casinos send spies and saboteurs, so security has to be managed[^ref-23][^ref-22].

The player can raise the house edge as far as a cheating mode and accept crooked deals such as money laundering, at the risk of financial and rating penalties if the city authorities find out[^ref-17]. The game also includes poker tournament rooms; Gaming Nexus described tournaments of Texas Hold'em or Five Card Draw played over 16 rounds in a Hoyle-style card table view[^ref-27].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Date |
|-------------|-------|------|
| GameZone | 8.4/10[^ref-24] | September 30, 2002 |
| GameSpot | 5.9/10[^ref-23] | October 4, 2002 |
| IGN | 6.2/10[^ref-26] | October 7, 2002 |
| GameSpy | 3.5/5[^ref-17] | October 30, 2002 |
| Gaming Nexus | 6.4[^ref-27] | November 8, 2002 |
| Computer Gaming World | 60%[^ref-21] | — |

GameZone's Michael Lafferty was the most positive, calling it "a smooth, glitzy program" and praising its sound in particular (8.7): "this is one of the few 'tycoon' class games that seems to have paid as much attention to the sound quality as to the graphical quality"[^ref-24].

GameSpot's Andrew Park gave it 5.9, writing that it "lacks a lot of features that you'd expect from any good management game, though it still manages to be enjoyable at times," and that the developer's inexperience with the genre was "pretty obvious"[^ref-23]. He also found it hard to justify the $40 retail price[^ref-23].

GameSpy's Kevin Rice listed "nice graphics, easy learning curve, sandbox mode, decent music and voice work" as pros and "perhaps too easy" among the cons, reporting that he finished all the scenarios "in a weekend of relatively casual play"[^ref-22].

IGN's Ivan Sulic was the harshest of the major sites, calling it "a tycoon game with zero lasting appeal and even less challenge" and reporting that he left the game running unattended for twenty minutes on several casinos and still completed the missions[^ref-25]. Gaming Nexus's John Yan called it "a valiant attempt at developing a fun and addicting tycoon type game" that "ultimately fails in keeping you interested after a few scenarios"[^ref-27].

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames averages 14 critic reviews at 65%. The highest are GameZone (84%) and ESC Magazine (80%), and the lowest are GameSpot (59%), PC Zone Benelux (53%) and PCG (40%)[^ref-21]. Its player average is 3.5 out of 5 from nine ratings[^ref-21]. On My Abandonware the game holds a 4.14/5 user rating from 58 votes[^ref-13].

## Development

### Origins

Sierra, then a Vivendi Universal studio, announced Casino Empire on February 19, 2002 for a worldwide release that fall[^ref-28]. Product manager Steve Beinner said: "We've captured the essence of Las Vegas, from the massive scale of the buildings and structures to the sights and sounds that you'd really see in a casino"[^ref-28].

### Production

The game was made by an in-house Sierra team[^ref-17]; review sites credited the developer as "Sierra Attractions"[^ref-22][^ref-23]. MobyGames lists 111 people in the credits[^ref-18]. Stephen H. Van Horn was executive producer, Jason Piel and Scott Bodenbender were lead designers, Mark Seminatore was principal software engineer and project lead, and Jason Piel was also art director[^ref-19]. Ryan Spain wrote the manual and script, Cara Ely was script writer, editor and casting director, and Alex Jacobs was credited as casino consultant[^ref-19]. [[Rabih AbouJaoudé]] is among the additional artists[^ref-19]. The casino modelling and fly-through sequences were done by Mondo Media[^ref-19], and IMDb lists Jason Piel as director[^ref-10].

### Music and Sound

Mike Caviezel composed the score and was sound engineer, with additional compositions by Evan Buehler and Ben Houge[^ref-19]. The credits also list licensed tracks: "Oh Yeah" and "What's Next" by Scotty Morris, "Dirty Boogie" by Brian Setzer, and Wagner's "Ride of the Valkyries" (credited as "Chevauchee Des Walkyries")[^ref-19]. GameSpot noted that the licensed songs, including Brian Setzer Orchestra tracks, are "few and far between" and only become available later in the campaign[^ref-23]. The game also lets players drop their own MP3 files into a folder to replace the in-game music[^ref-25][^ref-27]. Lip synchronisation used LIPSinc[^ref-19].

### Voice Cast

The credits list Cara Ely as casting and talent director, eight featured voice actors, and 45 further voice actors, including several development staff such as Mike Caviezel, Cara Ely and Ryan Spain[^ref-19].

### Technical Achievements

The game is sprite-based, which Gaming Nexus said allowed "a massive number of people to be on screen without taking down your system"[^ref-27]. It uses RAD Game Tools' Bink Video and the Miles Sound System, and shipped on CD-ROM[^ref-18][^ref-20].

**ESRB rating:** Teen, for Mild Language, Simulated Gambling and Suggestive Themes[^ref-22][^ref-26].

## Legacy

Reviewers at the time treated Casino Empire as Sierra's first try at taking the Hoyle name into the tycoon genre. GameSpy put its interface problems down to "a rookie mistake"[^ref-22], and Gaming Nexus hoped Hoyle would "expand on their initial attempt and improve on their execution"[^ref-27]. The game was reissued in 2004 under the Sold Out Software budget label[^ref-20]. IMDb records it as featured in a 2003 episode of Troldspejlet (Episode #29.6)[^ref-10].
