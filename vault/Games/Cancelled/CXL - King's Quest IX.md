# King's Quest IX (Cancelled)

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>


## Overview

"King's Quest IX" is an umbrella term for **multiple cancelled attempts** to continue Sierra's flagship adventure game series following [[1998 - King's Quest - Mask of Eternity|King's Quest VIII: Mask of Eternity]] (1998). Between 1998 and 2013, Roberta Williams floated ideas for a sequel, and Vivendi's Sierra Seattle studio, Silicon Knights and Telltale Games each worked on a new King's Quest, but none reached release.[^ref-1][^ref-20] None of these projects carried an official "IX" title; the name is a fan and press label for "the next King's Quest".[^ref-20]

Separately, a fan team spent a decade on *The Silver Lining*, which began life as an unofficial "King's Quest IX" and was released, without the King's Quest name, as a free episodic fan game.[^ref-19] The series' next official release was [[2015 - King's Quest|The Odd Gentlemen's 2015 reboot]], which its developers explicitly said was **not** a "King's Quest IX."[^ref-1]

As with KQ8, a sequel would not have carried a numeral in its title. Twins of Change writer Cindy Vanous recalled: "9 was not in the title, because Marketing decreed that several other big companies' series were no longer including installment numbers, so we should follow the naming trend."[^ref-2]

## The Official Attempts

### 1. Roberta Williams' Concept (1998–1999)

*King's Quest: Mask of Eternity: Prima's Official Strategy Guide* by Rick Barba referred to King's Quest IX as the next game in the series.[^ref-20] According to the King's Quest Omnipedia, this assumed KQ8 sold well and that [[Roberta Williams]] would remain at its helm.[^ref-1]

**Story Concept:** In a summary design document shared with the Omnipedia, the story involved a villain named **Rasputris** who would conquer Daventry from within as a hypnotic advisor to the King and Queen. Rosella, having failed to expose him, would bring help from her brother Alexander and from Connor, now the Marquis of Daventry.[^ref-1]

**Why It Failed:** These ideas never entered production.[^ref-20] Ken Williams has written that he and Roberta signed non-competition agreements when they sold Sierra, barring them "for some number of years (five, I think)" from working on competing software.[^ref-1] According to the Omnipedia, KQ8 did well enough that Sierra greenlit a follow-up, but Roberta was not brought in to lead it.[^ref-1]

---

### 2. King's Quest: Twins of Change (c. 1999–2002)

The most documented cancelled sequel, **King's Quest: Twins of Change**, was developed at Sierra Studios Seattle under Vivendi Games.[^ref-2] Its dates are estimates. The Omnipedia says work "probably began around 1999-2001" and that the game was being designed in 2002, while Wikipedia dates Vivendi's version to 2001–2002.[^ref-2][^ref-20]

**Development Team:**
- **Director:** Mark Seibert (KQ8 veteran)
- **Writer:** Cindy Vanous
- **Art Director:** Jimmy Kowalski

#### Story Summary

The game was set in a world that, according to Cindy Vanous, "looks rather like Daventry, except for all the parts that don't." Alexander and Rosella had been hit by wild magic and "weren't exactly human any more," and the script called them "Alex" and "Rose."[^ref-2] Vanous said the team was writing "a lot of fun familial banter between the two of them." The Omnipedia lists Lava World and Sea World among the game's lands.[^ref-2]

#### Gameplay

It was planned as a console-style 3D action-adventure in the vein of *The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time*, and the Omnipedia describes it as a cartoon-style action title.[^ref-2][^ref-20]

**Transformation System:** Each character had animal forms that were unlocked over time to solve puzzles. Rose's forms were small flying creatures, including a tiny fairy, and Alex had larger, stronger forms.[^ref-2]

The game was single-player. As Vanous explained: "It was, after all, a King's Quest game: the interactive version of curling up with a good storybook on a cold winter's night." In the prototype, the player controlled Alex, with Rose acting as a tutorial and navigation prompt like Navi in *Ocarina of Time*. Later, as new transformation skills were found, the player would control both characters. "Alex's transformations were more geared to physical puzzles; Rose's were suited for tactical and stealth puzzles." The game was shelved early, and the designers had not yet decided whether the two characters would play in alternating chapters or could be swapped at will.[^ref-2]

**Art Direction:** Jimmy Kowalski reimagined Alexander wearing a silver-crowned metallic helmet with a turtle-shell crown and a rhino-like horn, a callback to King Graham's Adventurer's Cap. He carried a huge sword and wore blue and red armor with clawed gauntlets.[^ref-2]

**Why It Failed:** Twins of Change died when Sierra's Seattle studio "finally died under Vivendi Games."[^ref-2] Mark Seibert recalled that the project "got 2 green lights… but never got fully funded. The company pretty much imploded while this project was in the early concept phase."[^ref-2] The Omnipedia suggests the project may have been "wounded" as early as Chainsaw Monday (February 22, 1999).[^ref-2]

### 3. Silicon Knights Project (c. 2007)

According to the Omnipedia, Canadian developer Silicon Knights (known for *Eternal Darkness* and *Too Human*)[^ref-3] negotiated with Vivendi to develop a King's Quest game around 2007. It reportedly got as far as a prototype, apparently using Epic's Unreal Engine 3, and its exact start and end dates are unclear.[^ref-1] Wikipedia notes that the prototype's existence became public through documents from Silicon Knights' lawsuit against Epic Games.[^ref-20]

**Why It Failed:** The project was cancelled. Its fate became tangled with Silicon Knights' legal battle with Epic Games. Silicon Knights sued Epic on July 19, 2007. Epic counter-sued, and on May 30, 2012 Epic won its counter-suit, with $4.45 million awarded. On November 7, 2012, the court ordered Silicon Knights to destroy all game code derived from Unreal Engine 3. The titles named in the order were *Too Human*, *X-Men Destiny*, *The Sandman*, *The Box*/*Ritualyst* and *Siren in the Maelstrom*. No King's Quest project is named.[^ref-3] The Omnipedia concludes that any material related to the KQ prototype has "probably been destroyed".[^ref-1] Silicon Knights filed for bankruptcy on May 16, 2014.[^ref-3]

No story, gameplay, or visual details from this version have ever surfaced publicly.[^ref-1]

---

### 4. Telltale Games (2011–2013)

Telltale Games announced its King's Quest project at a press event on **February 17, 2011**, as part of an agreement with Activision to make episodic games based on classic Sierra properties.[^ref-4][^ref-16]

**Development Details:** Telltale approached [[Roberta Williams]] to see if she was interested in working on the new game. Williams declined, saying she had retired from games, but she offered advice that developer Dave Grossman called "very valuable."[^ref-16][^ref-20] The game was to follow Telltale's episodic format, like *Tales of Monkey Island*, and fit the series canon. Grossman said it would "preserve those elements of peril, challenge, and yes, death", while trying to address the frustration that often came with them.[^ref-16][^ref-24]

**Why It Failed:** On **April 3, 2013**, Telltale's Steve Allison confirmed the project had been cancelled and the rights had returned to Activision:[^ref-17][^ref-20]

> "While we deeply love King's Quest here at Telltale, we can confirm that we are no longer working on the franchise. There was a time last year that we investigated partnering with third party developers to produce the game as a partnership but decided against outsourcing."[^ref-17]

Activision then took the series to The Odd Gentlemen for the 2015 reboot.[^ref-6][^ref-8]

## The Silver Lining (Unofficial Fan Project, 2000–2011)

[[2010 - The Silver Lining|The Silver Lining]] was never an official King's Quest IX. It was a fan project, originally titled **"King's Quest IX: Every Cloak Has a Silver Lining"**, that dropped the King's Quest name to resolve legal issues.[^ref-19]

Production began in October 2000, and in January 2002 the team was reorganised under César Bittar. Vivendi sent a cease-and-desist in September 2005. After a fan campaign, Vivendi granted the team a **Fan License** on November 29, 2005, on the condition that the King's Quest reference be removed from the title. After a further dispute with Activision, the company allowed the game's release on June 26, 2010, provided it stayed free.[^ref-19]

The first episode was released on July 10, 2010, and Episode 4 followed in November 2011. Episode 5 was never released. Phoenix turned the rights over to Activision around June 2014.[^ref-18][^ref-19] The game runs on the Torque Game Engine.[^ref-21] It remains an unofficial fan work outside the series canon.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The various projects took very different approaches. **Twins of Change** was planned as a console-style 3D action-adventure inspired by *Ocarina of Time*, not a point-and-click game.[^ref-2] **Telltale's** version was to be an episodic adventure in the mould of its *Tales of Monkey Island*.[^ref-16] Nothing is publicly known about the **Silicon Knights** prototype's design.[^ref-1] The unofficial **Silver Lining** used 3D graphics, but deliberately moved away from *Mask of Eternity*'s RPG-like approach and back toward the earlier games.[^ref-19]

### Structure and Progression

**Twins of Change** was built around the twins' transformations. Alex's forms were aimed at physical puzzles and Rose's at tactical and stealth puzzles. The team had not settled whether the characters would play in alternating chapters or be switchable at will.[^ref-2]

The **Telltale Games** version was to follow an episodic format. Rather than removing death, Telltale planned to keep the series' "peril, challenge, and yes, death" while reducing the frustration that came with them.[^ref-16][^ref-24]

## Reception

### Contemporary Coverage

No King's Quest IX was ever released, but several of the attempts drew press coverage. GameSpot later recalled that "Telltale announced that it had purchased the rights to King's Quest in 2011". When the rights returned to Activision in April 2013, it reported Telltale's statement that it was "no longer working on the franchise".[^ref-17]

**Retrospective Documentation:** The Digital Antiquarian has documented the dismantling of Sierra's original development studios under Vivendi.[^ref-5] The King's Quest Omnipedia has gathered first-hand correspondence from former developers, including Mark Seibert and Cindy Vanous, which preserves most of what is known about Twins of Change.[^ref-1][^ref-2]

### Modern Assessment

*The Silver Lining*, though forced to drop the "King's Quest IX" title, is the only KQ9-descended project that shipped anything, with four episodes released for free.[^ref-18][^ref-19] When the series did return in 2015, Chapter 1 of the reboot scored 82 (PC), 77 (PS4) and 80 (Xbox One) on Metacritic.[^ref-15]

## Development

### Origins

The idea of a ninth King's Quest goes back to discussions with Roberta Williams after *Mask of Eternity*. These ideas never went into development.[^ref-20] Sierra had by then changed hands repeatedly. CUC acquired it, CUC merged with HFS to form Cendant, and on November 20, 1998 Cendant announced the sale of its software division to Havas SA, a subsidiary of Vivendi. The sale closed on January 12, 1999, and the division became Havas Interactive.[^ref-23][^ref-5] Each later official attempt came from a different developer: Vivendi's Sierra Seattle studio, Silicon Knights and Telltale Games. All were cancelled before release, and The Odd Gentlemen rebooted the series in 2015.[^ref-20]

### Why No True KQ9?

Several factors contributed:

1. **Corporate Instability:** Sierra passed from CUC to Cendant and then to Vivendi in a few years. Vivendi came to treat Sierra as a publisher and distributor of games, not an active developer of them.[^ref-5]

2. **Adventure Game Decline:** When Chainsaw Monday came in February 1999, Sierra's Oakhurst studio had three major projects in development, and none was an adventure game. The Digital Antiquarian also notes "the diminished state of the adventure game in general".[^ref-5]

3. **Roberta Williams' Departure:** The series' creator left Sierra around the time of KQ8. The Omnipedia says she was bound by a non-competition agreement that kept her out of game-making for about five years.[^ref-1]

4. **Studio Closures:** Twins of Change died with Sierra's Seattle studio under Vivendi. Seibert said the project "never got fully funded" before "the company pretty much imploded".[^ref-2]

5. **Legal Issues:** Silicon Knights' 2007 suit against Epic Games ended in defeat in 2012. The court then ordered the destruction of all its Unreal Engine 3-derived code. The order does not name a King's Quest project, but the Omnipedia believes any prototype material was "probably" destroyed.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

6. **Differing Visions:** Without Williams, the official attempts took very different directions. Twins of Change was a Zelda-like console action game, and Telltale planned an episodic adventure.[^ref-2][^ref-16]

#### Chainsaw Monday and Corporate Dysfunction

On February 22, 1999, known as "Chainsaw Monday", Sierra's original Oakhurst office, by then renamed Yosemite Entertainment, was shut down, "putting most of its 125-plus employees out of work." About a third of the staff were offered relocation to the Bellevue office.[^ref-5] The Omnipedia suggests Twins of Change may have been "wounded" by the repercussions of that day, which eventually also took out Sierra's Seattle studios.[^ref-2]

#### A Pattern of Failure

Each official attempt took a fundamentally different approach to the series. Williams planned a traditional adventure, Twins of Change a Zelda-style action-adventure, and Telltale an episodic one. None survived long enough to reach completion.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-16]

### The 2015 Reboot

In 2015, The Odd Gentlemen released [[2015 - King's Quest|King's Quest]] (also known as *Adventures of Graham*),[^ref-1] an episodic re-imagining published by Activision under the revived Sierra label.[^ref-6][^ref-8] The developers said it would not be a point-and-click game, but clarified that it was "very much an adventure game".[^ref-10]

The developers explicitly said this was **not** King's Quest IX. "The game is completely reimagined; it's not King's Quest IX or anything like that," said The Odd Gentlemen's Matt Korba. As one developer put it, quoted by the Omnipedia: "It's going to be hard to make a King's Quest IX because everyone's so attached. The directive was to do a reimagining anyway."[^ref-1]

The 2015 game was the next King's Quest actually published after KQ8, but its developers did not consider it a true King's Quest IX; the Omnipedia calls it "not the fabled King's Quest IX".[^ref-1]

## Legacy

Despite never receiving a traditional ninth installment, King's Quest is widely considered a classic series from the golden era of adventure games. It was largely responsible for building Sierra's reputation.[^ref-20] The original games (1984–1998) pioneered animation and pseudo-3D environments in graphic adventures.[^ref-20]

The 2015 reboot received positive reviews[^ref-13][^ref-15] across its five-episode run, which ended in October 2016.[^ref-14]

### Fan Projects

The King's Quest community has produced numerous fan games and remakes, including [[2001 - King's Quest I VGA Remake|AGD Interactive's VGA remakes]]. [[2010 - The Silver Lining|The Silver Lining]] came closest to being a "King's Quest IX". It released four of five planned episodes between 2010 and 2011, Episode 5 never appeared, and it had to drop the KQ name entirely.[^ref-18][^ref-19]

### Documentation Efforts

Much of what is known about these cancelled projects comes from fan community efforts to interview former Sierra employees. The King's Quest Omnipedia has compiled extensive documentation from developer interviews.[^ref-1][^ref-2]
