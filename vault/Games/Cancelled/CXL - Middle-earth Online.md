# Middle-earth Online

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

Middle-earth Online (also known as MEO) was an ambitious massively multiplayer online role-playing game based on J.R.R. Tolkien's Middle-earth that was licensed by Sierra On-Line in 1998 and initially developed by Yosemite Entertainment.[^ref-1] The project represented one of the earliest attempts to bring Tolkien's legendary fantasy world into the online gaming space, predating what would eventually become The Lord of the Rings Online by nearly a decade.[^ref-2] Sierra wasn't the first MMO studio to take a crack at Tolkien's license—the company had a brief but memorable run designing what Engadget's retrospective called "What if LotRO had permadeath?"[^ref-3]

The game was distinguished by design ideas rarely seen in the genre, including permanent character death, a limit on the Elven population, and severe limits on magic.[^ref-4] The Elf limit was meant to keep player numbers in line with the diminished Elven population seen in the books.[^ref-3] However, Sierra hit financial trouble in 1999 and replaced the staff working on the game,[^ref-2] and by the end of the Sierra era the entire development team had been dismissed.[^ref-4] Years later fans were still asking why the game never shipped.[^ref-6]

This page covers Sierra's project. The name Middle-earth Online was later reused for a separate design by Turbine under Vivendi Universal Games (announced 2003), which became The Lord of the Rings Online; that later version is summarised separately below.[^ref-2][^ref-8]

## Story Summary

Sierra's Middle-earth Online was to take place after the events of the books, "a couple generations into the Fourth Age," trying to answer the question of "what happened next?"[^ref-3] It was designed as a sandbox experience in the style of Ultima Online rather than a themepark, letting players live as inhabitants of Middle-earth, build and destroy structures, and take on randomly generated adventures.[^ref-3]

The original Sierra vision placed players in a world where the consequences of their actions could be permanent.[^ref-4] Permadeath was to apply only in certain situations: according to Daniel James, only players who chose to become branded "murderers" would be fair game for it.[^ref-3]

### Turbine's later version (2003–2005)

Turbine's version, announced by VU Games in 2003, was a different design.[^ref-8] IGN's 2003 preview described it as set in the middle of the Third Age, with the Fellowship having just escaped Moria; players would never meet the heroes directly but would hear of them through NPCs such as Farmer Maggot.[^ref-9] Fans discussed this new version on forums in early 2004.[^ref-14]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Middle-earth Online was designed as a massively multiplayer online role-playing game in a persistent online world.[^ref-1] It was initially to be presented in a 2D isometric view, later changed to top-down 3D.[^ref-3]

### Structure and Progression

Player-killing and a heavy emphasis on crafting were layered on top of the sandbox, and characters were to have a "psychology" that players could modify through repeated or deliberate actions.[^ref-3] Ideas discussed by the team included characters automatically fleeing deadly fights and being captured by NPCs, requiring rescue by friends.[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

**Permanent Death System:**
Permanent death was one of the team's biggest ideas: under the high-level design, a player could work up to murder through lesser crimes and eventually permanently kill another player's character, and certain high-level monsters could also perma-kill.[^ref-3] Sierra programmer Joe Ludwig later called it "crazy."[^ref-3] A TheOneRing.net fan reviewer supported the idea, arguing it would add realism and importance to decisions, since in games with minimal death penalties "you might see very young characters tempting their fate against horrible creatures that eat the great warriors of the land."[^ref-5]

**Limited Elven Population:**
The team said it would limit the number of Elven players, to match the diminished population seen in the books.[^ref-3] The same TheOneRing.net reviewer noted that in a poll at the Mevault, Elves were the number one race players planned to play, and thought the limit would be hard to implement properly.[^ref-5]

**Restricted Magic:**
Magic was to be severely limited.[^ref-4] The TheOneRing.net reviewer endorsed this, writing that "even Gandalf didn't show a full array of fire balls and lightening [sic] bolts spitting forth from every orifice in his body," and that players casting spells of mass destruction everywhere would ruin the Middle-earth atmosphere.[^ref-5]

**Monster Play:**
To scratch the player-killing itch, players could join Sauron's side as a free-roaming Orc, growing bigger and badder with levels but increasingly tied to locations—possibly ending as a Balrog confined to Moria.[^ref-3]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

As Middle-earth Online was never released under its original name, there are no contemporary reviews of the final product. However, its design ideas drew comment from fan sites at the time.[^ref-4][^ref-5]

Eurogamer's 2007 review of The Lord of the Rings Online recalled that the developers of the original Middle-earth Online had "blabbed about crazy features like 'permanent character death', which instantly turned whole legions of gamers off the idea."[^ref-11]

### Modern Assessment

The project is primarily remembered as a fascinating "what if" in MMORPG history.[^ref-3] Engadget's Justin Olivetti suggested that had Sierra's version been released, it would most likely have remained niche because of its sandbox focus and the looming success of EverQuest.[^ref-3]

When The Lord of the Rings Online finally launched in 2007 under Turbine's development, Eurogamer gave it 9/10, saying it was "thrilled, delighted and in no small measure surprised" to be asking "How did it all go so right?"[^ref-11]

**Aggregate Scores (for The Lord of the Rings Online, the game's successor, as of October 11, 2026):**

- **Recent Steam Reviews:** 82% of 129 reviews in the last 30 days positive[^ref-12]

## Development

### Origins

In 1997, Sierra's Craig Alexander looked at the moderate success of The Realm Online and wondered how a similar game might do with a powerhouse license behind it; the idea of licensing Tolkien's works followed.[^ref-3] Sierra On-Line obtained the license in 1998, and development was assigned to Yosemite Entertainment.[^ref-1] Doing it right was expected to cost over $5 million, a massive sum for an unproven genre at the time.[^ref-3]

Sierra FX launched an introductory website for Middle-earth Online on what they described as the shared birthday of Bilbo and Frodo Baggins.[^ref-7] The website was designed as a "living atlas" providing story background and geographical information from Middle-earth, rather than a typical game site focused on features and screenshots.[^ref-7] Daniel James, writer on the project, stated that "in time, the site will feature extensively the game's art—which is presently under wraps—and material direct from the world and its players."[^ref-7]

### Production

The game began in 1998 and was optimistically slated for a 2000 release, but it faced massive layoffs and a studio relocation the following year as Sierra On-Line was sold to Vivendi; by all accounts the project also suffered from a lack of management.[^ref-3] After the project moved from California to Washington in 1999, little was heard from the new team aside from legal issues surrounding the license.[^ref-3]

When EverQuest came out during development, some of Sierra's higher-ups thought MEO could benefit from adopting EverQuest's features, such as more emphasis on magic and many more Elves. The team resisted these changes.[^ref-3] Stephen Nichols later said he "resisted every attempt to change the game's vision into a world where Gandalf is a dime a dozen and hobbits are hurling fireballs."[^ref-3]

The game had been in development for well over a year when the entire development team was axed and the game sent into a long delay.[^ref-4] TheOneRing.net speculated at the time that Sierra might want "to make the game more main stream and less accurate to Middle Earth."[^ref-4] Senior Designer Janus Anderson later recalled: "I actually began to think the license was cursed like the one ring, and that the game would never ship."[^ref-3]

### Technical Achievements

The Sierra-era game was to use The Realm Online's server code, at first in a 2D isometric format that was later changed to top-down 3D.[^ref-3]

Turbine's later, separate version was based on the engine Turbine had used for Asheron's Call 2.[^ref-9] An MMORPG.com E3 preview of Turbine's version reported that it would look "considerably better," with "enhanced support for improved water and dynamic lighting and shadow effects."[^ref-18]

### Technical Specifications

No contemporary source confirms target platforms or system requirements for the Sierra-era project. GameSpot's 2003 announcement of Turbine's version lists PC and Macintosh in its product metadata,[^ref-8] and Wikipedia's list of Middle-earth video games records the 2000 Sierra/Yosemite entry under Windows and macOS.[^ref-16]

### Cut Content

The design features that defined the original Sierra vision did not carry forward into The Lord of the Rings Online, which Turbine built as its own design:[^ref-3]

- **Permanent Death:** Not part of The Lord of the Rings Online[^ref-3]
- **Limited Elven Population:** Part of the Sierra design only[^ref-3]
- **Fourth Age sandbox setting:** Replaced by Turbine's Third Age "parallel journey" alongside the Fellowship[^ref-3][^ref-9]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Middle-earth Online itself generated no sales as it was never released. Vivendi Universal Games secured eight-year rights to Lord of the Rings games in 2001, reached an agreement with Turbine in 2003, and in March 2005 Turbine bought the rights itself.[^ref-2] The result launched in April 2007 as The Lord of the Rings Online: Shadows of Angmar.[^ref-2] The eventual game allowed players to create characters of four races and seven classes and adventure throughout the region of Eriador.[^ref-2]

The Lord of the Rings Online underwent a shift from subscription-based to free-to-play in 2010 and continued receiving expansions including Mines of Moria (2008), Siege of Mirkwood (2009), and content covering Isengard, Rohan, Gondor, and eventually Mordor (2017).[^ref-2] The game continues to operate today under Standing Stone Games and Daybreak Game Company.[^ref-2]

### Collections

Middle-earth Online was never released and thus never appeared in any game collections. The Lord of the Rings Online is available as a free-to-play title on Steam.[^ref-12]

### Fan Projects

For players interested in an older online Middle-earth, MUME (Multi-Users in Middle-earth), a text-based MUD, has been running since 1991.[^ref-10]

### Related Publications

No official publications were released for Middle-earth Online due to its cancellation. The introductory website served as the primary source of information during its development.[^ref-7]

### Critical Perspective

Middle-earth Online is a case study in a project that tried to challenge MMORPG conventions in pursuit of authenticity to its source material. Its ideas—permanent death, limited Elves and restricted magic—drew support from fan writers[^ref-5] but were mocked in later coverage,[^ref-11] and even one of its own programmers came to see permadeath as "crazy."[^ref-3]

The project's troubles coincided with Sierra's financial difficulties in 1999, when the staff working on the game was replaced.[^ref-2] Daniel James summarised the period: "I do think that spectacularly bad decisions were made, and an absence of leadership was demonstrated by some people who should have led."[^ref-3] The license eventually passed to Turbine, which "took the IP and did its own thing."[^ref-3]
