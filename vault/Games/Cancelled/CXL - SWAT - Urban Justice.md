# SWAT: Urban Justice

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

SWAT: Urban Justice was an ambitious tactical shooter developed by Sierra Entertainment, intended as the fourth installment in the SWAT franchise and the direct sequel to SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle.[^ref-1] The game was set in Los Angeles in the year 2006, coinciding with the city's 225th anniversary celebration, during which escalating gang warfare threatened to engulf the streets.[^ref-2] Players would take on the role of an elite SWAT officer tasked with restoring order and protecting civilians from three rival gangs vying for dominance.[^ref-3]

Despite extensive pre-release promotion and detailed developer diaries published throughout 2002, SWAT: Urban Justice slipped out of 2002 and then out of 2003, and in April 2004 Vivendi Universal Games announced that work on it had been "discontinued".[^ref-4][^ref-6][^ref-17][^ref-16] French site Gamekult described it as "l'épisode le plus décalé de la série des S.W.A.T" (the most offbeat episode of the SWAT series).[^ref-4] GameSpot's game page billed the title as featuring "more weapons, more combat gear, more bad guys, and a quicker pacing than ever before."[^ref-18]

The cancellation of Urban Justice led directly to the development of SWAT 4 by Irrational Games, as GameSpot reported on 28 April 2004: "According to a carefully parsed statement from a VU spokesperson, work on Urban Justice has been 'discontinued' in favor of SWAT 4's development."[^ref-16][^ref-6] Despite never being released, Urban Justice left a lasting mark on the franchise—an easter egg for the cancelled game appears in SWAT 4, where an arcade cabinet named "SWAT: Urban Justice" sits in the waiting room of the Victory Imports Auto Garage mission.[^ref-1][^ref-6][^ref-13]

## Story Summary

SWAT: Urban Justice was set in a near-future Los Angeles of 2006, where years of intense gang warfare had reached an all-time high, threatening to overwhelm law enforcement's ability to maintain order.[^ref-3] The timing was significant—the city was celebrating its 225th anniversary, and the festivities were being overshadowed by a vicious three-way battle for territorial dominance among the city's most dangerous criminal organizations.[^ref-2]

Three rival gangs—the Loco Riders, Krazy Boyz, and Compton 187—were engaged in open warfare for control of Los Angeles' streets.[^ref-7] The escalating violence required intervention from the city's elite SWAT unit, putting players in the boots of highly trained tactical officers tasked with taking back neighborhoods block by block through 16 planned missions.[^ref-3]

In a notable creative decision, the development team chose to feature a fictional SWAT team rather than the actual LA SWAT, granting themselves what they termed "liberdade poética" (poetic license) to explore scenarios and tactics without the constraints of strict realism.[^ref-7] UOL stressed that the differences were "discretas" (subtle) and did not undo the game's realism; as examples, the team could use shields and some of its members were women.[^ref-7] IGN's coverage likewise described the unit as "the highly trained men and women of the elite Special Weapons and Tactics unit."[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

SWAT: Urban Justice was designed as a first-person tactical shooter (Gamekult files it under "FPS - Tactique") putting players in the role of an elite SWAT officer.[^ref-4][^ref-2] For multiplayer, Sierra's Mike Nicolino described an improved matchmaking and lobby system in IGN's Developer Diary #13.[^ref-2]

### Structure and Progression

The game offered multiple gameplay modes to accommodate different playstyles:

- **Mission Mode:** Individual tactical operations that could be played standalone[^ref-3]
- **Career Mode:** Progressive advancement through the ranks of the SWAT team[^ref-3]
- **Multiplayer Mode:** Online competitive and cooperative play[^ref-3]

The full campaign would have comprised 16 missions set across various Los Angeles locations, each presenting unique tactical challenges related to the ongoing gang conflict.[^ref-3]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Urban Justice promised significant advances in tactical gameplay mechanics. Character models featured 5,000 polygons each with 29 differentiated impact points, allowing for precise hit detection and realistic damage modeling.[^ref-7] Players could utilize shields for protection during breaches, customize their weapons with various attachments, and even pistol-whip opponents and steal their equipment.[^ref-7]

The equipment system was extensive, including radios for team communication, infrared visors for low-light operations, gas masks for chemical environments, camouflage and disguises for covert approaches, various armor types, differentiated restraints for suspects, laser sights, and suppressors.[^ref-7] This arsenal was described as "arma suficiente para deixar até o Rambo contente" (enough weaponry to satisfy even Rambo).[^ref-7]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

As a cancelled title, SWAT: Urban Justice never received formal reviews from gaming publications. However, preview coverage was generally positive and anticipated. GameSpot's pre-release coverage highlighted that "Sierra's popular SWAT franchise is back with more weapons, more combat gear, more bad guys, and a quicker pacing than ever before," while its preview declared "SWAT is back, and it's badder than ever."[^ref-18]

Brazilian gaming publication UOL Start expressed optimism in their February 2002 preview, stating "Se o mesmo cuidado que foi investido em SWAT 3 se repetir, Urban Justice será um sério concorrente para as elaboradas tramas de Tom Clancy" (If the same care invested in SWAT 3 is repeated, Urban Justice will be a serious competitor for Tom Clancy's elaborate plots).[^ref-7]

### Modern Assessment

The game's cancellation means no formal reviews exist. However, the gaming preservation community has shown interest in documenting the title's development history. On Unseen64's page for the game, a reader poll of 9 votes averages 3.89 out of 5.[^ref-6]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **Metacritic:** Not Reviewed (cancelled)[^ref-5]
- **IGN:** NR (No Review)[^ref-2]
- **Unseen64 User Rating:** 3.89/5 (9 votes)[^ref-6]

## Development

### Origins

The SWAT series had evolved considerably since its origins, as noted by UOL Start: "A série SWAT já passou por uma infinidade de formatos: começou como Adventure, virou um jogo de estratégia com perspectiva isométrica, e depois amadureceu seguindo o rastro de Rainbow Six" (The SWAT series has gone through countless formats: it started as an Adventure, became a strategy game with isometric perspective, and then matured following in Rainbow Six's footsteps).[^ref-7]

Urban Justice was conceived as the fourth title in Sierra Entertainment's tactical action series, building upon the foundation established by SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle.[^ref-2] Sierra began advertising the game as "Coming Soon" in 2002 with a view to releasing it later that year.[^ref-6]

### Production

SWAT: Urban Justice was in development at Sierra in 2001–2002.[^ref-6] GameSpot described Rod Fung as the project lead,[^ref-18] and sound designer Evan Buehler called him "my executive producer".[^ref-9] Travis Steiner was the game designer.[^ref-2] Other team members named in IGN's coverage include QA Lead Craig Bradford, programmer Jason Olander and Mike Nicolino (multiplayer).[^ref-2]

The development team demonstrated exceptional attention to audio authenticity. Sound designer Evan Buehler traveled from Sierra's Bellevue, Washington headquarters to Idaho to record realistic weapon sounds at the Gemtech facility, accompanied by Rod Fung and fellow Sierra sound designer and composer Ben Houge.[^ref-9] Using professional studio equipment brought directly to the shooting range, the team recorded over 40 firearms.[^ref-9] Buehler's primary microphone was a DPA 4011, which can record sounds up to 158dB without distorting; for comparison, he noted, most of the gunshots recorded ranged from 140dB to 170dB.[^ref-9]

- **Composer/Sound Designer:** Evan Buehler
- **Sound Designer/Composer (Gemtech recording trip):** Ben Houge

- **Programmer:** Jason Olander
- **Multiplayer:** Mike Nicolino

### Technical Achievements

The game utilized the Takedown 3D engine, which supported detailed character models with 5,000 polygons each.[^ref-7] A sophisticated damage system featured 29 differentiated impact points on character models, allowing for realistic wound simulation and tactical implications for shot placement.[^ref-7]

Evan Buehler's sound design philosophy emphasized authenticity above all else. "Visual and aural realism is the hallmark of the SWAT series and is integral to my sound design for Urban Justice as well," he stated in a developer diary.[^ref-9] The team's dedication to realistic audio extended to suppressed weapons: "The sounds you hear in the movies when someone fires a suppressor are quite inaccurate. A suppressed sound is very unique and consists mostly of the sound of the gun mechanics. It's more of a clicking sound as opposed to the airy sound you often hear."[^ref-9]

### Cut Content

The entirety of SWAT: Urban Justice represents cut content, as the game was cancelled before release. A trailer for the game was produced and included on Disc 2 of No One Lives Forever 2, giving players a glimpse of what might have been.[^ref-1]

### Technical Issues

In March 2003 Sierra confirmed the game would not ship that year, while stressing it had not been cancelled.[^ref-17] On 10 March 2004 French gaming site Gamekult reported that the game, "Déjà repoussé à maintes reprises" (already pushed back numerous times), had its development "gelé pour quelques temps" (frozen for some time).[^ref-4] Wikipedia notes the project "was already suffering from internal delays" when the poor reception of SWAT: Global Strike Team reduced confidence in it.[^ref-10] Seven weeks later, on 28 April 2004, VU announced that work had been discontinued.[^ref-16]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Despite never being released, SWAT: Urban Justice lives on through an in-game reference:

- **SWAT 4 Arcade Cabinet:** In SWAT 4's Victory Imports Auto Garage mission (the Police Quest Omnipedia calls it Victory Imports Auto Center), an arcade game called "SWAT: Urban Justice" appears in the waiting room.[^ref-1][^ref-13] Unseen64, quoting IMDb trivia, notes: "This is a company in-joke; Urban Justice was supposed to be Sierra/Vivendi's follow up to SWAT 3: Close Quarter Battle (1999)."[^ref-6]

- **NOLF2 Trailer:** A 2002 promotional trailer for Urban Justice was included on Disc 2 of No One Lives Forever 2, preserving early footage of the cancelled game.[^ref-1]

- **ESRB Rating:** GameSpy lists an ESRB rating of M for Mature with content descriptors for Blood, Strong Language, and Violence.[^ref-3]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

SWAT: Urban Justice never reached market, but its cancellation had significant implications for the franchise's direction. The project's discontinuation led directly to SWAT 4's development by Irrational Games.[^ref-6] GameSpot reported that the VU spokesman "stressed that SWAT 4 is both a different game and a true sequel, so it will retain the series' gameplay elements, albeit in a refined form."[^ref-16][^ref-6]

### Collections

The game was never released and therefore did not appear in any compilation packages.

### Fan Projects

Due to the limited availability of development materials, no significant fan projects have attempted to reconstruct or complete SWAT: Urban Justice.

### Related Publications

- **Developer Diary Series:** Multiple developer diaries were published on IGN in 2002, including detailed coverage of sound design by Evan Buehler[^ref-9]
- **UOL Start Preview:** Extensive Portuguese-language preview from February 22, 2002[^ref-7]

### Critical Perspective

Wikipedia's account ties the cancellation to Sierra's console spin-off: Urban Justice "was to be a PC-only SWAT game to launch in 2003," and "the poor reception to SWAT: Global Strike Team reduced confidence in the project which was already suffering from internal delays."[^ref-10]

The cancellation of Urban Justice and the subsequent commissioning of SWAT 4 from Irrational Games represented a strategic pivot by Vivendi. Rather than continuing with the delayed internal Sierra project, VU Games substituted an all-new title from Irrational Games;[^ref-16] SWAT 4 later received an 8.5 from GameSpot.[^ref-11]
