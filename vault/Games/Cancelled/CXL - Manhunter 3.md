# Manhunter 3: London

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 17, 2026</small>

## Overview

Manhunter 3: London is a cancelled post-apocalyptic adventure game that was planned as the third installment in the Manhunter series.[^ref-1] The game was to be designed by Barry Murry, Dave Murry, and Dee Dee Murry of Evryware and published by Sierra On-Line, continuing the story of the unnamed Manhunter protagonist as he tracks down the serial killer Phil Cook to London and attempts to drive the alien Orbs away from Earth.[^ref-1] The game's existence was teased in 1989 through an in-game Easter egg in Manhunter 2: San Francisco, where a poster advertising "Manhunter 3: London" can be seen on the side of a tram at the Cable Car Barn.[^ref-1]

The cancellation of Manhunter 3: London represents one of Sierra's more notable unrealized projects, as the second game had ended on a dramatic cliffhanger that left fans anticipating the continuation of the story.[^ref-2][^ref-4] The series was originally conceived as a trilogy, and the Murrys also envisioned a fourth game following the London chapter.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-5] The rights to Manhunter are held by Activision, and according to Wikipedia "there are no plans for resumption"; the only reported revival effort is an unverified 2008 forum claim (see Revival Attempts).[^ref-10][^ref-3]

## Story Summary

No plot for Manhunter 3: London was ever published. The Manhunter shrine at Larry Laffer Dot Net states that "the plot is completely unknown" and that, since no deal was struck to move the game into production, it probably "never went beyond the conceptual stage."[^ref-2]

What is known comes from the end of the second game and from fan wikis. Manhunter 2: San Francisco closes with Phil Cook escaping in an Orb ship with the player hanging on to the outside, flying off towards London.[^ref-10] The Manhunter Wiki describes the third game as taking place in London, with the Manhunter tracking down Phil Cook and driving the Orbs away from Earth.[^ref-1] The shrine adds that, as the "third and deciding game" in the trilogy, it is "likely safe to assume" the Orbs would have been driven not only from London but from Earth as well.[^ref-2]

A 2008 forum post, discussed under Revival Attempts below, claimed that a revived London game would have the player control a new Manhunter for the first half and the original protagonist for the second.[^ref-3] That claim concerns a possible later revival, not the original Sierra-era project, and was never confirmed.[^ref-3]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The first two Manhunter games used Sierra's Adventure Game Interpreter (AGI) development tool but diverged significantly from other AGI games by not using a text parser.[^ref-5] Instead, the games featured a rudimentary point-and-click interface that foreshadowed later games like Myst.[^ref-5] No details of the interface planned for a third game are known.[^ref-2]

The series was notable for incorporating a first-person perspective rather than the third-person view common in other Sierra adventures.[^ref-5] The Manhunter games featured no live dialogues; much of the gameplay revolved around tracking individuals with the MAD device to unlock new places and addresses.[^ref-5]

### Structure and Progression

Because the game never entered production, its structure is unknown.[^ref-2] In the released games, the backstory and plot remained vague and obscure, requiring players to piece together the movements of suspects and understand the narrative through observation rather than explicit exposition.[^ref-5] Each chapter began with the Orbs assigning the Manhunter a crime to investigate, and the player reported the correct suspect's name at the end of the day.[^ref-8]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The previous Manhunter games combined tracking sequences using the MAD device, first-person investigation of locations, and arcade-style challenges.[^ref-8][^ref-9] Manhunter 2 kept the arcade challenges but added difficulty modes, and its investigations still centred on gruesome murders whose clues could seem arbitrary.[^ref-6] Players had to follow suspects through their daily movements, identify key locations, and gather evidence to progress the story.[^ref-5][^ref-8]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

As Manhunter 3: London was never released, no contemporary reviews exist for the title itself. However, the context for its anticipated reception can be understood through the success of its predecessors.

According to the Larry Laffer Dot Net shrine, Manhunter: New York was released in 1988 to overwhelmingly positive reviews and was voted "Adventure Game of the Year" by several computer gaming magazines.[^ref-2] The developers reported that each Manhunter game sold more than 100,000 copies.[^ref-16] Manhunter 2: San Francisco followed in 1989 with sales roughly equaling those of the first game, and the shrine considers it the better game of the two.[^ref-2][^ref-9]

### Modern Assessment

The question of why no third game appeared has been raised by fans in forums such as a 2003 Sierra Gamers thread asking "Why was there never a third game?"[^ref-4] The Manhunter series as a whole is remembered for its gritty, sometimes gory visuals, unique interface, and use of real-life locations that set it apart from Sierra's other, more family-oriented titles.[^ref-5][^ref-19]

The shrine credits the series with bizarre camera angles, picture-in-a-picture views, long pans, extreme close-ups, overhead shots, and faux 3-D sequences.[^ref-2] Modern reviews are mixed: Just Games Retro's review of Manhunter 2 criticises its repetitive structure and still-painful arcade sequences.[^ref-6]

## Development

### Origins

The Manhunter series was the brainchild of Barry Murry, Dave Murry, and Dee Dee Murry of Evryware Inc., a software company they had founded in 1979.[^ref-2] Barry and Dave had previously earned accolades for The Ancient Art of War (1984) and The Ancient Art of War at Sea (1987), both published by Brøderbund.[^ref-2][^ref-18] They had also created Sierra Championship Boxing, which Sierra published in 1983 after a Microsoft contact introduced their game to Ken Williams.[^ref-2][^ref-16]

While the Murrys were working on The Ancient Art of War at Sea, Sierra offered them the chance to use its game system to make whatever kind of game they wanted.[^ref-2][^ref-16] The Murrys decided that they wanted this new game to be different from any of the other adventure games for which Sierra was becoming famous.[^ref-2] Their concept went through many different changes over the course of the design process, resulting in the unique style that would define the Manhunter series.[^ref-2]

### Production

No production of Manhunter 3: London is documented. The Larry Laffer Dot Net shrine states that "the association between Evryware and Sierra ended in 1989 and the two companies never partnered up again," and that no deal was ever struck to move the third game into production.[^ref-2] The next Evryware project discussed in a 1997 interview, after the two Manhunter games, was The Ancient Art of War in the Skies, begun "to complete our war trilogy"; it was published by MicroProse in 1992.[^ref-16][^ref-20]

Sierra co-founder Ken Williams offered a different recollection in a 2003 Sierra Gamers forum post, opening with "I hope I am remembering this right." He wrote that he had the Murrys "planned for Manhunter 3," but encouraged them to pursue their idea for Ancient Art of War, which Brøderbund then convinced them to publish; under his rule of never being the "high bidder," they never returned to Sierra.[^ref-4] Williams's account does not match the published chronology: The Ancient Art of War was released by Brøderbund in 1984, four years before Manhunter: New York, as another poster in the same thread pointed out.[^ref-4][^ref-18][^ref-19] In the same post Williams recalled that Brøderbund recalled the first Ancient Art of War box because the writing on its pictured vase turned out to be x-rated; this anecdote rests only on his recollection.[^ref-4]

### Technical Achievements

Manhunter 2: San Francisco was Sierra's final AGI-based title, after which Sierra focused exclusively on SCI for new adventure game development.[^ref-17] The Murrys said they used unusual camera angles, extreme close-ups, first-person views, long pans, aerial views and picture-in-a-picture, "many other things that hadn't been used before but became standard soon after," and that Manhunter was the first Sierra title with a no-text, point-and-click interface.[^ref-16]

No engine was ever specified for Manhunter 3: London, since the game never entered production.[^ref-2]

### Revival Attempts

In a 1997 Games Domain interview, Evryware said they did not own the rights to Manhunter but would "like to eventually do another Manhunter," adding that they did not have the time.[^ref-16]

In August 2008, a first-time poster on the AGD Interactive forums claimed to have emailed "the one Murray, Mike," who said the developers were working on a Manhunter London game and trying to polish a demo to pitch to a publisher.[^ref-3] The same post said the game would not necessarily be called "London" because, after 20 years, the developers wanted to bring in new players, and claimed the shrine's operator had been in contact with them about it.[^ref-3] The claim is second-hand and unverified, and the thread's final reply stated: "Nothing has been confirmed."[^ref-3]

No further announcements followed. The rights to Manhunter are held by Activision, and there are no plans for resumption.[^ref-7][^ref-10]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The most famous Easter egg related to Manhunter 3: London appears in Manhunter 2: San Francisco itself:[^ref-1]
- **London Poster:** Looking at the side of the tram at Cable Car Barn reveals posters advertising both "Manhunter: New York" and "Manhunter 3: London", a preview of the sequel that was never to be.[^ref-1]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

According to Evryware, both released Manhunter games sold more than 100,000 copies each.[^ref-16] One 2003 forum poster recalled that the series "didn't become successful enough for more sequels," but no published source confirms that sales were the reason for the cancellation; the documented sources point instead to the end of the Evryware–Sierra association in 1989.[^ref-4][^ref-2]

### Fan Projects

The Manhunter Omnipedia, a dedicated fan wiki, documents the series' lore and the cancelled third game, and lists a separate page titled "Manhunter 3: Ultimate Online Adventure" under See Also.[^ref-1] Fan sites such as the Manhunter shrine at Larry Laffer Dot Net have preserved the series' history.[^ref-2]

### Critical Perspective

The Manhunter series diverged from Sierra's parser-based adventures with a point-and-click interface that, in one retrospective's words, "foreshadowed later games like Myst."[^ref-5] The developers themselves said many of the games' presentation techniques "became standard soon after."[^ref-16] The games' gritty, sometimes gory tone stood in contrast to Sierra's other, more family-oriented adventure series.[^ref-5]

The series was meant to be a trilogy with the third game set in London, but only two games were created.[^ref-7] The story of Manhunter 3: London remains a notable "what if" among Sierra's unfinished series.
