# High Seas Trader

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

High Seas Trader is a naval strategy and trading simulation game that puts players in the role of a young impoverished nobleman who must take to the high seas in order to regain fame and fortune[^ref-1]. Set in the age of merchant sail, it asks players to rise through the ranks of the merchants' guild by increasing their rating in four categories: daring, honor, loyalty, and nobility[^ref-39]. Players begin as a Peddler and advance through Journeyman, Tradesman, Merchant and Master Merchant to Viscount[^ref-39][^ref-29].

Impressions' own marketing copy promised a "unique first-person, you are there 3-D perspective" that put the player at the helm, with "no more little ships on flat maps"[^ref-4]. *Computer Gaming World* previewed it as "Impressions Software's first foray into first-person perspective games"[^ref-30]. The game was developed and published by Impressions in 1995 for DOS and Amiga[^ref-8], as a follow-up to the company's earlier Merchant Colony[^ref-8]. Sierra On-Line acquired Impressions later in 1995 and then sold the game through its own catalog[^ref-34][^ref-35].

## Story Summary

In High Seas Trader, players assume the role of a merchant sea captain; the manual places the action "during the 17th and 18th centuries"[^ref-39]. The manual's introduction opens with "The year is 1651"[^ref-39], and Sierra's 1996 catalog likewise describes "a trading ship in 1651"[^ref-36]; secondary sources give the starting year as 1650[^ref-6][^ref-10]. The player's family name and fortune have been "stripped away by an evil Earl", and the goal is to rise to the rank of Viscount and so regain that status[^ref-36]. Players can choose their nationality from several seafaring European nations: England, France, Holland, Spain, or Portugal[^ref-10]. Nationality sets the home port and decides which ships are allies or enemies[^ref-39].

The central goal is to become the best merchant sailing the seven seas[^ref-11]. Players begin with a small merchant ship called a fluyt and about 5000 gold[^ref-29]. Standing in the merchants' guild depends on four ratings: daring, honor, loyalty, and nobility[^ref-39]. *Computer Gaming World* explained that daring rises with victorious sea battles, honor depends largely on how you react to random business propositions, loyalty on how you treat countrymen, allies and enemies, and nobility on your familiarity with the world map[^ref-29].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

High Seas Trader is mouse-driven: port activities are presented as paintings with clickable hotspots that open spreadsheet-style screens for the dock, market, charthouse, bank and tavern[^ref-29]. A mouse is required, but there are keyboard equivalents for sailing: [Z] for steering left, [S] for raising and lowering sails, [C] for steering right, [P] to pause, [=] to increase game speed, [-] to decrease game speed, and [ALT] + [X] to exit to DOS[^ref-2]. The game runs at 320x200 resolution and requires VGA graphics[^ref-9].

Players navigate through various port cities where they engage in trading activities, deal with banks and merchants, acquire property, and outfit their ships with guns, equipment, and crew[^ref-8]. The market screen shows buying and selling prices, the amount available and the amount aboard, and a Logs button keeps price records from other ports[^ref-29]. Combat lets players fight enemy nations and pirate ships, adding an action element to the primarily economic gameplay[^ref-10].

### Structure and Progression

The game operates on a ranking system where players advance through merchant guild levels based on their performance in the four core attributes[^ref-13]. As status and wealth grow, players may buy larger and faster ships that carry heavier weapons and more cargo[^ref-39]. The player runs trade routes, fends off pirates, collects artifacts and offers transport to fellow countrymen in need, all to climb the ranking ladder[^ref-6].

Trading forms the core of the gameplay experience, with players buying commodities where they are cheapest and selling them where they command the highest prices[^ref-10]. Abby Mullen notes that players learn where commodities were cheapest and most expensive, the historical names of many ports, and how alliances changed through the 17th and 18th centuries[^ref-10]. *Next Generation* noted that would-be merchants could trade "in 15 different commodities, from cotton to opium"[^ref-33]. Players can also acquire property, building up their trading company "into a regular East India type place"[^ref-15].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

High Seas Trader combines economic simulation with real-time sailing and combat. The trading system is based on what one reviewer called "a reasonable economic model"[^ref-3]. Players hire three types of crew: apprentices, sailors, and soldiers, each with different skills and wages[^ref-39]. Rations of meat, fruit and water keep the crew healthy; fruit spoils and runs out faster than meat, and rum is not needed for health but keeps up morale[^ref-39].

Ship combat takes place in the same first-person view as sailing, with a choice of round, chain and grape shot[^ref-39][^ref-29]. Period reviewers were unimpressed with it: *Computer Gaming World* found the combat "doesn't quite hold together" and the boarding sequence "a battle of attrition"[^ref-29], and *Dragon* wrote that the first-person sailing "falls short in execution"[^ref-38]. Players can collect treasures and artifacts during their travels, including the Mona Lisa[^ref-19].

Johnny L. Wilson of *Computer Gaming World* wrote that he "wanted to like this game" and that "if the sailing and combat had been designed as well as the trading and navigational approach, it would be an outstanding game"[^ref-29]. He also noted that the setup allowed only one sound card, so players had to choose between sound effects and General MIDI music[^ref-29]. In *Dragon*, John Brunkhart called the trading "quite frankly the game's best feature" but said of the 3-D sailing view: "Five years ago, such a presentation would have been ground-breaking, but today, it seems rather uninspiring"[^ref-38]. Alex Simmons of *PC Games* judged it "a well-designed game" that "hasn't got quite enough fluency or style to get its hooks into me"[^ref-37].

### Modern Assessment

Modern opinions are mixed. MyAbandonware users rate the game 4.47/5, and its reviewer HOTUD praised the "reasonable economic model" but felt the game "tacked on fancy graphics and a first person perspective sailing segment, to a big spreadsheet program"[^ref-3]. OldGames.sk gave the title a 70% rating[^ref-13], and MobyGames lists a critic average of 64% from 20 ratings[^ref-8]. Squakenet called the trading "extremely well implemented" but found "combat and sailing are awkward and dull"[^ref-18].

One GOG wishlist commenter wrote: "I learned so much about the spice trades, world geography, geopolitics, and just general life for people on the high seas"[^ref-5]. Another wrote: "A true classic that has aged better on nostalgia than in playability"[^ref-5]. A Lemon Amiga commenter (fyl2049, 2025) wrote that it was "clearly inspired by the classic Pirates game" but rated it 3/10[^ref-20].

## Development

### Origins

High Seas Trader was developed by Impressions, a strategy publisher founded in the UK by David Lester and Edward Grabowski that later moved its headquarters to the United States[^ref-32]. *Computer Gaming World* described Lester in 1993 as "Impressions President and Managing Director"[^ref-32], and by 1995 the company was based in Cambridge, Massachusetts[^ref-29]. MobyGames describes the game as a follow-up to Impressions' earlier Merchant Colony[^ref-8].

During development, under the working title High Seas Trader, the design team chose to leave slavery out of the game. *Computer Gaming World* reported in September 1994 that the team, like the designers of Sid Meier's Colonization, found the idea "to be repugnant" and resisted "rewarding" the gamer for it[^ref-31].

Sierra On-Line announced in 1995 that it had acquired Impressions Software, "creators of the popular strategy titles Front Lines, High Seas Trader, and Powerhouse"[^ref-34]. MobyGames' Sierra history records that the purchase "improved Sierra's strategy game offerings"[^ref-21].

### Production

MobyGames credits David Lester with the concept, Christopher J. Foster and David Lester with the design, Chris Gurski with programming, Chris Beatrice with art direction, and Jeremy A. Bell and Jason P. Rinaldi with sound and music; Foster also produced[^ref-8]. Speech was recorded by PolyLang Multimedia[^ref-8]. MobyGames lists the AIL/Miles Sound System as the game's sound engine[^ref-8].

According to *Dragon*, the designers' notes explained that the player commands only one ship because they "didn't want anything to interfere with the game's 'first-person' perspective"[^ref-38].

The game shipped on both CD-ROM and 3.5" floppy disks at $69.95[^ref-29]. Requirements were a 386DX-33 or better, 4 MB of RAM, VGA graphics, a CD-ROM drive and a mouse[^ref-29]; PCGamingWiki lists DOS 3.0 as the minimum operating system[^ref-9]. A floppy installation takes about 13 MB of hard drive space, while the CD-ROM version installs about 3.5 MB[^ref-40].

### Technical Notes

The CD-ROM version loads graphics such as LBM files from the disc and assumes the CD is drive D, which matters when running it under DOSBox[^ref-23]. The setup program only allows one sound card to be selected, so players cannot combine Sound Blaster effects with General MIDI music[^ref-24][^ref-29]. PCGamingWiki documents a driver-file workaround for this[^ref-9].

Players can edit saved games with a hex editor to change money and ship or cargo capacity[^ref-25], and to add soldiers, sailors or apprentices to the crew[^ref-16].

## Legacy

High Seas Trader has kept a small following among fans of historical trading games. GOG users have added it to the store's wishlist, where commenters ask for a re-release[^ref-5], and players still look for modern games like it[^ref-27]. Historian Abby Mullen called it "a worthy candidate for some updating and improving"[^ref-10].

### Commercial and Cultural Significance

After the acquisition, Sierra sold the CD-ROM version for $24.95 in its Holiday 1995 InterAction catalog[^ref-35] and promoted it again in Spring 1996 under the heading "Swashbuckling Strategy"[^ref-36]. Sierra fans count the Impressions games among the "Sierra Family"[^ref-22].
