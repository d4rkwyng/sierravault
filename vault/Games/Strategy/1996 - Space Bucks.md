# Space Bucks

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 14, 2026</small>

## Overview

Space Bucks is a space-based business simulation in which the player founds a trading company and tries to build "the best and biggest transport empire in the galaxy before the year 2500."[^ref-10][^ref-2] The box billed it as "Build a Galactic Transport Empire!"[^ref-1] Developed by Impressions Games and published by Sierra On-Line in 1996, it is a Windows 3.1 game on CD-ROM that also runs under Windows 95.[^ref-3][^ref-10] Players start with one ship and one starport, acquire landing rights to nearby planets and ferry needed goods to them, while competing against three computer-controlled trading companies.[^ref-4][^ref-10]

Victory comes through infrastructure investment, the stock market, piracy, sabotage and trade.[^ref-10][^ref-15] MyAbandonware's reviewer called it "essentially a space trading game, a genre that goes back to M.U.L.E. and more recently Gazillionaire."[^ref-8] Before release, Computer Gaming World's Martin E. Cirulis wrote that "Space Bucks could easily become all that Gazillionare should have been."[^ref-26]

## Story Summary

The manual's backstory opens in the year 2250. About two centuries earlier, a Tesarian trader stumbled on a human deep-space probe and introduced humanity to the other races of the Local Sector: the Colikar, the Secanii, the Krec N'had and the Madorians, who alone knew the secret of faster-than-light travel.[^ref-10] In 2208 the Madorians vanished, FTL travel was lost, and interstellar civilisation collapsed. Forty-two years later the drive has been rediscovered, and this time all five remaining races know how to build it.[^ref-10] Sierra's InterAction magazine likewise put players in the galaxy "as president of a fledgling shipping business in the year 2250."[^ref-33] MobyGames' description gives 2375 as the year a game begins.[^ref-7]

Each game starts by naming a company and choosing one of the five races,[^ref-5] each of which has its own history with the others; the game assigns a different race to each of the three competitors.[^ref-10][^ref-12] The player then picks a home planet for the first starport and ship.[^ref-12] Sierra's own blurb described the galaxy as "wide open," with landing rights on nearby planets up for grabs.[^ref-13]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

A mouse is required.[^ref-10] The game runs at 640×480 in 256 colours, and keyboard and mouse input are supported.[^ref-9] Time passes in real time, counted in quarters and years, and the speed can be raised or the game paused.[^ref-10][^ref-31] News and status messages arrive as full-motion video clips from an "IGN" newscaster and the player's personal assistant.[^ref-10]

### Structure and Progression

According to the manual, the galaxy has twelve types of planets, each with a development level from one to six; more developed planets produce more cargo, and trade makes them develop faster.[^ref-10][^ref-32] There are twelve cargo types: four raw goods, two manufactured goods, five race-specific specialty goods, and toxic waste, a by-product of metal and chemical industries.[^ref-10] Each new game generates a random galaxy; Computer Gaming World counted "over a hundred stars."[^ref-27]

**Ships and Fleet Management**

Ships are bought ready-made or designed from six hulls of varying cargo capacity, plus engines, shields and weapons, which improve over the course of the game.[^ref-27] Cargo bays are configured per route, so that a ship carries what one planet produces and the next one wants.[^ref-10]

**Planets and Landing Rights**

To expand, the player negotiates for the right to build a starport on a planet. The terms can include quarterly rent, regular deliveries of a specified cargo, and infrastructure to be built.[^ref-10][^ref-27] Bids near a rival's trade lanes can turn into bidding wars.[^ref-27] Failing to deliver on promises lowers the Customer Satisfaction Index (CSI), and if it drops far enough the locals may cancel the licence and invite a competitor in.[^ref-10] Industries can be built to turn raw goods into more valuable cargo. One player guide advises building near Tesarian planets, because the Tesarians pay well for toxic waste.[^ref-27][^ref-11]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

**Stock Market:** All four companies start privately owned with 1,000 shares. Issuing shares raises cash, and buying 51% of a rival forces it into liquidation, which opens all its starports for negotiation.[^ref-10]

**Covert Operations:** If the option is switched on, companies can hire pirates to harass a rival, sabotage a ship, or bribe locals to raise their CSI.[^ref-10] Pirate attacks are resolved automatically from the ships' equipment.[^ref-31] Computer Gaming World found that pirates "hardly ever attack."[^ref-27]

Computer Games Magazine's Scott Udell summed up play this way: "The best metaphor I can come up with for Space Bucks is juggling: there are not a lot of different things to do, but it's all too easy for things to get out of control quickly."[^ref-14] MyAbandonware's reviewer HOTUD wrote that the game "suffers from the same 'spreadsheet syndrome' of many old Impressions games."[^ref-8]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| Computer Gaming World (May 1996, p. 218) | 2½/5 stars | Martin E. Cirulis: "definitely no Air Bucks in Space"[^ref-27][^ref-28] |
| Next Generation #18 (June 1996, p. 125) | 1/5 stars | "No cut-throat strategy… no space battles"[^ref-29][^ref-15] |
| Joystick #70 (April 1996, p. 86) | Intérêt 75%, Technique 70% | Original subject, but difficult handling[^ref-30] |
| PC Joker (April 1996, p. 85) | 71% | Recommended to fans of the genre[^ref-31] |
| PC Player 5/96 (p. 114) | 3/5 stars (Monika Stoschek) | Rated above average; other staff gave 2–3 stars[^ref-32] |
| Gamer's Zone (Rich Cunningham) | 3 globes (overall) | Graphics and interface 4, fun factor 3[^ref-16] |

Contemporary reception was mixed to negative in the US and somewhat warmer in Europe. Computer Gaming World's Martin E. Cirulis judged it "simple and repetitive enough to make Transport Tycoon seem like Capitalism," criticised "dubious AI 'advantages'" and concluded the game was "pretty to look at, but definitely no Air Bucks in Space."[^ref-27] The magazine's 1996 index lists the review at 2½ stars.[^ref-28] Next Generation complained that there was "No cut-throat strategy for undermining the competition's prices, no sabotage of trading routes, no space battles — not even the occasional price war," and gave it one star.[^ref-29] PC Player's Monika Stoschek praised the "ordentliches Handbuch" (solid manual) and the randomly generated galaxies, and concluded: "Alles in allem landet Space Bucks über dem Durchschnitt" (all in all, Space Bucks lands above average).[^ref-32] Joystick found the subject original but criticised the difficult handling and limited depth that it said were typical of Impressions games.[^ref-30] Gamer's Zone called it fun but noted "a rushed feeling," and said its main weakness was "lack of originality."[^ref-16]

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames lists a critics' average of 64% from 11 ratings.[^ref-7] On MyAbandonware, the reviewer HOTUD (whose review carries no score) concluded that "ultimately the awful user interface and extreme micro management makes Space Bucks more repetitive than fun." The site's users rate the game 4.38/5 from 8 votes.[^ref-8]

Scott Udell of Computer Games Magazine concluded that "Beginning business sim fans might get more out of Space Bucks than I did, but I suspect advanced gamers will want to stick with a more detailed game like Capitalism."[^ref-14] The retro site Squakenet called it "a very underrated space strategy game that is concentrated solely on trading," under the headline "Too complicated to be truly fun."[^ref-21]

## Development

### Origins

Space Bucks followed Impressions' airline-business game Air Bucks. PC Joker noted that Impressions had limited itself to air trade with Air Bucks in spring 1992, and that "der Nachfolger" (the successor) reached for the stars, something "Konzernmutter Sierra" (parent company Sierra) would welcome.[^ref-31] Gamer's Zone's reviewer found Air Bucks v1.2 in a discount bin and saw Space Bucks as a rewritten and updated version of it.[^ref-16] Computer Gaming World also called it "a child of the very successful Air Bucks."[^ref-27] In early 1996 Sierra sold it alongside Impressions' Caesar II as the "Sierra Strategy Pack."[^ref-33]

### Production

The manual credits David Lester with core design. Glenn Oliver, Ken Ford, Steve Serafino, Chris Bamford and Jennifer Hawthorne did additional design. Glenn Oliver produced, Thanh Pham and Chris Gurski programmed, and Chris Beatrice handled art direction. Dennis Rose did graphics and animation.[^ref-10] Computer Gaming World's review box lists David Lester as designer.[^ref-27]

### Voice Cast

The game's news and advice come through video clips.[^ref-10]

**Principal Cast:**[^ref-10][^ref-7]

| Actor | Characters |
|-------|------------|
| Karen Miller | Personal Assistant |
| Enrique Cerna | IGN Newscaster |
| Michael Stanton | Technology Correspondent |

Karen Miller and Enrique Cerna are credited in the manual. Michael Stanton is listed in the MobyGames credits.[^ref-10][^ref-7]

Sound and music were by Jay Rinaldi, Jeremy Bell and Keith Zizza.[^ref-10] The game offers a choice of two music sets.[^ref-10]

### Technical Achievements

Space Bucks was distributed on a single CD-ROM. Computer Gaming World listed the requirements as Windows 3.1 or 95, a 486-33 or better, 8 MB RAM, SVGA graphics, hard-drive space, a 2x CD-ROM drive and a mouse, with support for SoundBlaster and Windows-compatible sound cards. The game had no copy protection, but the CD had to stay in the drive.[^ref-27][^ref-16][^ref-31] The list price was $54.95.[^ref-27][^ref-16][^ref-33] The installer copies part of the game to the hard drive and reads the rest from the CD.[^ref-10]

## Legacy

### Critical Assessment

In a 2014 Retro Scan of the Week post on the game, Benj Edwards remarked: "I have this feeling that most Windows games from the 1995 era slipped through the cracks and were mostly forgotten."[^ref-22] Players still discuss it occasionally: a 2024 thread in the Impressions Games subreddit asked whether anyone had played it.[^ref-19]

### Developer Legacy

Impressions was already the maker of the Caesar series when Space Bucks shipped. Sierra bundled the game with Caesar II in 1996.[^ref-8][^ref-33]
