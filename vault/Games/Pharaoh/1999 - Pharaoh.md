# Pharaoh

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Pharaoh is an isometric city-building game set in ancient Egypt, developed by Impressions Games and published by Sierra Studios for Windows; it was released in North America around the turn of October and November 1999, with sources giving dates from 31 October to 5 November[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-11][^ref-27]. Built on the same engine and principles as Caesar III, it was the first game in Sierra's City Building series to move away from Rome to another ancient civilization[^ref-1][^ref-12]. Players guide a family lineage through the eras of ancient Egypt, with the ultimate goal of becoming Pharaoh[^ref-11], and the game adds Egypt-specific systems such as Nile floodplain farming, monument construction and a pantheon of five gods[^ref-1][^ref-14].

Eurogamer reported that after Caesar III, "Impressions decided it was time for a change of scenery"[^ref-12]. jeuxvideo.com's review credited the developers with remarkable documentary research ("recherches documentaires remarquables")[^ref-19], and composers Henry Beckett and Keith Zizza wrote that the game "is truly epic in scope and in scale. You start with nothing but the sand in your shoes and end ruling a Pharaoh's kingdom"[^ref-5].

## Story Summary

Players take on the role of a family lineage that rises through the eras of ancient Egypt, from humble village beginnings to the throne, with the ultimate goal of becoming Pharaoh[^ref-11].

The campaign runs through five periods of Egyptian history, from the Predynastic period to the New Kingdom, with the first period acting as a tutorial. In later periods players usually choose between a peaceful and a military mission, and only one of the two needs to be completed to move on[^ref-1][^ref-8]. Pharaoh Heaven's walkthrough index lists 38 career missions across those five periods[^ref-8], while jeuxvideo.com's 1999 review counted "3 campagnes, 30 missions" plus standalone missions[^ref-19].

Monument labour is recruited and paid rather than enslaved: IGN noted that "Impressions has decided not to use any sort of references to slavery," so players must build work camps, house workers and pay them every month[^ref-17].

The game incorporates Egyptian religion throughout, requiring players to appease the gods by building temples and holding festivals[^ref-10].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Pharaoh is played from a two-dimensional isometric perspective; the magnification level is fixed, but the view can be rotated in 90-degree steps, and control runs through the mouse and keyboard shortcuts in an interface similar to Caesar III's[^ref-1][^ref-11]. The game employs the same walker system as its predecessor, where service providers and goods distributors move through the city following roads, though this system has been criticized for its random pathfinding at intersections[^ref-12]. GameSpot noted that a new roadblock option gives players some control over where patrolling workers walk[^ref-14].

### Structure and Progression

The game offers two modes, campaign and free-build; free-build provides around a dozen standalone scenarios, some open-ended and some with goals[^ref-1]. Cities are rated on Population, Prosperity, Culture, Kingdom (the Pharaoh's view of the player's performance) and a new Monument rating tied to the monuments each mission requires[^ref-1]. The soundtrack is organized around three Kingdoms matching the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms, with the composers aiming for Old Kingdom music that sounds "old and primitive," a Middle Kingdom "full of hope and possibility," and a New Kingdom reflecting "all the glory, magic and mysticism befitting a mighty Pharaoh"[^ref-5].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Pharaoh introduces several mechanics specific to its Egyptian setting, including the annual flooding of the Nile, monument construction[^ref-14] and a religious system tied to festivals and temples[^ref-1][^ref-10]. Raw materials such as clay, reeds and stone are processed into goods and traded with other cities[^ref-1]. Players on the Heaven Games forums have documented walker limits: most service walkers can walk a maximum of 326.4 tiles, while bazaar buyers only purchase goods within a 40-tile range[^ref-15]. Eurogamer noted, approvingly, that "many of those missions can take you days to complete"[^ref-12].

Floodplain farms built near the Nile are a new feature, and irrigation ditches can increase the fertility of farmland[^ref-1]. GameSpot noted that the regular flooding demands players produce or import enough food to last through the flood season, and that a poor inundation can lead to bad irrigation and food shortages[^ref-14].

The game has five gods to appease: Ra (the kingdom), Bast (the home), Osiris (agriculture), Ptah (industry) and Seth (warfare). Players construct shrines, temples or temple complexes; appeased gods bestow gifts, while angered ones bring disasters tied to their focus[^ref-1]. Neglect can mean a drop in reputation from Ra, destroyed farms from Osiris, destroyed goods from Ptah, the deaths of soldiers from Seth or plague from Bast[^ref-9].

### Monument Construction

Monuments are new to the series: they can be built on a variety of missions, require worker facilities and building materials, and, unlike most other buildings, take time to be built[^ref-1]. GameSpot noted that most missions require monuments, which "get larger and more costly as you progress"[^ref-14].

### Modern Assessment

GameSpot's Ron Dulin concluded that "Pharaoh is slow-paced but addictive and is immensely complex but incredibly easy to play"[^ref-14]. The Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences named it a finalist for Outstanding Achievement in Art Direction at the 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards[^ref-1]. MobyGames lists two German magazine awards for 1999: PC Player (issue 01/2000) named it "Best Managerial Simulation in 1999" and Power Play (issue 02/2000) named it "Best Building Strategy Game in 1999"[^ref-11]. Jimmy Maher's 2025 retrospective was more critical: he wrote that he "desperately wanted to love Pharaoh" but cited "the excruciating worker-recruitment system" and "the drawn-out, repetitive campaign"[^ref-20]. As of November 2026 the GOG.com Pharaoh + Cleopatra release holds a 4.7/5 rating from 336 user reviews[^ref-7].

### Sales

In Germany, Pharaoh (sold as "Pharao") stood fifth on Media Control's CD-ROM sales chart for the second half of December 1999, in its sixth chart week[^ref-35]. The German trade association VUD's sales awards for April 2000 list "PHARAO" (Havas Interactive, PC CD-ROM) with a Gold award, given for passing 100,000 units sold within twelve months of release; the accompanying text says the game had sold "über 100.000" units by then[^ref-34]. According to a MeriStation report of 7 July 2000, sales of Pharaoh had been high worldwide, with more than 1,000,000 copies sold between Pharaoh and Caesar III[^ref-36].

## Development

### Origins

Impressions followed Caesar III with a move to ancient Egypt, building Pharaoh on the same engine[^ref-12]. Chris Beatrice, Impressions' Director of Design and Development, later said the studio had always considered Rome, Greece and Egypt the "big three" of ancient, classical civilizations and that Egypt "was our first choice after Rome"; he added that "we actually came close to doing Pharaoh on the Caesar 2 engine, but we really wanted to evolve the engine and game model a bit before expanding into other settings"[^ref-33]. In Pharaoh, he said, "we developed the world level a bit further, covering a much broader span of time, and making cities more interconnected with each other and with what came before"[^ref-33]. The game's help menu and manual include limited encyclopedic information about ancient Egyptian practices[^ref-1], and jeuxvideo.com observed that players finish the game knowing much more about Egyptian customs and ways of life[^ref-19].

### Production

Chris Beatrice was the game's lead designer, with Ken Parker and Greg Sheppard as producers, Darrin Horbal as art director and Lisa Gagnon as writer[^ref-6][^ref-25]. Beatrice, Horbal, quality assurance manager Jon Payne, Sheppard and Parker were the team members announced for a developer chat at the game's launch, held on Thursday, November 4, 1999[^ref-28]. The composers Henry Beckett and Keith Zizza faced unique challenges, noting "no one really knows what the music in the time of the Pharaohs sounded like," leading them to listen to other artists' interpretations and to watch "The Ten Commandments" more than once[^ref-5].

### Release

Sources give different dates for the North American release. GameSpot's game page lists 31 October 1999[^ref-27]; Wikipedia gives 2 November 1999, citing a GameSpot news item of that date announcing that the game "releases to retailers nationwide"[^ref-1][^ref-28]; MobyGames lists November 1999[^ref-11]; and PCGamingWiki gives 5 November 1999[^ref-3]. The UK release was on 19 November 1999[^ref-1][^ref-11]. The retail CD offered small, medium and full installs[^ref-25]. The original game is still sold digitally, bundled with its expansion as Pharaoh + Cleopatra, which appeared on GOG on 24 May 2012[^ref-7] and on Steam with a listed release date of 15 December 2016[^ref-26][^ref-37].

## Legacy

Pharaoh was followed by the Cleopatra: Queen of the Nile expansion in 2000, developed by BreakAway Games[^ref-23]. GameSpot found that the expansion "adds more of just about everything, from imports to monuments"[^ref-23], and Wikipedia notes it extended the campaign into the Hellenistic period[^ref-1]. The next City Building game, Zeus: Master of Olympus, is considered in most respects very similar to Pharaoh[^ref-1], and Jimmy Maher wrote that it looks like Zeus "fixed all of these problems and more"[^ref-20].

A remake, Pharaoh: A New Era, was released by Triskell Interactive and Dotemu on 15 February 2023[^ref-1].

### Collections

- **Pharaoh Gold:** the game and the Cleopatra expansion bundled together; Wikipedia dates it to 2001[^ref-1], while MobyGames lists Pharaoh: Gold under 2000[^ref-11].
- **Other compilations:** MobyGames lists Pharaoh as included in Legenden Der Antike (1999), The Great Empires Collection (2000), The Great Empires Collection II (2001), 10 Spiele-Hits Vol. 1 (2003) and The Biggest Creators of Empires (2003)[^ref-11].
- **Pharaoh + Cleopatra:** the current digital edition on GOG (2012)[^ref-7] and Steam[^ref-26][^ref-37].
