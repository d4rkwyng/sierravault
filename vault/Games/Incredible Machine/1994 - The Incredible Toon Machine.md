# The Incredible Toon Machine

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Incredible Toon Machine is a puzzle game released in 1994 by [[Sierra On-Line]], developed by [[Jeff Tunnell Productions]] with development work also handled by Presage Software Development Company for both the Windows and Macintosh versions.[^ref-1][^ref-2] The game updates [[1993 - Sid & Al's Incredible Toons|Sid & Al's Incredible Toons]] (1993), the cartoon-themed spin-off of The Incredible Machine series, adding a range of in-puzzle animations to its puzzle-solving.[^ref-1]

The game challenges players to complete a series of Rube Goldberg contraptions with crucial parts deliberately left out, using a selection of over 71 gadgets and cartoon-themed items.[^ref-5][^ref-6] While not considered an official part of The Incredible Machine series proper, it was created by designer Chris Cole as "essentially a cartoon version of the same concept."[^ref-7] The box copy advertises "130 new mind-crushing puzzles," a player-versus-player mode and the "Hometoons" level editor for designing your own puzzles;[^ref-6][^ref-8] Wikipedia, drawing on the *Boston Globe* review, counts 100 puzzles divided into four groups.[^ref-12]

What distinguished The Incredible Toon Machine from its more realistic counterparts was the emphasis on cartoon physics and humorous animations. *Computer Gaming World* noted that "the difference in the Toon version is that the result is always some wild animation where the cute, but not cuddly, creatures get anvils on the head and singed by dragon fire in order to get a chuckle out of you."[^ref-8] The game was targeted at younger players aged 6 and up while still offering challenging puzzles that kept adults engaged.[^ref-10]

## Story Summary

The Incredible Toon Machine takes place in a cartoon world populated by anthropomorphic characters engaged in slapstick rivalries. The primary protagonists are Sid E. Mouse and Al E. Cat, whose relationship mirrors the classic Tom and Jerry dynamic—Al will chase and eat Sid whenever he has the chance, making their interactions a central mechanic of the puzzle design.[^ref-13][^ref-14] As one fan aptly described it, players essentially "create your own Tom and Jerry cartoon simulator."[^ref-15]

The cast of characters extends beyond the cat-and-mouse duo to include Eunice Elephant, Hildegard Hen, Cliff Ant, and Bik Dragon, each with their own unique behaviors and interactions within the puzzle machinery.[^ref-13][^ref-16] MobyGames' description of the 1993 original, [[1993 - Sid & Al's Incredible Toons|Sid & Al's Incredible Toons]], puts it this way: "Sid Mouse and Al E. Cat have probably never heard of Tom & Jerry, but they're just as single-minded: any means to hurt the adversary is a good one."[^ref-17]

Rather than following a traditional narrative structure, the game presents puzzles as cartoon productions, treating each level as a scene to be completed. The marketing tagline captured this theatrical approach: "When You Play The Incredible Toon Machine, You Give Life to Cartoons."[^ref-18] Players progress through increasingly challenging scenarios, witnessing "cats gag on hairballs or elephants sneezing their way through puzzle solutions."[^ref-8]

The game's premise drew from the rich tradition of cartoon physics established by animators of the early to mid-1900s, most notably Rube Goldberg, whose elaborate contraption drawings became the conceptual foundation for the entire Incredible Machine series.[^ref-19] The cartoon setting allowed for more fantastical elements including dragons, dynamite, and other items that would be out of place in the more grounded original series.

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The Incredible Toon Machine operates as a single-player puzzle game utilizing a mouse-driven interface within the Windows 3.x environment.[^ref-5][^ref-20] The game presents a fixed 2D screen where players must arrange various objects and gadgets to achieve specific goals.[^ref-5] Players interact with the puzzle space by selecting items from their available inventory and placing them within the contraption, then activating the machine to observe the results.

The interface includes a tutorial section to familiarize players with the game mechanics and the unique behaviors of each part.[^ref-21] Most items can be flipped horizontally, with some offering vertical flipping as well, while special items like the Hat Pin can be rotated in any of four directions, allowing for precise placement.[^ref-13]

### Structure and Progression

The game's 100 puzzles are divided into four groups[^ref-12] (the box copy advertises "130 new" puzzles[^ref-6]); the GameFAQs walkthrough names them:[^ref-21]

- **Cake Walk:** Introductory puzzles for learning the basics
- **All Brawn No Brains:** Easy to moderate challenges
- **Really REALLY Hard:** Advanced puzzles requiring careful planning
- **LOONEY BIN!:** The most challenging puzzles in the game

According to Wikipedia, the first 30 puzzles serve as tutorials; each of the other three groups has 15 puzzles that unlock five more, and finishing all 60 non-tutorial puzzles opens 10 more.[^ref-12] The Windows CD version added animated cut scenes and CD music tracks to enhance the presentation between puzzle sections.[^ref-13]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core objective mirrors the broader Incredible Machine series: completing Rube Goldberg-type contraptions using a provided set of parts.[^ref-23] Players receive a selection of gadgets for each puzzle and must arrange them so that when the machine is activated, a chain reaction accomplishes the stated goal. The game includes a "Make A Machine" or "Hometoons" mode allowing players to design their own levels using all 71 available gadgets.[^ref-6][^ref-8]

The cartoon-themed parts behave according to exaggerated physics, with interactions including cheese luring Sid Mouse, anvils falling on characters' heads, dragons breathing fire, and elephants sneezing to propel objects.[^ref-8][^ref-17] Each puzzle has multiple possible solutions, encouraging creative thinking and experimentation.[^ref-11]

Copy protection was implemented through a password system requiring players to reference the manual.[^ref-21] Additionally, players could enter the password "WIND" to unlock secret puzzles not available through normal progression.[^ref-24]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

*The Boston Globe* reviewer Adam Baratz gave The Incredible Toon Machine 4 out of 5 in his June 5, 1995 review, and *Computer Player* scored it 8/10 in April 1995.[^ref-12] *Computer Gaming World*'s 1994 notice (quoted in the Overview) stressed that "the entertainment value has been punched up to the limit."[^ref-8]

**Reception of the 1993 original.** [[1993 - Sid & Al's Incredible Toons|Sid & Al's Incredible Toons]], the game this release updates, was reviewed on its own: *Electronic Entertainment* gave it 80 out of 100 ("Rube Goldberg meets Wile E. Coyote"), and it was a runner-up for *Computer Gaming World*'s Strategy Game of the Year in June 1994.[^ref-4] Those scores belong to the 1993 game, not to this one; see its page for its full reception.

### Modern Assessment

Games Nostalgia describes The Incredible Machine series as "one of the most original puzzle games ever created for personal computers," praising its ability to teach physics principles while encouraging critical thinking and creativity.[^ref-25] The Home of the Underdogs review, reproduced on MyAbandonware, calls it "one puzzler that will keep you glued to the screen until the wee hours" and "a must-have for every puzzle fan."[^ref-8]

## Development

### Origins

The Incredible Toon Machine emerged from [[Jeff Tunnell]]'s desire to continue innovating within the puzzle game genre he had helped establish. Tunnell left Dynamix in 1991 to start Jeff Tunnell Productions,[^ref-28] which he founded in early 1992 hoping to return to "the guerrilla model of game development" and make smaller, more innovative games.[^ref-11] The Incredible Machine concept behind it drew on Rube Goldberg's cartoons,[^ref-19] and, as *The Digital Antiquarian* traces it, on Electronic Arts' Pinball Construction Set and the Mouse Trap board game.[^ref-11]

The original The Incredible Machine (1992) was conceived by Tunnell and Slye as early as 1983, but the technology and market weren't ready for such a title until the early 1990s.[^ref-11] The success of the original game and its expansion, The Even More Incredible Machine (1993), led to the spin-off concept that would become Sid & Al's Incredible Toons, which was then updated and expanded into The Incredible Toon Machine.

### Production

The Incredible Toon Machine was developed by Jeff Tunnell Productions with Presage Software Development Company handling the programming for both the Macintosh and Windows versions.[^ref-2] The game built upon the foundation of Sid & Al's Incredible Toons, advertising "130 new" puzzles on the box (Wikipedia counts 100), a player-versus-player competitive mode, and the "Hometoons" level design feature.[^ref-6][^ref-8][^ref-12]

The credits list Chris Cole, Jeffrey Tunnell and Brian Hahn as original designers, Randy Dersham as Windows and Macintosh producer, and Ryan Hahn for the original game concept.[^ref-36]

### Technical Achievements

The game's physics simulator engine allowed for complex chain-reaction puzzles with multiple possible solutions.[^ref-11] The Windows CD version introduced animated cut scenes and CD music tracks.[^ref-13] The soundtrack rip's track titles include four classical pieces: "Tocatta en Fugue" (Bach), "Blue Danube" (Johann Strauss II), "The Can Can" (Offenbach) and "Barber of Seville" (Rossini).[^ref-29]

The game featured 21 distinct sound clips and music tracks, with the complete soundtrack totaling 45 minutes and 55 seconds of audio content.[^ref-29] The cartoon animations were noted for their entertainment value, with MobyGames observing that "the Toons adds a whole range of in-puzzle animations that increase the entertainment value of this release."[^ref-1]

### Technical Issues

The CD version is a 16-bit Windows application and will not function on 64-bit Windows operating systems without emulation or compatibility software.[^ref-32] Sierra Help Pages provides custom installers that work with either the floppy or CD version; the CD installer allows for full hard drive installation enabling CD-less play.[^ref-32]

Some users reported an "MCI Error: the specified parameter is out of range for the specified command" when attempting to run the game on modern systems.[^ref-8] The game requires a Windows 3.x environment, leading forum members to note that "This program must be run under Microsoft Windows."[^ref-20]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

The Incredible Machine series proved to be one of Sierra On-Line's most enduring franchises, spawning numerous sequels and spin-offs throughout the 1990s and early 2000s.[^ref-35] In October 2009 the series and its intellectual property were acquired by PushButton Labs, founded by Jeff Tunnell;[^ref-19][^ref-37] PushButton Labs was later acquired by Playdom, a division of Disney Interactive, so the rights passed to Disney.[^ref-37]

### Collections

The Incredible Toon Machine was not part of The Incredible Machine Mega Pack, which Playdom released on GOG.com on October 1, 2009 with The Even More Incredible Machine, The Incredible Machine 3, Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions and The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions.[^ref-37]

### Fan Projects

In 2014, a spiritual successor titled Contraption Maker was released, continuing the legacy of Rube Goldberg-style puzzle games;[^ref-35] its Steam page lists a July 7, 2014 release with Kevin Ryan, the original Incredible Machine's designer, as developer.[^ref-37][^ref-38] The original games remain available through abandonware preservation sites and game archiving services like the Internet Archive.[^ref-13]
