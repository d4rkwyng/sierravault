# Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura: Incredible Toons

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura: Incredible Toons is a Japan-exclusive puzzle game released on August 30, 1996, for both the Sony PlayStation and Sega Saturn[^ref-1][^ref-2]. The game is a crossover between two distinct franchises: [[Dynamix|Dynamix's]] *The Incredible Machine* series and Capcom's *Ghosts 'n Goblins* (known as *Makaimura* in Japan)[^ref-3][^ref-4]. Capcom licensed the engine of Dynamix's *Sid & Al's Incredible Toons* and populated it with *Ghosts 'n Goblins* characters, including chibi versions of Arthur, Astaroth, Red Arremer and the Grim Reaper[^ref-7][^ref-5].

MobyGames and LaunchBox both describe it as the only *Incredible Machine* game with a plot[^ref-1][^ref-3]. Players place machines, traps, cogwheels and other items into each level to fulfil a goal, such as getting Arthur to the exit, then start the level to see whether the setup works[^ref-6]. One user-written description calls it "really a lot of fun and quite humorous"[^ref-6].

Developed by Magical Formation Co., Ltd. under license from Dynamix, the game was published by Capcom exclusively for the Japanese market[^ref-1][^ref-9]. It features remixed music from the *Ghosts 'n Goblins* series[^ref-3][^ref-7], 100 puzzles[^ref-12], and the option to create original puzzles[^ref-6]. *Famitsu*'s Cross Review scored both versions 29 out of 40[^ref-24].

## Story Summary

The plot follows the standard *Ghosts 'n Goblins* premise: the princess is kidnapped by Astaroth, the series' main villain, and Arthur must save her[^ref-1][^ref-3]. The game opens with an intro almost exactly like that of the arcade games[^ref-7].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura employs a point-and-select interface, viewed from the side with 2D scrolling[^ref-1]. Both versions support a mouse: the PlayStation version is compatible with the PlayStation Mouse[^ref-14], and the Saturn version supports the Shuttle Mouse[^ref-25].

Each level has a goal, and the player chooses items to place in it before setting the level in motion[^ref-6][^ref-26]. If the setup succeeds, the player moves on to the next stage[^ref-26]. On-screen goals are given in Japanese, but they are usually easy to work out through experimentation[^ref-7].

### Structure and Progression

*Saturn Fan* No. 18 (1996) says the puzzles are divided into three sets named for the series: *Makaimura*, *Daimakaimura* (大魔界村) and *Chōmakaimura* (超魔界村), by difficulty (難易度). Each set has 30 regular puzzles (各30問のレギュラーパズル), and the two later sets also have bonus puzzles that open after the regular ones are cleared (大魔界村と超魔界村にはレギュラーパズルクリア後のお楽しみボーナスパズルもある)[^ref-30].

The total is given as 100 puzzles by the RetroAchievements set and by a reader's letter in *Saturn Fan* No. 21[^ref-12][^ref-31]. The magazine page quoted above does not state a total or say how the bonus puzzles are counted, so this page does not derive one from the 30-per-set figure. The fan-made RetroAchievements set for the Saturn version, mirrored on Exophase, groups them into twenty achievements of five puzzles each, from "Clear Puzzles 1 through 5" to "Clear Puzzles 96 through 100"[^ref-12]. These achievement names are the work of the RetroAchievements community, not part of the original game.

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzle design follows *The Incredible Machine* formula: given a goal, the player builds a chain-reaction machine from a selection of parts[^ref-7]. Available items include machines, traps, cogwheels and many other contraptions[^ref-6]. *Saturn Fan* No. 18 describes an "Original" (「オリジナル」) mode for working through the supplied puzzles, and a separate "Home Toons" (「ホーム トゥーンズ」) mode in which players can freely build their own puzzles[^ref-30].

Some puzzles take a dark comedic turn by requiring the player to kill Arthur to win the stage[^ref-3][^ref-7]. The game incorporates items from the *Ghosts 'n Goblins* series. For example, Arthur will only move when lured with the armor power-up[^ref-3][^ref-7].

Unlike other games in *The Incredible Machine* series, this title has no Head-to-Head puzzles and no Freeform Machine mode, so it offers only single-player puzzles[^ref-1][^ref-3].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The game was not released outside Japan, and no contemporary English-language reviews have been identified. In Japan, *Weekly Famitsu*'s Cross Review (No. 403, September 6, 1996) gave both the Saturn and PlayStation versions 29 out of 40, from scores of 8, 7, 7 and 7[^ref-24]. For the Saturn version, the reviewers praised its freedom, its novel ideas and its addictiveness. They criticised puzzles that can be solved by accident and felt the *Makaimura* re-skin was somewhat forced[^ref-24]. For the PlayStation version, the reviewers found the roles of the many characters harder to grasp than in the PC original. They called the mouse controls marginal, but valued the sense of achievement on clearing a puzzle[^ref-24]. Two of the four reviewers named it among their Best Picks of the week[^ref-24]. The same issue's release listings describe it as 「パソコンで人気のパズルゲームの移植版だ」 ("a port of a puzzle game popular on PC") in which characters from the *Makaimura* series appear (「『魔界村』シリーズの…が登場するぞ」, with one OCR-garbled word elided)[^ref-29]. *Sega Saturn Magazine* (Japan) issue 15 (September 13, 1996) lists the game at page 204 of its contents[^ref-32].

#### Japanese magazine and reader reception

*Saturn Fan* No. 18 (1996) covered the game in its "Saturn Soft Impression" section under the headline 「またまたさらわれたプリンセスをパズルで救え!!」 ("Save the princess, kidnapped yet again, with puzzles!!"), noting that the princess, who keeps being kidnapped across the *Makaimura* series, "has been kidnapped again" (「またまたさらわれてしまった」). It judged that the game has a wealth of items and a variety of clear conditions, and that although some puzzles are mean (「いじわるな問題もある」), it "looks like it will be enjoyable for a long time" (「末長く楽しめそうだ」)[^ref-30].

*Saturn Fan* No. 21 (1996) printed reader comments on the game, with a reply from Capcom's PR department[^ref-31]. A reader from Hokkaido wrote that it was a puzzle game "with some bite" (「骨のあるパズルゲーム」) after a long gap and that, despite the *Makaimura* name, it was nothing like the easy game he had expected (「ぜんぜん違った」). A reader from Tokushima found it too hard (「難易度が高すぎる」): of the 100 [OCR-corrected from "10U"] puzzles he could barely get through half (「難しくて半分いくのがやっと」), and he called it a game for enthusiasts (「マニア向け」). The Hokkaido reader's view of difficulty was the same, but he added that solving a puzzle by accident almost never happens (「偶発的に解けた、なんてことはほとんど…」, with the last word garbled in the OCR), whereas the *Famitsu* reviewers had criticised puzzles that can be solved by accident[^ref-31][^ref-24]. Capcom's PR reply thanked readers for the comments and conceded "there are certainly difficult parts" (「確かにこのゲームは難しい部分もあると思います」), and the printed reply is cut off in the OCR just as it recommends the game to people who like solving puzzles (「謎を解くのが好きな方には、ぜひ」)[^ref-31].

*Saturn Fan* No. 21's spec box for the game lists Capcom as both publisher and developer (「発売元 カプコン」, 「開発元 カプコン」), which differs from MobyGames, which credits Magical Formation as developer[^ref-31][^ref-1]. *Saturn Fan* No. 18's spec box likewise lists カプコン as both publisher and developer[^ref-30], PSXDataCenter lists the developer as "Capcom / Dynamix"[^ref-14], and IGN lists Capcom and Dynamix as developers[^ref-4]. This page follows MobyGames and Sega Retro for the developer credit[^ref-1][^ref-25].

Sega Retro's review table for the Saturn version also lists *Saturn Fan* scores of 63 (No. 18, "Saturn Soft Impression", from individual marks of 6, 6, 5, 7, 7, 7, 5, 6, 6 and 8, shown as 6.3/10) and 65 (No. 21, from the "Ranking Street" report-card table, which gives six sub-ratings out of 5)[^ref-33], and *Sega Saturn Magazine* (Japan) scores of 57 and 82 (the 82 comes from the magazine's "Readers rating final data" of March 2000, a reader poll rather than a review)[^ref-25]. The same table's *Weekly Famitsu* figure of 73 cites the September 6, 1996 issue, so it is the 29/40 Cross Review above expressed as a percentage, not a separate review[^ref-25].

### Modern Assessment

GameFAQs users rate the Saturn version "Great" (17 ratings)[^ref-2]. Hardcore Gaming 101 called it "pretty difficult and really only for the most diehard puzzle fanatics, but it's an interesting curiosity for a Ghouls 'n Ghosts fan"[^ref-7]. The Retro Gaming Discussion Show podcast included the game in its episode 248, "Sega Saturn – A Japanese Voyage of Discovery" (July 19, 2019), a selection of Japanese-only Saturn games to play[^ref-15].

## Development

### Origins

Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura emerged from a licensing arrangement between Capcom and Sierra On-Line's subsidiary Dynamix[^ref-1][^ref-9]. The game is based on *Sid & Al's Incredible Toons*, a puzzle game developed by Dynamix and released by Sierra On-Line in 1993[^ref-7][^ref-14].

Capcom discussed the game in an interview with *Mean Machines Sega* (issue 40, February 1996), describing *Incredible Toons* as "an upgrade version of The Incredible Machine"[^ref-26]. The interviewer asked whether the game had been sent back to be re-planned. Capcom replied that it was "almost complete", but that because the items had been changed to characters from *Ghouls 'n Ghosts* the team had had to go back and redo that work. Capcom confirmed that the decision to use those characters was made late[^ref-26].

### Production

Development was handled by Magical Formation Co., Ltd., with Capcom as publisher and Dynamix as licensor[^ref-1][^ref-9][^ref-25]. Hideki Kamiya, who had joined Capcom in 1994, assisted with the game's development before joining the *Resident Evil* project as a planner[^ref-27]. English Wikipedia's infobox lists Kamiya as the game's designer, while its article text does not name a designer[^ref-34].

The game uses visual and audio material from the *Ghosts 'n Goblins* universe, including chibi-style renditions of series characters such as the Red Arremer and Astaroth[^ref-5][^ref-7]. The series' standard theme song is remixed in a number of variations[^ref-3][^ref-7].

### Cut Content

Unlike the standard *Incredible Machine* games, this title was released without Head-to-Head puzzles or the Freeform Machine mode[^ref-1][^ref-3]. Whether these features were intentionally omitted or cut during development remains unclear.

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

As a Japan-exclusive release, the game had limited commercial reach outside its home market. No official sales figures have been published, and no digital re-release or compilation appearance has been identified.

### Collections

No inclusion of Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura in an official game collection or compilation has been identified.

### Fan Projects

No significant fan projects, remakes, or translations have been documented. A fan-made RetroAchievements set exists for the Saturn version[^ref-12].

### Related Publications

Mainichi Communications published an official hint book, *Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura Hint Book* (アーサーとアスタロトの謎魔界村 ヒントブック), on November 1, 1996 (ISBN 4-89563-754-9)[^ref-24]. The GameFAQs archive contains a "Series Ending FAQ" by ReyVGM (2006) listed under this game on GameFAQs and tagged for the SNES[^ref-21].

### Critical Perspective

Arthur to Astaroth no Nazo Makaimura is an obscure spinoff of both *The Incredible Machine* and *Ghosts 'n Goblins*, a licensed crossover of Dynamix's puzzle engine with Capcom's characters that never left Japan[^ref-4][^ref-7]. Japanese critics at the time praised its puzzle design but were less convinced by the *Makaimura* re-skin[^ref-24]. Retrospective user ratings on GameFAQs are positive[^ref-2].
