# The Incredible Machine 2

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 23, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Incredible Machine 2 is a puzzle video game published for MS-DOS in 1994 by Sierra On-Line, part of The Incredible Machine series[^ref-44]. Developed by Jeff Tunnell Productions, it asks the player to finish machines with missing parts: "Each level of the game contains a machine designed to do a specific task. The only problem is, there are parts missing, and it is your job to figure out where to put what in order to get the machine to work"[^ref-34]. The game introduced new levels, an extended assortment of parts, a new interface, improved graphics, sound and music, and two-player hotseat play to the main series[^ref-44][^ref-9]. Sierra's own InterAction magazine promised "150 new brain twisting puzzles" in its Fall 1994 preview[^ref-49].

PC Gamer's Gary Meredith gave it 90% in January 1995, writing that "In every way, TIM2 is an improvement, keeping the best parts of the original yet giving the player more options and flexibility"[^ref-19]. Readers of Computer Gaming World voted it their Readers' Choice in the Classics/Puzzles category of the magazine's 1996 Premier Awards[^ref-45]. Computer Game Review named it Puzzle Game of the Year for 1994[^ref-53].

## Story Summary

The Incredible Machine 2 has no plot. The puzzles are presented by the host character Professor Tim (called "Dr. Tim" in PC Gamer's review)[^ref-19][^ref-11]. The manual opens in his voice: "Professor Tim here. Welcome to my subterranean machine-making lab. We've got gazillions of gadgets lying around this place"[^ref-11]. He also explains that the puzzles are "all built out of stuff I had lying around the lab"[^ref-11].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Players start by signing in to Professor Tim's "Inventor's Registry", which keeps each player's solved puzzles separate[^ref-11]. Hardcore Gaming 101 notes that this replaced the original game's password system: progress is now saved to a player profile[^ref-9]. In Puzzle Play, parts are picked from a Parts Bin and set down on the playfield, and a green flag starts the machine[^ref-11]. A broom button sweeps the player's placed parts back into the bin, and a lightbulb button shows Professor Tim's own solution[^ref-11].

A hint mode puts pointing hands on the puzzle, and clicking one gives a one-sentence hint from Professor Tim. Using hints carries no penalty, but they stop appearing around the end of the Medium difficulty[^ref-9]. Computer Gaming World likewise found that the online hints give "much-needed advice for many of the puzzles, although the hardest seem to have left this option out"[^ref-46]. Sierra's preview described this as a new "automatic hint" feature in which Professor Tim points out areas of the puzzle where a part might work[^ref-49]. PC Gamer also praised the game's flexibility: "You can change the music, the game screen, or the puzzles themselves"[^ref-19].

### Structure and Progression

The manual promises "150-plus puzzles", ranging "from Tutorial Puzzles (which teach you how to use various parts) to Really Hard puzzles — which can be mind-numbingly nasty!"[^ref-11]. PC Gamer counted "over 150" puzzles[^ref-19]. Unlike the original, which kept a running point total, "TIM2 dispenses entirely with the scoring system", according to Computer Gaming World[^ref-46]. Hardcore Gaming 101 lists five difficulty groups (Tutorial, Easy, Medium, Hard and Really Hard) and says puzzles can be played in any order[^ref-9]. Besides Puzzle Play, the main menu offers a Guided Tour, Professor Tim's Workshop for changing or building puzzles, a puzzle-building tutorial walk-through, and Head-to-Head play[^ref-11].

Head-to-Head lets players "hold puzzle-solving contests with a pal"[^ref-11]. Unlike the series' earlier head-to-head mode, it can be played on any puzzle, not just dedicated two-player ones[^ref-9]. MobyGames lists 1–2 offline players on the same screen[^ref-34]. Computer Gaming World described the two-player game as "strictly a head-to-head one-computer match": players take turns against a time limit, and each turn they may add one new part from the bin and move or remove one old one[^ref-46]. Sierra's preview said the game included twenty head-to-head puzzles, which could be played cooperatively or competitively, with a handicap that sets each player's time limit[^ref-49].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

PC Gamer summarised the basic premise: "Fans blow air, for example, while balloons float, balls bounce (most of the time), and conveyor belts, well, they convey"[^ref-19]. MobyGames' description lists parts "including cats, super balls, lava lamps, flashlights, can openers, monkeys on treadmills"[^ref-34]. Computer Gaming World counted "over a hundred pieces", among them lasers and phasers, balloons and blimps, nitroglycerine, remote-control bombs and antigrav pads[^ref-46]. According to the manual, "There are twelve programmable parts in the game", and a computer-icon handle lets the player "set the quantity, appearance, behavior, or state of the selected part"[^ref-11]. Sierra's Holiday 1994 InterAction gave the same count: "A dozen of the new parts are programmable"[^ref-50]. A separate Parts Bin is "devoted entirely to background goodies", scenery such as mountains, the sun, clouds and trees[^ref-50][^ref-49].

Puzzles carry their own gravity and atmospheric pressure settings. In the manual's words, "The greater the gravity, the faster most things will fall. The greater the pressure, the slower most things will move through the atmosphere"[^ref-11]. These settings can only be adjusted in Professor Tim's Workshop[^ref-11]. The Workshop also improved the series' freeform mode: players can build fully playable puzzles by setting both the parts and the conditions under which the puzzle counts as "solved"[^ref-44]. They can also lock parts in place and add hints[^ref-11][^ref-9]. Computer Gaming World noted that the editor now lets the designer define when a puzzle counts as "solved", with "no more 'honor system'"[^ref-46]. Wikipedia calls this version "the biggest addition to the series" in gameplay terms[^ref-44].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| PC Gamer US (January 1995) | 90% | Gary Meredith: "a game that's as rewarding as it is challenging"[^ref-19] |
| PC Player (Germany, 1/95) | 82% | Michael Kim[^ref-55] |
| Power Play (Germany, 2/95) | 85% | Peter Schwindt[^ref-55] |
| Computer Game Review (February 1995) | Puzzle Game of the Year | Best of 1994, ahead of Oxyd magnum! and JigSoft[^ref-53] |
| PC Joker (Germany, 3/95) | 73% | Manfred Duy[^ref-55] |
| Computer Gaming World #129 (April 1995) | 4.5/5 | Jason Kapalka[^ref-46] |
| Computer Gaming World #143 (June 1996) | Readers' Choice | Classics/Puzzles, 1996 Premier Awards[^ref-45] |

In PC Gamer, Gary Meredith listed the game's highs as "Dozens of clever puzzles; a do-it-yourself mode and near open-ended game play". He found no real lows: "None, really, unless you consider a somewhat sensitive cursor and a bit of hunger for base memory to be fatal flaws." His bottom line was "How often can you say you really got what you paid for?"[^ref-19]. In Computer Gaming World's 1996 Premier Awards, the Classics/Puzzles section printed "Readers' Choice: The Incredible Machine 2, Sierra"[^ref-45]. Wikipedia notes that the game won although it was not among the editors' nominees[^ref-44]. MobyGames also records a Power Play award (issue 02/1996) for Best Puzzle Game of 1995[^ref-34].

In Computer Gaming World, Jason Kapalka called the changes "more evolutionary than revolutionary" and said "the single biggest change is in the graphics and animation"[^ref-46]. The magazine's verdict box listed the pros as "Great graphics, more parts, more puzzles, a better editor, clever and absorbing gameplay"; among the cons, "The two player competitive mode could have been better. And where the heck are Sid and Al?"[^ref-46]. The magazine's March 1995 new-products column praised its "outstanding soundtrack", and one editor suggested it be renamed "The Incredible Time Sucking Machine"[^ref-47].

Computer Game Review's Puzzle Game of the Year write-up noted that this was "the third year in a row that a game from the Incredible Machine series has taken the top spot", and praised the fact that "the puzzle solutions were not unique"[^ref-53]. In Germany, PC Player gave it 82%, Power Play 85% and PC Joker 73%; Power Play and PC Joker both rated its graphics lower than its sound (40% and 52%)[^ref-55].

### Modern Assessment

On MobyGames the game had a Moby Score of 8.0 in October 2026 (read via the MobyGames API); the site's January 2026 page showed critic reviews averaging 79% across 13 ratings[^ref-34]. Jimmy Maher's The Digital Antiquarian groups it with The Incredible Toon Machine as sequels that "were just what they sounded like they would be", "the very definition of 'more of the same'"[^ref-56]. Hardcore Gaming 101 calls it "a complete and total improvement on the first game, fixing every nitpick and adding a few new features, too"[^ref-9].

## Development

### Production

MobyGames credits Jeffrey Tunnell as producer of the DOS version. Its designers were Brian Hahn, Steven D. Letsom, Richard Rayl, Kevin Ryan, Jeffrey Tunnell and Richard Tunnell, with Letsom, Rayl and Ryan as software engineers. Brian Hahn was art director and Michael Waite was writer[^ref-34]. The music is credited to Timothy Steven Clarke, Randy Dersham, Jan Paul Moorhead and Ken Rogers[^ref-34].

PC Gamer listed the DOS version's requirements as a 386SX, 2MB RAM, DOS 5.0 or later, 4.5MB of hard disk space, VGA and a mouse. It recommended a 486SX with 4MB RAM[^ref-19]. The DOS release shipped with a disk labelled "Easy Start". The installer could make it into a bootable floppy that starts the game without loading unneeded TSR programs, to help with memory problems[^ref-11].

### Release

Sierra's Fall 1994 InterAction said the game was "due to hit the stores the end of September"[^ref-49]. It slipped: Computer Gaming World's November 1994 "Pipeline" table of estimated release dates listed it for 11/94[^ref-48], and Computer Game Review's December 1994 issue still said it was "due for release in the very near future"[^ref-54]. Computer Gaming World's April 1995 review listed it at $44.95 on floppy and $49.95 on CD-ROM, with no copy protection[^ref-46]. By spring 1995 Sierra was offering extra levels for download, saying "There are dozens of new levels available"[^ref-51], and its Fall 1995 catalogue still sold it with "over 100 custom parts" for DOS on disk and on a DOS/Windows/Mac CD[^ref-52].

## Legacy

**Series background.** The Incredible Machine 2 follows [[1992 - The Incredible Machine]] and its follow-up, The Even More Incredible Machine. PC Gamer's review said the original "brought budding Rube Goldbergs out of the woodwork in droves"[^ref-19]. When PushButton Labs acquired the series rights in 2009, Jeff Tunnell said, "TIM was a genre defining franchise"[^ref-32].

Wikipedia notes that later re-releases of the series, including the 2009 compilation The Incredible Machine Mega Pack, used Version 3.0[^ref-44].
