# The Incredible Machine

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Incredible Machine is a puzzle game in which players create Rube Goldberg machines, arranging collections of objects in a complex fashion to perform a simple task such as "put the ball into a box" or "start a mixer and turn on a fan"[^ref-1]. It was made by Jeff Tunnell Productions, the small studio Jeff Tunnell set up next door to Dynamix in early 1992, with Kevin Ryan programming[^ref-17]. Sierra On-Line published it, and the game came out at the very end of 1992: Sierra Chest gives a first release of November 19, 1992, and The Digital Antiquarian gives December 1, 1992[^ref-13][^ref-17]. (Wikipedia dates the DOS release to 1993[^ref-1].)

The game challenges players to build elaborate contraptions using a variety of objects, all governed by real-world physics[^ref-4]. Each object behaves according to real-world physics, adding an extra layer of complexity[^ref-5]. The series' engine does not use a random number generator in its physics simulation, so the results for any given machine are deterministic[^ref-38].

Computer Gaming World's April 1993 review said that in the game "the curious, tinkering 10-year-old is re-awakened, given a digital toy box and set loose in the backyard of his or her mind"[^ref-1]. In 1996 the magazine named it the 62nd best game ever, calling it "fresh in concept and long on gameplay"[^ref-1].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls
Parts are dragged from a Parts Bin onto the play screen, where small "handles" next to each object let the player resize it, flip it or send it back to the bin; belts and ropes are attached by stretching a line between two parts, and electrical parts are plugged in by moving them next to an outlet[^ref-9]. The machine is started by clicking a runner-in-starting-blocks icon[^ref-9]. On the keyboard, X and Y flip a shape, + and − resize it, and keys 1-9 and a-g change the music in Freeform Mode[^ref-42]. MobyGames classifies the interface as point and select, with a side-view, fixed/flip-screen presentation[^ref-8].

The game's copy protection is built from its own parts: after the intro, players must click, in order, the three parts pictured at the bottom of a given manual page[^ref-9].

One forum user remembered it this way: "Well its a Puzzle Game where you must add little gadgets like a plank to a wall so that the little kitty cant get to the fishbowl and you must put a trampoline under a Basketball to let it bounce and go into the Net"[^ref-43].

### Structure and Progression
The game has a total of 87 premade puzzles that get harder as they go, and puzzles 1 to 21 are tutorial puzzles[^ref-13]. (Computer Gaming World's 1993 review spoke of 80 puzzles[^ref-1].) Preset puzzles must be solved in order, and each solved puzzle gives a password for later access[^ref-9].

In Free-Form Mode players can build any machine they can imagine, save it and load it again, and adjust gravity (from weightlessness to Saturn-strength) and air pressure (from deep space to the ocean floor)[^ref-9]. Saved machines are written to disk[^ref-8]. According to a commenter on The Digital Antiquarian who interviewed Kevin Ryan, the freeform mode was a late addition, made when the team realised its puzzle-editing tools could be cleaned up for players with almost no effort[^ref-17].

### Puzzles and Mechanics
Each puzzle has a simple objective, such as "put the baseball into the basket" or "turn on the fan", and the player is given parts such as balls, girders, rope, balloons, seesaws, cats or monkeys[^ref-8]. Some puzzles use different gravity or air pressure from that of Earth[^ref-8]. The experience of working through the stages of a solution, getting a little closer each time, is "almost indescribably satisfying for anyone with the slightest hint of a tinkering spirit"[^ref-17].

The game's own description pitched it this way: "Want to build a better mousetrap? All it takes are bike-riding monkeys, treadmill mice and a few bowling balls. Genius and junk combine to solve the convoluted contraptions in The Incredible Machine."[^ref-3]

### Modern Assessment

The game sold over 800,000 units[^ref-1]. On release it was nearly Dynamix's best-selling game to date, beaten only by *Aces of the Pacific*, which came out days earlier and sold a few more units but cost far more to make[^ref-17]. Computer Gaming World named it the 62nd best game ever in 1996[^ref-1].

MyAbandonware users rate it 4.39/5 from 705 votes[^ref-22], and IMDb users rate it 8.4/10 from 82 ratings[^ref-45]. AbandonwareDOS lists it as selected for *1001 Video Games You Must Play Before You Die*[^ref-2].

The Digital Antiquarian notes that the game "wasn't explicitly pitched as an educational product, but, like a lot of Sierra's releases during this period, it nevertheless had something of an educational — or at least edutational — aura"[^ref-17].

## Development

### Origins
Jeff Tunnell stepped down as head of Dynamix to found Jeff Tunnell Productions in early 1992. He wanted to return to "the guerrilla model of game development", so he recruited Kevin Ryan, a programmer who had been at Dynamix almost from the beginning, and set up shop in the office next door[^ref-17]. The idea of a machine-construction game went back to 1983, when Tunnell and Damon Slye batted it around while first forming Dynamix[^ref-17]. Wikipedia adds that it was originally going to be developed by Electronic Arts for the Commodore 64 in 1984, and that work did not start until the spring of 1992[^ref-1].

Ryan later described his part in a comment on The Digital Antiquarian: "Jeff presented the concept to me, but he didn't do the design except for feedback as dev went along." He went to the University of Oregon library to study Rube Goldberg books, and said *Lemmings* influenced the game's progression; the character Mel is "Lem" spelled backward[^ref-17].

### Production
Kevin Ryan programmed The Incredible Machine in nine months on a $36,000 budget[^ref-1]. Ryan drew on the coding expertise, and even the actual code, from the vehicular simulators he had built at Dynamix, and had the basic engine working within a handful of months[^ref-17]. Ryan has written that the physics engine used integers "because floating point just wasn't fast enough back then"[^ref-39]. The finished game fit on a single floppy disk[^ref-17].

### Technical Achievements
PCGamingWiki lists the DOS minimum requirements as DOS 3.10, an Intel 386SX, 640 KB of RAM and VGA graphics[^ref-30]. MobyGames gives an Intel 80286, DOS 3.0 to 3.3 and 640 KB of RAM as the minimum, on 3.5" floppy disk[^ref-46]. The game simulates not only collisions between objects but also gravity and air pressure, which players can change in Free-Form Mode[^ref-9][^ref-38].

A related patent, US 5,577,185 ("Computerized puzzle gaming method and apparatus"), was filed by Dynamix in November 1994 and names Jeffrey A. Tunnell and Christopher M. Cole as inventors[^ref-41]. Wikipedia credits the patent to the follow-up *Sid & Al's Incredible Toons* rather than to this game[^ref-1].

### Music and Sound
Christopher Stevens is credited as Music Director on the DOS version[^ref-32]. MobyGames lists support for the PC speaker, Sound Blaster, Pro Audio Spectrum, Roland MT-32 (and LAPC-I) and Thunderboard[^ref-46]. In Freeform Mode, keys 1-9 and a-g switch between music tracks[^ref-42].

## Legacy

The Incredible Machine started a series[^ref-38]:

- **The Even More Incredible Machine** (1993) – an extended version with 160 levels, about twice the original's number, and more parts[^ref-1]
- **Sid & Al's Incredible Toons** (1993)[^ref-38]
- **The Incredible Machine 2** (1994)[^ref-38]
- **The Incredible Machine Version 3.0** (1995)[^ref-38]
- **Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions** (2000)[^ref-38]
- **The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions** (2001)[^ref-38]
- **The Incredible Machine** (2011, iPad)[^ref-38]

A planned Xbox Live Arcade game was announced and later cancelled[^ref-38]. PushButton Labs, co-founded by Tunnell, acquired the series in October 2009[^ref-38]. Tunnell said at the time: "TIM was a genre defining franchise and I could not be happier to have the property available to PushButton Labs"[^ref-40].

Contraption Maker, released on Steam on July 7, 2014, is billed as "the spiritual successor to The Incredible Machine from its original designer and programmer"[^ref-37].

### Platform Ports

- **3DO** – reviewed by EGM in June 1994, with "dramatically improved graphics and sound" over the PC version but no mouse support[^ref-1]
- **FM Towns** (April 1994) and **PC-98** (May 26, 1994) – Japanese releases published by Cybelle[^ref-8]
- **Macintosh** – listed by Sierra Chest and PCGamingWiki[^ref-13][^ref-30]

### Collections

The Incredible Machine Mega Pack, released on GOG.com on October 1, 2009, contained The Even More Incredible Machine, The Incredible Machine 3, Contraptions and Even More Contraptions. It did not include this original game[^ref-38], although PCGamingWiki notes that the original's levels are available as part of The Even More Incredible Machine[^ref-30]. The pack has since been delisted: its GOG page now redirects to the store front, and a March 2026 archived copy of the page still exists[^ref-44].
