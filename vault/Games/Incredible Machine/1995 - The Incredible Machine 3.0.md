# The Incredible Machine 3.0

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Incredible Machine 3.0 (also referred to as The Incredible Machine Version 3.0, or TIM3) is a puzzle video game developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] in 1995 for Windows and Macintosh[^ref-21][^ref-22][^ref-28]. Its credits close with "A Jeff Tunnell Production", and PCGamingWiki lists Jeff Tunnell Productions as the developer[^ref-22][^ref-33]. MobyGames records French and German releases titled Les Incroyables Machines du Professeur Tim and Professor Tims verrückte Werkstatt[^ref-21].

It is an enhanced version of The Incredible Machine 2: both games have the same levels, but Version 3.0 has an improved interface, CD music tracks and other additions[^ref-2][^ref-23]. PCGamingWiki describes it as a "Windows re-release of The Incredible Machine 2 CD-ROM version, with enhanced graphics/UI"[^ref-33], and Sierra's own advertising was candid: "The puzzles in TIM 3.0 will look familiar to owners of TIM 2 — 'cause they're the same!", with a rebate offer for TIM 2 owners in the box[^ref-24]. MobyGames catalogues it as the Windows/Mac hybrid release of The Incredible Machine 2[^ref-21].

Like the rest of the series, it challenges players to build Rube Goldberg devices, arranging a given collection of objects in a needlessly complex fashion to perform a simple task[^ref-2]. Sources differ on how to divide the series' design credit: Wikipedia says the games were "designed and coded by Kevin Ryan and produced by Jeff Tunnell", while a 2015 Choicest Games profile says Tunnell "was the designer and producer" but did not program them[^ref-2][^ref-13]. MobyGames' credits list both Ryan and Tunnell among the designers[^ref-22]. By 2000, the series had sold over one million copies[^ref-2].

The 2009 GOG.com compilation's description captures the premise: "Do you remember watching with amazement when those cartoon heroes constructed machines to catch someone or start something? Those very complicated Rube Goldberg-like ones with knives, toasters, anvils etc?"[^ref-15]

## Story Summary

The Incredible Machine 3.0, like its predecessors, has no narrative. Each level contains a machine designed to do a specific task, but parts are missing, and the player must work out where to put what to make the machine work[^ref-21]. Typical goals are simple tasks such as "put the ball into a box" or "start a mixer and turn on a fan"[^ref-2].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Version 3.0 uses the same levels as The Incredible Machine 2 but with an improved interface[^ref-2][^ref-23]. Sierra introduced it as "the definitive game in the series", one that "makes full use of Windows 95 architecture" and "offers 256 colors"[^ref-24]. Players solve each puzzle by arranging a provided set of objects around fixed items that cannot be moved[^ref-2].

The game includes an editor for designing machines from scratch, either just for fun or as puzzles in which another player must fill in the missing parts[^ref-21]. The Incredible Machine 2 had extended the "freeform" mode so that players could define not only the participating parts but also the conditions under which a puzzle counts as solved[^ref-2][^ref-23].

### Structure and Progression

The level set is The Incredible Machine 2's, which introduced new levels, an extended assortment of parts and two-player hotseat play[^ref-2][^ref-23]. Sierra's 1996 product page promised "over 150 different machines" to construct, a "free-form" mode for building puzzles "from hundreds of active parts and background items", and a "head to head" mode for playing against someone else[^ref-28].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The series' engine does not use a random number generator in its physics simulation, so the results for any given machine are deterministic[^ref-2]. The games simulate physical interactions between objects as well as ambient effects such as air pressure and gravity[^ref-2].

Parts range from ropes and pulleys to electrical generators, bowling balls, cats and mice, many with specific interactions: mice run towards nearby cheese, and light placed next to a magnifying glass will ignite wicks[^ref-2]. MobyGames' description lists cats, super balls, lava lamps, flashlights, can openers and monkeys on treadmills among the parts[^ref-21].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

GameRankings showed 70% for The Incredible Machine 3, but its page noted the score was "based on 1 review": Quandary's 3.5 out of 5, dated 06/03/96[^ref-29]. Sierra's Holiday 1995 advertisement quoted Shane Mooney of PC Magazine (August 1995): "...you'll love this latest addition to the Incredible Machine series"[^ref-24].

Reviewing The Incredible Machine 2, whose levels Version 3.0 shares, PC Gamer US's Gary Meredith (January 1995) gave it 90% and wrote: "In every way, TIM2 is an improvement, keeping the best parts of the original yet giving the player more options and flexibility"[^ref-31][^ref-23]. In Computer Gaming World's 1996 Premier Awards (June 1996), the Readers' Choice in the Classics/Puzzles category went to The Incredible Machine 2[^ref-27][^ref-23].

Sierra's Spring 1996 InterAction nonetheless advertised Version 3.0 with the blurbs "Puzzle Game of the Year" (attributed to Computer Gaming World) and a "Seal Of Approval" from the National Parenting Center, and placed it third in its top ten best-selling home productivity and education titles[^ref-26]. The "Puzzle Game of the Year" line is Sierra's own ad copy; the CGW award itself named The Incredible Machine 2[^ref-27].

### Modern Assessment

MobyGames' combined TIM 2/3.0 entry has 57 player ratings and a MobyScore of 8.0[^ref-21]. No dedicated modern critical retrospective of Version 3.0 was found in the sources consulted.

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **GameRankings (Version 3.0):** 70%, based on a single review (Quandary, 3.5/5)[^ref-29]
- **MobyGames (TIM 2 / 3.0 entry):** MobyScore 8.0; 57 player ratings[^ref-21]

## Development

### Origins

Jeff Tunnell, who had co-founded Dynamix in 1984, left in early 1992, after *The Adventures of Willy Beamish*, to form Jeff Tunnell Productions[^ref-35][^ref-36]. He was the designer and producer of the original 1992 game, although he did not program it[^ref-13]. Kevin Ryan programmed the first game[^ref-2].

In November 1996, Tunnell and Christopher M. Cole were granted US patent 5,577,185, "Computerized puzzle gaming method and apparatus", filed in November 1994 and assigned to Dynamix[^ref-34]. The patent's background names The Incredible Machine "and its progeny" as an existing puzzle game, and its detailed description is written around "Toons" puzzles[^ref-34].

### Production

The 1993 through 1995 versions had the same development team[^ref-2]. Jeff Tunnell Productions' last titles, 3-D Ultra Pinball and The Incredible Machine 3.0, were released in 1995, before Dynamix took the studio over later that year[^ref-14].

Sierra's Fall 1995 product release schedule, "Valid as of July 1, 1995", listed "Incredible Machine 3.0" for October on Windows and Macintosh, captioned "The definitive Incredible Machine game"[^ref-25]. By the Holiday 1995 issue of InterAction the game was advertised as "Now Shipping!" on Windows 95, Windows and Mac CD at $34.95[^ref-24], and Sierra's website still listed both CDs at $34.95 in December 1996[^ref-28].

### Technical Issues

The game has 19 songs. When the TIM3 CD-ROM is detected the music plays as CD audio; otherwise the game falls back to a General MIDI version of the same songs[^ref-32]. PCGamingWiki notes that the GOG.com re-release does not include the CD-audio tracks[^ref-33]; a fan patch restores the CD soundtrack, noting that "The Incredible Machine 3 uses exactly the same soundtrack" as The Incredible Machine 2[^ref-12].

## Legacy

### Later Entries

The series continued with Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions (2000), which IGN described as "a vintage blend of educational and entertainment software at their finest" and which won the PC Family award at the 4th Annual Interactive Achievement Awards[^ref-2]. Jeff Tunnell's Contraption Maker (2014) is a spiritual successor to the series, made with Kevin Ryan and Brian Hahn of the original team[^ref-2].

### Collections

- **The Incredible Machine Mega Pack** (GOG.com, October 1, 2009) – includes Even More Incredible Machine, The Incredible Machine 3 (containing all levels from The Incredible Machine 2), Contraptions and Even More Contraptions[^ref-2][^ref-15]. Shacknews reported the pack at $10, describing The Incredible Machine 3 as "Windows compatible The Incredible Machine 2 with new interface and enhanced graphics"[^ref-30]

On October 1, 2009, Jeff Tunnell's PushButton Labs announced it had acquired the rights to The Incredible Machine games[^ref-30]; the rights later passed to Disney after Playdom acquired PushButton Labs[^ref-2].
