# The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions is a puzzle game released in 2001, developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] for Windows, Mac OS and Palm OS.[^ref-1] It contains 250 puzzles, although the 50 tutorial puzzles are identical to those in Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions (2000), whose gameplay it reuses unchanged.[^ref-1] As in the rest of the series, each puzzle asks the player to finish a partially built Rube Goldberg-style chain reaction to achieve a simple goal, "anything from feeding cheese to a mouse to lining up different types of balls in a cage."[^ref-7]

Gamepressure describes it as a direct continuation of Return of the Incredible Machine released a year earlier, "basically a package of additional tasks."[^ref-4] Sierra's product description promised "250 brand-new puzzles, a more entertaining head-to-head mode, the ability to swap puzzles with the world over the Internet, and an updated easy-to-use interface."[^ref-2] The puzzle-sharing service was called WonSwap.[^ref-3]

All Game Guide wrote that "although the game applies properties of physics to problem solving, it is so delightfully entertaining that gamers of all ages won't realize the depth of learning involved."[^ref-5]

## Story Summary

The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions has no traditional narrative. Each puzzle is a self-contained scenario with its own objective, and the objectives and instructions that appear as on-screen text are also read aloud by the game's host.[^ref-7] TV Tropes describes the game's framing device as earning a "Doctorate of Contrapology."[^ref-9]

The scenarios use household objects, animals and outlandish devices. GameSpot listed parts such as bricks, bowling balls, rope, pulleys, lasers, timers, miniature blimps, explosives, toasters and fans, along with "an ape-powered motor, a mouse-powered motor, an alligator, and a miniature person."[^ref-7] TV Tropes singles out one Even More Contraptions puzzle in which the player must lure a mouse out of its home with cheese in order to feed it to an alligator.[^ref-9]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The Incredible Machine: Even More Contraptions is controlled with keyboard and mouse.[^ref-1] Players place the limited set of parts each puzzle provides into a partially assembled contraption, then press the start button to see whether the chain reaction works; there are no penalties for failed attempts.[^ref-7] GameSpot called the point-and-click interface intuitive.[^ref-7] PC Zone found part selection fiddly, noting that the cursor "won't always be the piece you want", a problem it said also affected the previous game.[^ref-38]

Hardcore Gaming 101 notes that the Contraptions games split the parts into categories in freeform mode and give every part a help button that shows a close-up and explains how it works.[^ref-11]

### Structure and Progression

The game contains 250 puzzles in total, the first 50 of them tutorial puzzles carried over from Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions.[^ref-1] PC Zone counted "210 levels plus a helpful 50 tutorials, playable in any order", adding that easy and medium "can be completed with no problems in a day or so" while the difficult and expert levels take longer.[^ref-38] The speedrun.com leaderboard lists Tutorial, Easy, Medium, Difficult, Expert and Two Player categories.[^ref-14]

GameSpot noted that the game "includes hints for all but the most difficult puzzles", along with a complete tutorial, "more than 60 head-to-head puzzles" and a puzzle construction set.[^ref-7] Lord_Seth's GameFAQs guide calls the game "quite a bit HARDER than The Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions."[^ref-13]

Earning the title of Doctor of Contrapology, which requires solving every contraption, is a speedrun.com category; its record as of October 2026 is 2 hours, 54 minutes and 39 seconds, set by thebagler5 on May 28, 2026.[^ref-14]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

GameSpot counted "more than 100 machine parts", ranging from simple items to complex ones and humorous ones.[^ref-7] Old-Games.com mentions levers, pulleys, candles and walls "as well as even stranger items like cheese, lasers, and anti-gravity pads."[^ref-16]

GameSpot wrote that "most puzzles can be solved in multiple ways, and even incorrect solutions are often entertaining," and that failed attempts often give clues to the correct solution.[^ref-7] PC Zone was more measured, saying there is "(slight) room to manoeuvre" and that players sometimes don't need to use the same pieces in the same order as the official solution.[^ref-38] The series' physics engine uses no random number generator, so a given machine always produces the same result.[^ref-3]

Some solutions demand precise placement. One forum player advised that "sometimes it's just a matter of making sure every single thing is in exactly the right place."[^ref-18]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Contemporary reviews were generally positive, though critics noted that the game added puzzles rather than new features.

GameSpot's Trey Walker gave the game 7.6 out of 10 in October 2001, calling it "an excellent puzzle game that features humorous Rube Goldberg-style machines" that "is sure to please amateur inventors and puzzle game fans alike."[^ref-7] He noted that "while the game's two-dimensional graphics won't impress players who are used to the latest 3D technology, they do a good job of representing the contraptions in a colorful cartoon style."[^ref-7]

PC Zone's James Lyon (issue 106, September 2001) was cooler, writing that "it is also an add-on pack to an earlier title" and that "there's nothing intrinsically new in here." He said the game was "probably only worth 47 per cent if you've already got its predecessor," but that for newcomers "its fiendish puzzles should keep you gripped for a good few weeks."[^ref-38]

Metacritic lists All Game Guide's score of 90 and GameZone's 80. GameZone called it "a wonderful program that will challenge players to use their brains in ways that involve both logic and creativity."[^ref-5]

Children's Software Revue (2001) wrote: "Our testers, who ranged in age from seven to 43, all loved the program."[^ref-2]

### Modern Assessment

Hardcore Gaming 101's series retrospective says that "Even More Contraptions is pretty much an expansion pack to ROTIM, but except for the first 50 tutorial puzzles from the original, the puzzles are brand-new," adding that there are no new parts or graphics.[^ref-11]

Players have left nostalgic comments on MyAbandonware, including "This game was my LIFE as a kid" and "I Loved this game as a kid, me and a friend both had a copy at one time, the two of us would spend hours putting together intricate contraptions."[^ref-22]

## Development

### Origins

The Incredible Machine series began in 1992 as a collaboration between designer [[Kevin Ryan]] and [[Jeff Tunnell]]'s team at Dynamix, on a budget of $36,000.[^ref-15] Inspired by Rube Goldberg's cartoons of absurdly complicated machines, the series went on to release seven more titles over nine years.[^ref-9]

Even More Contraptions was a direct continuation of Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions, released a year earlier in 2000.[^ref-4] Kevin Ryan and Jeffrey Tunnell are both credited with additional design on Even More Contraptions.[^ref-1]

### Production

The game was developed by Dynamix, Inc. and published by Sierra.[^ref-1] Its gameplay is identical to Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions; of its 250 puzzles, the 50 tutorial puzzles are carried over from that game.[^ref-1] MobyGames credits Score! Music & Sound Design for the music, and a MobyGames player review notes that it includes music from The Even More Incredible Machine and The Incredible Machine version 3.0.[^ref-1]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-1]
- **Designer/Art Director:** Brian Hahn
- **Contraption Design:** Neil Witkin, Jared W. Eden, Richard Tunnell, Garrett Turner
- **Additional Design:** includes Kevin Ryan and Jeffrey Tunnell
- **Original Series Designer:** Kevin Ryan[^ref-15]

### Technical Achievements

Like Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions, the game's parts are 3D-rendered; Hardcore Gaming 101 notes that Even More Contraptions adds no new parts or graphics.[^ref-11] The Windows version uses DirectDraw and runs at 800x600 only.[^ref-27]

The game introduced the WonSwap service, allowing players to share homemade puzzles over the Internet.[^ref-3]

### Technical Specifications

**Windows Version:**[^ref-27]
- **Resolution:** 800x600 only
- **Audio:** General MIDI; digital (sampled) or MIDI (synthesized) soundtrack selectable
- **API:** DirectDraw; DirectX 8 required
- **Frame Rate:** Less than 30 FPS
- **Multiplayer:** 2-player hot seat

**Windows System Requirements:**[^ref-27]
- **OS:** Windows 95/98/2000
- **Processor:** Intel Pentium 90 MHz minimum, Pentium 166 MHz recommended
- **RAM:** 32 MB minimum, 64 MB recommended
- **Storage:** 40 MB minimum, 300 MB recommended
- **CD-ROM:** 4X speed[^ref-1]

**Macintosh Version:**[^ref-29]
- **Resolution:** 800x600, 16-bit color
- **OS:** Mac OS 8.6, Mac OS X or higher
- **Processor:** PowerPC 120 MHz
- **RAM:** 32 MB
- **Storage:** 50 MB hard drive space
- **Additional:** CarbonLib 1.2, QuickTime 4.1

- Some Amazon customer reviewers report that the game did not work on newer Apple systems, while running under Windows 7 and Windows 8.[^ref-2]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The game features several holiday-themed special items that appear when the computer's system date matches specific holidays:[^ref-34]

- **Halloween (October 31):** A bat appears when building contraptions, flying around when hit with a ball. A Jack-O-Lantern part also becomes available that can be lit with matches, lasers, or ignition devices and scares cats.[^ref-34]
- **Christmas Day:** A Santa Claus lamp appears, functioning like a lava lamp but shaped like Santa.[^ref-34]
- **St. Patrick's Day:** A Leprechaun appears when building contraptions, dancing when hit with a ball.[^ref-34]
- **Valentine's Day:** A Cupid part unlocks, flying straight like a blimp and capable of popping things with its arrow.[^ref-34]

Additional trivia:
- TV Tropes observes that across the series "fundamental laws are broken right and left"; for example, a solar panel can be activated by any light source, from a lit candle to a flashlight.[^ref-9]
- A cheat allows instant victory: pressing 'V' at any time while the puzzle is running completes it immediately.[^ref-35]
- A password unlocks a level select.[^ref-35]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

By 2000, the Incredible Machine series had sold over one million copies.[^ref-3] Its predecessor, Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions, received the "PC Family" award at the 4th Annual Interactive Achievement Awards.[^ref-3] The original game was ranked #62 in Computer Gaming World's "150 Best Games of All Time" list in 1996, was inducted into its Hall of Fame in 2006, and appears in "1001 Video Games You Must Play Before You Die."[^ref-36]

Even More Contraptions was named 2001 Puzzle Game of the Year in a Game Industry News readers' vote.[^ref-1] The next game in the series was The Incredible Machine for iOS, published by Disney in 2011.[^ref-37]

### Collections

- **The Incredible Machine Mega Pack (2009):** A digital compilation released October 1, 2009; MobyGames lists PushButton Labs and Disney Interactive as publishers,[^ref-31] and Wikipedia says it was released on GOG.com.[^ref-3] Wikipedia lists its contents as The Even More Incredible Machine, The Incredible Machine 3 (which contains all levels from The Incredible Machine 2), Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions, and Even More Contraptions.[^ref-3] The GOG release included a 37-page manual and the soundtrack as bonus content.[^ref-32]
- **Hoyle Puzzle Games (2005):** Even More Contraptions was included as a bonus game.[^ref-3]

### Spiritual Successor and Later Releases

**Contraption Maker (2014):** A spiritual successor developed by Spotkin. Jeff Tunnell wrote on the game's blog that Kevin Ryan and Brian Hahn were both working on the project at Spotkin.[^ref-39] Tunnell said on the game's dev blog: "Working on Contraption Maker with modern processors and GPUs, huge memory, connectivity, and high definition monitors is like being in a dream world."[^ref-37] In a Reddit post, Kevin Ryan, who described himself as having been "designing and programming games for almost 5 decades", listed over 200 parts and critters, 196 puzzles plus 54 tutorial puzzles, and online building "with up to 7 other players."[^ref-25]

**The Incredible Machine (iOS, 2011):** Published by Disney, then quietly pulled from the iOS store within a year of release.[^ref-37]

**Speedrunning:** Even More Contraptions has a speedrun.com leaderboard; see Structure and Progression above.[^ref-14]

### Related Publications

- **Fan Walkthroughs:** Lord_Seth's guide on GameFAQs won FAQ of the Month in August 2005.[^ref-30]
