# Time Zone

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 15, 2026</small>

## Overview

Time Zone, released in February 1982 by On-Line Systems, was one of the most ambitious adventure games yet made for a home computer.[^ref-1][^ref-16] Computer Gaming World had announced it for a January 5 release at $99.95,[^ref-17] and Jimmy Maher dates its shipping to about the beginning of March.[^ref-5] Designed by [[Roberta Williams]], this fifth entry in the Hi-Res Adventures series filled both sides of six floppy disks and, by its manual's count, held approximately 1,300 rooms with over 1,400 hi-res pictures.[^ref-14][^ref-2] Softalk counted thirty-nine separate scenarios, spanning 400 million B.C. to 4082 A.D.[^ref-20] On-Line estimated that it would take most players from six months to a year to solve,[^ref-20] and Computer Gaming World called it "easily the largest game program commercially available."[^ref-16]

The game's premise centers on a player chosen by a terrestrial guardian to secure Earth's future by traveling through time and space to destroy the evil ruler of the distant planet Neburon.[^ref-4][^ref-9] Along the way, players encounter historical and legendary figures including Julius Caesar, Christopher Columbus, Robin Hood, Benjamin Franklin, Napoleon and Cleopatra.[^ref-2][^ref-8] Roberta Williams told Computer Gaming World the project took fourteen months: six months of design on her own, then eight more working with the rest of the team.[^ref-16] The manual lists ten people who worked on it.[^ref-14]

At $99.95, Time Zone was the most expensive game on the market; Softline noted in March 1982 that it had just surpassed Wizardry for that title.[^ref-24] It nonetheless sold well enough to top Softalk's Adventure chart for March 1982 sales,[^ref-19] and BYTE declared, "Without a doubt, it is the longest adventure to date."[^ref-26] In retrospect, Maher calls it "a notorious, high-profile flop," quoting John Williams.[^ref-5]

## Story Summary

The manual's backstory is set in the year 4081, when Earth is a fast-paced, highly technological society.[^ref-14] Its progress has drawn the jealousy of the Neburites, an alien race from the planet Neburon, and their evil ruler Ramadu has built a ray gun aimed at Earth; unless he is stopped, Earth "will never see the year 4082."[^ref-14]

In 1982, the player is out for a stroll near home and comes across a strange pulsating metal cylinder that turns out to be a time machine.[^ref-14] Roberta Williams later described the opening this way: "you're just walking through the trees outside your home, and suddenly there's a time machine."[^ref-6] In the game's opening, a terrestrial guardian, or keeper, has chosen the player to secure Earth's future by destroying the evil ruler of Neburon, and grants the ability to travel through time and space.[^ref-4][^ref-9]

The player visits seven continents in nine time periods,[^ref-14] from the Prehistoric Era of 400,000,000 B.C. through 10,000 B.C., 50 B.C., 1000, 1400, 1700 and 2082 A.D., before the final assault on Neburon in 4082 A.D.[^ref-14][^ref-4] Along the way the player meets historical figures and gathers the items needed to defeat Ramadu.[^ref-2]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Time Zone uses a two-word parser typical of early 1980s illustrated adventures.[^ref-1] The screen is split between a hi-res picture and four lines of text, and players type commands of the form verb-noun, such as "GET KEY".[^ref-3] Players can switch between graphic and text modes.[^ref-8] According to Roberta Williams, Time Zone's pictures draw about three times faster than those in On-Line's earlier games, and the inventory is kept in memory, so it no longer requires a disk access.[^ref-16][^ref-14]

### Structure and Progression

Each place and time period is handled as a game within itself, and the manual likens playing Time Zone to "thirty full sized regular adventure games."[^ref-14] One walkthrough author counts "well over 1,000 different locations spread over 39 time zones."[^ref-9] Inside the time machine, the player picks a destination and an era and pulls a lever to travel.[^ref-7] Solutions often require objects gathered in other eras, which makes the game a vast scavenger hunt.[^ref-6][^ref-2] The time periods and locations include:

- **400,000,000 B.C. (Prehistoric Era):** A lake and a Tyrannosaurus near the shore[^ref-6][^ref-14]
- **10,000 B.C. (Stone Age):** A cave where the player must make a fire[^ref-14][^ref-7]
- **50 B.C.:** Cleopatra in Africa and the Roman Senate in Europe[^ref-9]
- **1000 A.D.:** Robin Hood in Europe[^ref-9]
- **1400 A.D.:** Signing on to Columbus's *Santa Maria* in Europe[^ref-9]
- **1700 A.D.:** Benjamin Franklin's print shop in colonial North America[^ref-6][^ref-7]
- **2082 A.D.:** Future cities, including a train in Asia[^ref-7][^ref-14]
- **4082 A.D.:** The planet Neburon, which takes up two disk sides by itself[^ref-14][^ref-20]

Each location has its own artwork.[^ref-5] Because of the game's difficulty, On-Line offered hints by telephone: the rulebook promised hints from May 1982, and Roberta said the company would answer specific questions from players who called, though it would not publish hint sheets.[^ref-16] Softalk reported that Howard Luthy had become the official hint-giver for Time Zone.[^ref-19]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The gameplay revolves around exploration, item collection and puzzle-solving across multiple time periods. One walkthrough author calls Time Zone "a well written game that is enjoyable to play through," provided players know which locations to visit and which objects to use.[^ref-9] The same author warns that the game "must be completed in a very strict order," because important objects can vanish if carried to the wrong era.[^ref-9] The manual explains that an item cannot be taken back to a time before it was invented.[^ref-14]

Saving requires a separate disk initialized from the game's menu or with the INIT DISK command.[^ref-14][^ref-8] One modern player using an emulator noted that there is "no need to constantly swap floppies and wait for the drive to read the data."[^ref-10]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Softalk reported in April 1982 that the game "was generally favorably received despite its high price and the well-publicized existence of some bugs in the first release version."[^ref-18] Its June 1982 review said that Time Zone "is so different in depth and scope from any other computer game, it prompts the coining of a new term: the microepic."[^ref-20] BYTE wrote in December 1982: "Without a doubt, it is the longest adventure to date with more than 1400 color graphic pictures."[^ref-26]

Electronic Games gave its 1983 Arcade Award (the "Arkie") for Best Computer Adventure to Infocom's Deadline, and gave Time Zone a Certificate of Merit, describing it as "the magnum opus from the folks who are largely responsible for perfecting the hi-res adventure."[^ref-27][^ref-1]

### Sales Charts and Polls

No unit sales figure has been found. Softalk's monthly Adventure charts track the game through 1982:

| Softalk issue | Adventure chart position |
|---------------|--------------------------|
| April 1982 | #5 (new entry)[^ref-18] |
| May 1982 (March sales) | #1[^ref-19] |
| June 1982 | #3[^ref-20] |
| July 1982 (May sales) | #2[^ref-21] |

During the industry downturn reported in the July 1982 issue, Softalk observed that "adventure fans were willing to shell out the $100 necessary for Time Zone."[^ref-21] In Softalk's poll of 1982, published in April 1983, Time Zone ranked 20th in the Top Thirty of 1982 and first in the Adventure Ten.[^ref-23]

### Retrospective Assessment

Later accounts are harsher. Maher writes that Time Zone "became a notorious, high-profile flop, the first such that On-Line had ever released," and quotes John Williams, Roberta's brother-in-law and an On-Line employee: "It frankly wasn't that good."[^ref-5] Wikipedia states that the game sold poorly.[^ref-1] Neither source gives a sales figure, and the Softalk charts above show it near the top of its genre for much of 1982.

Blogger Jason Dyer, who finished the game in 24 hours of play, found it easy next to some contemporaries but full of red herrings. He wrote that the game asked, "what if you really could go anywhere at any time?" and tried to answer it.[^ref-6]

- **MobyGames Critics:** 46% (2 ratings)[^ref-2]

## Development

### Origins

According to Maher, Roberta Williams began planning the game in early 1981. She wanted a treasure hunt through time and space, ending on an alien planet in the far future.[^ref-5] By the time she gave Ken the complete design that summer, it had grown from an estimated five or six disk sides to twelve.[^ref-5] Most games of the period rarely took up more than one side of a floppy disk.[^ref-3]

Taking into account the sheer quantity of locations, On-Line priced the game at $99.95. Maher writes that this was "about twice what anyone had dared to charge for even the most ambitious of computer games before," and that, adjusted for inflation, Time Zone is "still quite possibly the most expensive videogame ever released."[^ref-5]

### Production

Roberta Williams told Computer Gaming World that she spent six months on the design by herself, working three or four hours a day, and then worked with the others on and off for eight more months: "It was fourteen months before it was finally done."[^ref-16] The manual lists ten people who worked on the game: Roberta and Ken Williams, Bob Davis, Rorke Weigandt, Jeff Stephenson, Warren Schwader, Eric Griswold, Terry Pierce, Barry Blosser and Michelle Pritchard.[^ref-14] Roberta clarified that some of the ten were not full-time; Ken, for example, had written the original interpreter that Jeff Stephenson reworked for Time Zone.[^ref-16]

Maher argues that the team was probably the largest yet assembled to make a computer game, and that it marked a step toward the modern model in which designers, programmers and artists are separate specialists.[^ref-5] He describes Terry Pierce, an 18-year-old hired out of the local high school, drawing the pictures in pencil on graph paper for others to trace into the computer.[^ref-5] In Maher's account, the project "started to go off the rails pretty quickly" under coordinator Bob Davis, until [[Jeff Stephenson]] arrived as co-head; Time Zone was Stephenson's first assignment at On-Line.[^ref-5] Softalk reported that the game first filled twenty-two disks before new algorithms "crammed" it onto six double-sided disks.[^ref-20]

Roberta Williams later reflected on the scope of the undertaking: "Once we got into it and saw how big a job it was, we were almost sorry we started it in the first place."[^ref-16] She added that the project "put a lot of strain on our company."[^ref-16]

### Technical Achievements

Time Zone used On-Line's ADL (Adventure Development Language) engine, which powered the Hi-Res Adventures series.[^ref-1] According to the manual, fitting the game into memory "required a complete restructuring of our adventure programming procedures," including new graphics routines and a new way to move information from one scenario disk to another.[^ref-14] Warren Schwader's new color-fill routines put color on screen about three times faster than in On-Line's other adventures.[^ref-14] Moving to a new region triggers an "information transfer" delay of about ten seconds.[^ref-14]

The game shipped on six double-sided floppy disks, twelve disk sides in total.[^ref-12] The disks are differentiated by volume number, and players must swap disks as they travel between regions.[^ref-12][^ref-14]

### Technical Issues

Players running Time Zone in emulators have reported trouble with the save function. The game needs a specially initialized save disk, and one forum user reported difficulty getting the virtual save game to work.[^ref-13] The .dsk container format requires substantial modification to work, though .woz files work.[^ref-12]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Profanity Protection:** A MobyGames player review notes that typing a swear word kicks the player out of the game entirely.[^ref-2]
- **Geographical Error:** Roberta Williams placed a rhea in Australia instead of South America after seeing the birds in the Australian area of the Los Angeles Zoo. By the time the mistake was found the game was almost ready to ship, so a disclaimer was added to the documentation instead.[^ref-16][^ref-2]
- **Swimming Easter Egg:** In the Prehistoric Era lake, the game asks "CAN YOU SWIM?" and keeps repeating the question until the player types YES, which lets them swim ashore.[^ref-6]
- **Historical Poster:** The promotional poster in one preserved copy was probably taken from Softalk magazine, volume 2 number 3 (November 1981), rather than shipped in the box.[^ref-12]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Time Zone's commercial record is disputed. Contemporary charts show it selling strongly among adventure games in 1982: Softalk ranked it first for March sales and still second for May, and Softalk's 1982 poll placed it 20th among all programs of the year.[^ref-19][^ref-21][^ref-23] Retrospective accounts call it a failure. Maher calls it a "flop,"[^ref-5] and one walkthrough author writes that it "did not sell well when it was first released due to a very high retail price of $99," and that it had lost much of its popularity by the time the price came down.[^ref-9] No sales figures have been published to settle the question.

### Collections

The IF-Legends archive says Time Zone was "probably rereleased by SierraVenture in 1982."[^ref-4] Softalk's December 1982 catalog still listed it at $99.95.[^ref-22] The game was later included as an Apple II disk image in *The Roberta Williams Anthology* (1996).[^ref-2][^ref-4]

### Fan Projects

In 1982, players on The Source online service started the "Vault of Ages," a group project to map Time Zone completely.[^ref-25] Today the game is preserved through archival copies at the Internet Archive, including WOZ disk images.[^ref-12][^ref-11] It can be played through Apple II emulation.[^ref-13]

### Related Publications

- **Time Zone Manual:** Included in the six-disk package, with instructions and the game's backstory[^ref-14]
- **Computer Gaming World interview:** "Time Zone: An Interview with Roberta Williams," held April 2, 1982, shortly after the game became available[^ref-16]
- **Softalk Magazine:** The November 1981 issue (vol. 2 no. 3) is the likely source of a Time Zone poster found in one preserved box[^ref-12]

### Critical Perspective

Time Zone holds an unusual place in adventure gaming history. Computer Gaming World said in 1982 that a game of its size "must, because of sheer size alone, become a landmark in the computer gaming hobby," while asking whether it would become a "classic."[^ref-16] Asked whether it might be "looked back upon at some future time as a classic," Roberta Williams replied, "I hope so. That would be nice." She also hoped it could be used to teach history in schools.[^ref-16]

Modern writers are more divided. Maher sees its team-based production as a step toward modern big-studio development, while judging the game itself "something of a nadir in the annals of adventure-game design."[^ref-5] Dyer found that he "did have genuine fun throughout," red herrings aside.[^ref-6]
