# Adventure in Serenia

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 23, 2026</small>

> [!note] Port stub
> This page covers only the 1982 IBM PC/PCjr release. The game itself (story, Apple II original, other ports, reception and legacy) is documented on **[[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 2 - The Wizard and the Princess]]**.

## Overview

*Adventure in Serenia* is the IBM Personal Computer version of On-Line Systems' second Hi-Res Adventure, [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 2 - The Wizard and the Princess|The Wizard and the Princess]], released in 1982 as a self-booting disk and published by IBM[^ref-6][^ref-12]. It is an IBM PC release, not an Apple II one: *Softalk for the IBM Personal Computer* described it as "a new implementation of Wizard and the Princess, originally marketed by Sierra On-Line for the Apple II"[^ref-33], and Sierra's own 1988 newsletter recorded the original as "Published for the IBM as Adventure in Serenia"[^ref-38]. Its manual introduced it as "the first of On-Line Systems' pictorial adventures for the IBM Personal Computer"[^ref-15][^ref-37], and Wikipedia describes it as Sierra's first game for the PC platform[^ref-6].

The title screen credits the game to Ken and Roberta Williams, carries both "(c) Copyright IBM Corp. 1982" and "(c) Copyright On-Line Systems 1980", and states that it was "Implemented by Jeff Stephenson"[^ref-15][^ref-37]. Softalk's review listing likewise credited it as "Adventure in Serenia, by Ken and Roberta Williams, IBM"[^ref-33]. Apart from the new title, a different 4-colour CGA palette, improved upper- and lower-case text, alternative inventory graphics and some reworded messages, there is little difference from the original game[^ref-14][^ref-21]. *PC Magazine* later listed *Serenia* as "a disk package for the PCjr" among Sierra's titles for IBM's new home computer[^ref-32].

## Story Summary

The IBM manual's introduction sets up the quest directly: "When you load this game on your computer, you are magically transported to the land of Serenia, where the evil Wizard Harlin has made off with the beautiful Princess Priscilla"[^ref-15]. The player must rescue Priscilla from Harlin's castle "beyond the mountains to the North" and return her to the village where the adventure began, starting out with a flask of water, a small knife, a loaf of bread and a blanket[^ref-15].

Some database descriptions say this version was framed as a sequel in which Harlin "uses the sands of time" to undo his defeat[^ref-12]. The 1982 manual does not contain that framing[^ref-15]; the "sands of time" prologue is part of a second introduction that, per the King's Quest Omnipedia, was added to the manuals of later releases[^ref-16].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Commands are typed as "one or two word sentences", usually a verb followed by a noun such as LOOK CHASM or GET STICK; directions can be abbreviated to their first letter[^ref-15]. The upper part of the screen shows a picture of the current location and the bottom four lines carry text[^ref-15]. When a message runs longer than four lines the game beeps and waits for the Scroll Lock key[^ref-15]; Gaming After 40 warned modern players that "access to the Scroll Lock key is absolutely essential"[^ref-9].

Function keys switch between the picture screen and a full text screen (F1), toggle between two colour sets (F2), and enter common commands such as save, restore, inventory and LOOK ROOM[^ref-15].

### Technical Specifications

| Item | Requirement |
|------|-------------|
| Memory | "48K of user memory"[^ref-15][^ref-37]; PCjr Magazine listed "64K of memory" for the PCjr release[^ref-36] |
| Video | Color/Graphics Monitor Adapter (CGA)[^ref-15] |
| Drives | "one diskette drive"; two drives make saving more convenient[^ref-15] |
| Operating system | None needed (self-booting)[^ref-15] |
| Saves | Up to twenty games per save disk, which "must be formatted by the Adventure in Serenia program, and not by DOS"[^ref-15] |

PCGamingWiki lists CGA, CGA composite and Tandy/PCjr video modes for the PC booter release[^ref-19]. The King's Quest Omnipedia describes the two in-game colour schemes, and notes that a composite monitor yields additional blended colours[^ref-14].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The IBM release was reviewed in the period computer press. *Softalk for the IBM Personal Computer* (December 1982, review signed "fj") called it "the first full-color adventure ever implemented for a microcomputer. It is appropriate that it should be the first for the Personal Computer. Though there are shortcomings, in choosing this game to market, IBM is going with a classic"; it listed the game at $35, requiring "48K and color/graphics adapter"[^ref-33]. In *Video and Computer Gaming Illustrated* (June 1983), Dale W. Rupert wrote that "The program is well written, the sound and graphics are very good" and that "If you are new to adventure games, this is a good beginning", though he found that the eighteen-page booklet "offers clues which are more tantalizing than helpful"[^ref-35].

Don Kennedy's "Lost in Serenia" in *PCjr Magazine* (March 1984) was harsher. He found that "The graphics for this game are splashy, use bright colors and are done in cartoon style", but that "many solutions to Adventure in Serenia's problems are the type that need blind luck to work out (or a call to Sierra, of course)", and concluded: "because of the difficulties in solving many of its problems, Adventure in Serenia is not recommended"[^ref-36]. In the 1984 *Whole Earth Software Catalog*, Steven Levy wrote that "this fairy-tale-style program (known to the IBM world as ADVENTURE IN SERENIA) has a sense of whimsy and wonder that has made it a favorite for three years"; the catalog's entry listed the IBM version at $29.95[^ref-39].

### Modern Assessment

In a 2010 retrospective of the IBM version, Gaming After 40 noted that "the game runs in 4-color CGA mode, with very simple PC speaker sound effects", and judged that "the plotting is almost non-existent, few meaningful clues are provided, and many of the puzzles depend on trying random actions until something works"[^ref-9]. The reviewer still credited Roberta Williams as "a pioneer in the graphic adventure genre"[^ref-9]. Abandonware DOS users rate it 3.50/5 (40 votes)[^ref-1].

## Development

### Production

IBM announced the game in October 1982 as "a new game, Adventure in Serenia ($35) by On-Line Systems, Inc.", according to *Creative Computing*'s February 1983 IBM column[^ref-34]. Two fan references, the King's Quest Omnipedia and Wikipedia, say Roberta Williams reputedly disliked this version and thought its colours "atrocious"[^ref-6][^ref-14]; neither cites an original interview or statement, and no period source for the remark has been found, so it should be treated as unconfirmed. Gaming After 40 links IBM's relationship with On-Line Systems to the later development of the AGI engine and the original King's Quest for the PCjr[^ref-9].

The PCjr disk package was reported by *PC Magazine* in its 24 January 1984 PCjr games preview, which noted that it "was not on display"[^ref-32]. Wikipedia gives March 1984 as the PCjr release date[^ref-6], the same month *PCjr Magazine* reviewed "IBM's Adventure in Serenia" as "Available at IBM product centers and authorized dealers"[^ref-36].

## Legacy

When Sierra assembled the King's Quest Collection, it included the Apple II version of *The Wizard and the Princess* instead, explaining in the collection's READ2.WRI file: "That version will not work on today's fast computers, so we have included the Apple version instead"[^ref-14]. The game's wider legacy, including Serenia's return in King's Quest V, is covered on [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 2 - The Wizard and the Princess]].
