# MissionForce: CyberStorm

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

MissionForce: CyberStorm is a turn-based strategy game developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line in 1996[^ref-1]. Set in the Earthsiege/Starsiege universe, the game departed from the first-person mech combat of its predecessors; one FAQ writer said it "gave Earthsiege fans a taste of BattleTech-style combat instead of the first-person sims Sierra had focused the brand on"[^ref-20]. The game challenges players to "command combat in a robot-based universe with a myriad of futuristic weapons and battle systems"[^ref-3].

Phil Salvador describes it as "one of the greatest turn-based strategy games of its time, an absolute milestone for the genre"[^ref-4]. The game combines strategic depth with corporate intrigue, as players take "the part of a hired soldier, and must continually turn a profit or be ousted by the company you're working for"[^ref-5]. CyberStorm was promoted as taking "strategy gaming to a new level, with a detailed and rich turn-based game that uses superb graphics and gameplay to challenge even the most experienced gamer"[^ref-6].

## Story Summary

The game is set in the Earthsiege universe where "you start the game as an Ensign in Unitech, a corporation that achieves power and profit through planetary mining"[^ref-9]. The player serves "as a commander of a private military taskforce to fight a race of mechanical beings mankind created long ago called Cybrids"[^ref-10]. MobyGames' official description sets the premise: "Prometheus is believed destroyed, but the Cybrid menace has not ended"[^ref-7].

The story explores dark themes of corporate exploitation and the dehumanization of warfare. BioDerms, genetically engineered humanoids, "are actually slaves: disposable humanoids to be used on the battlefield, and 'recycled' for a few credits or blown up in kamikaze attacks by corporate officers when they are no longer useful"[^ref-10].

As Salvador notes, the game depicts how "no one can escape the perpetual war machine. Expendable treatment of life begets further mistreatment and the gradual supplanting of human decency by profiteering and self-justification"[^ref-4].

The corporate setting is cold and ruthless: military decisions are controlled by a computer system called the Main Synthetic Intelligence (MSI), and an early communique implies Unitech will execute officers who fail to deliver profits[^ref-1]. BioDerm templates are created from the genetic material of past HERC pilots and commanders, and if the player wins, Unitech makes clear it will do the same with the player's avatar[^ref-1].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

MissionForce: CyberStorm is played from a top-down isometric view on a hexagon-based map[^ref-1]. Players command a squad of mechs called HERCs in a series of random missions against an AI-led army of Cybrids[^ref-12]. HERC is short for HERCULAN, "Humaniform-Emulation Roboticized Combat Unit with Leg-Articulated Navigation"[^ref-1]. The game is controlled with keyboard and mouse[^ref-18].

### Structure and Progression

The game's progression system is built around corporate advancement. "By completing missions and progressing, the player gains rank in order to get access to more advanced equipment and hardware, as well as being able to control more HERCs"[^ref-13][^ref-1]. Rank rises through 14 stages, from Ensign to Herc Commander[^ref-15]. Missions can be selected from a list of Cybrid-controlled planets within a specified system, and "all maps and enemy locations are randomly generated"[^ref-1].

The game's itch.io patch page lists an average session length of "about a half-hour"[^ref-18]. MobyGames' description cites "hundreds of different weapons" and "over a dozen different HERC models to outfit"[^ref-7].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core mechanics revolve around tactical combat and resource management. Early in the game the main source of income is mining ore scattered across the maps with an "ore extractor", alongside credits for destroyed enemies and mission bonuses[^ref-1]. A contemporary review counted "eight basic chasses"[^ref-15], and a GameFAQs guide lists HERC types ranging from the light "Shadow" and "Remora" to the heavy "Juggernaut," described as having "the ultimate in offense and defense" and capable of equipping "ANY weapon"[^ref-14].

The same guide groups weapons into Energy, Plasma, Cannons, Missiles, ELF and Advanced types[^ref-14]. Players can also administer chemicals to BioDerms to boost their effectiveness temporarily, at a cost of genetic instability that can be fatal if prolonged[^ref-7]. Multiplayer "can be played on a single computer, live over the Internet, or even though email"[^ref-4].

HERC customization is one of the game's deepest systems. GameSpot noted that "every piece of technology you add has to be carefully considered for its weight, energy use, and price if you want to survive to see the next battle"[^ref-9].

## BioDerm Management

The BioDerm pilot system added unique strategic considerations to CyberStorm's tactical gameplay.

Between missions, in the HERC Base, the player can create, train, assign or "recycle" BioDerms, and can fit better life support to a HERC to raise BioDerm survival rates[^ref-1]. BioDerms can be enhanced with chemicals for a temporary edge, at a cost of genetic instability[^ref-7].

The instructions and early parts of the game present BioDerms as sub-human, but later communiques show they are intelligent, can think independently and even rebel[^ref-1]. Their status as corporate property means they can be "recycled" for a few credits when no longer useful[^ref-10].

### Modern Assessment

The game has been well regarded by retrospective writers. The patch author's itch.io page calls it "the highest-rated game at Home of the Underdogs (current score: 9.23)"[^ref-18], while MyAbandonware describes it as "one of the best giant robot strategy games ever made, bar none"[^ref-2]. User reviews on GameSpot average 8.5/10, with players praising the "excellent turn based combat in the brilliant Starsiege universe"[^ref-19].

However, the game faced criticism for accessibility. One reviewer noted that "MissionForce: Cyberstorm is a ton of fun to play, but it isn't very deep – a con for more hardcore strategy gamers, but a nice touch for those who like their turn-based strategy games to be somewhat lightweight"[^ref-5]. Despite this, "gamers almost universally preferred this to the sequel, Cyberstorm 2: Corporate Wars"[^ref-20].

## Development

### Origins

CyberStorm is the third game in the Earthsiege franchise, a series of giant-robot games that eventually evolved into Starsiege and the shooter Tribes[^ref-4]. MobyGames places it in the Metaltech / Tribes universe, following EarthSiege 2 (1996) and preceding Cyberstorm 2: Corporate Wars (1998)[^ref-7]. Release dates differ between sources: the Exiled wiki gives May 31, 1996[^ref-22], and PCGamingWiki gives May 1996[^ref-8].

### Production

MobyGames credits the game design to John Garvin, Graeme Bayless, Don McClure, Robert Kraft and Stephen Cordon[^ref-7]. The game shipped with "two copies of the software in each box to allow friends to compete with just one purchase"[^ref-9].

The development team was responsive to player feedback. Introducing the v1.1 patch, Dynamix wrote: "Many of these features are a direct result of the great user requests and early feedback we received"[^ref-21]. The readme explains that the update was originally intended for a fall 1996 release, but was held back "due to some extra features we added and other tasks that were required to fine-tune our Holiday-products"[^ref-21]. The late-1996 patch added two new game modes, Hotseat and Play-by-EMail[^ref-1].

### Technical Achievements

CyberStorm ran in Super VGA 640x480 under Windows 95[^ref-15]. Network play included team games with or without Cybrid interference, a free-for-all for up to eight players, and a cooperative mode[^ref-9], and it was possible to play online via Sierra's WON.NET while the service was supported[^ref-7].

## Legacy

PC Gamer UK named MissionForce the 53rd best computer game ever in 1997, calling it a "compelling strategy game", and in 1996 Computer Gaming World listed "the flesh burning off the bioderms" as #12 on its list of "the 15 best ways to die in computer gaming"[^ref-1]. Andy Butcher of Arcane wrote that "CyberStorm is excellent, and will be a tough act to follow for any other strategy-based 'big robot' game"[^ref-1]. Commercially, though, CyberStorm was "a limited commercial success"[^ref-1], and the Exiled wiki notes it "seems to have spawned no large fanbase or cult following unlike many other strategy-games of its time"[^ref-22].

It sold well enough to spawn a 1998 sequel, Cyberstorm 2: Corporate Wars[^ref-1]. Fans "almost universally preferred this to the sequel"[^ref-20], which "tried to introduce real-time combat rather unsuccessfully" and replaced the hex-based map with a more open, RTS-like one[^ref-20].

Modern preservation efforts have kept the game accessible, with community developers creating compatibility patches for newer Windows versions[^ref-23][^ref-25]. Activision released the game digitally on GOG.com on July 23, 2019[^ref-1][^ref-26], describing it as offering "unlimited replayability while you create the ultimate fighting machines"[^ref-26].

The game's dark themes regarding corporate exploitation and the ethics of creating disposable soldiers remain its most discussed feature. Salvador writes that CyberStorm "tells that cynical message through both its strategy and its writing, which is an enormous triumph"[^ref-4].
