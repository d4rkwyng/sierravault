# Starsiege: Tribes

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Starsiege: Tribes was a pioneering multiplayer-focused first-person shooter developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line in 1998[^ref-5]. The game was notable as "the first of its kind: a retail first-person shooter designed specifically for multiplayer competition"[^ref-2], and it took the market risk of being essentially a multiplayer-only game with only training missions for single-player content[^ref-3][^ref-28]. Set in the 40th century within the Starsiege universe, the game featured multiple human tribes fighting for territory[^ref-5].

The game was built around jetpack movement, large outdoor maps and team objectives[^ref-5]. A later retrospective in SuperJump Magazine argued that "Starsiege: Tribes was, arguably, the birth of the team-based, objective-focused multiplayer shooter as a subgenre in its own right"[^ref-6]. The game's most famous feature was its "skiing" movement technique, which began as an unintended physics bug that the designers chose to keep[^ref-7][^ref-28].

## Story Summary

Starsiege: Tribes is set in the 40th century[^ref-5]; one retail description frames it as a universe "that has been infested with a deadly virus that almost wiped out an entire race of people"[^ref-9].

Conflict has broken out between several factions of humans, with innumerable splinter tribes constantly fighting for territory[^ref-5].

Unlike traditional single-player focused games, Tribes concentrated almost entirely on multiplayer combat scenarios with only basic training missions providing narrative context[^ref-3].

The game follows the story of Metaltech: Earthsiege and Starsiege[^ref-5].

The four largest tribes are the Children of the Phoenix, Blood Eagle, Diamond Sword and Starwolf, and the player fights as a warrior loyal to one of them[^ref-5]. Dynamix's announcement described "the merciless Blood Eagle and the idealistic Children of the Phoenix" struggling "over strategic worlds and alien relics"[^ref-20].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Starsiege: Tribes is played with keyboard and mouse[^ref-4]. Players wear light, medium, or heavy armor; heavier armors supply more armor, energy and ammunition, and each armor type supports different weapons and equipment[^ref-5]. Every player has a jetpack that draws on the armor's energy cell[^ref-5], and players could equip various "packs" that altered their abilities[^ref-13].

### Structure and Progression

The game supported 2 to 32 players over the Internet or LAN[^ref-20] and shipped with 40 levels[^ref-5]. The five default game types are Capture the Flag (which ran on 75 percent of servers), Deathmatch, Capture and Hold, Defend and Destroy, and Find and Retrieve[^ref-5]. The game's structure emphasized team-based objectives, with bases featuring generators, turrets, stations, and sensors[^ref-13].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay revolved around mastering the physics-based movement system, particularly "skiing", which relies on an exploitation of the game's physics engine to build speed down hills[^ref-7]. As one player review described it: "Tribes has a huge skill curve created through the complex use of 'skiing' up and down hills and valleys in order to gain speed. Add to that precision aiming at moving targets while flying through the air at the equivalent of hundreds of miles per hour"[^ref-15]. The game featured eight weapons: heavy mortar, sniper rifle, explosive disc launcher, short-range gatling gun, grenade launcher, blaster, plasma rifle, and laser rifle[^ref-5].

Armor choice shaped each player's role. A contemporary strategy guide called heavy armor the choice "for the destruction-lovers and defenders" and recommended light armor with an energy pack for capture-the-flag runs[^ref-12].

Base management added strategic depth beyond direct combat. Generators power a base; destroying them can disable a team's defenses by deactivating its turrets and stations[^ref-5], and the same guide warned that without power a team loses its inventory stations, vehicle purchases and built-in base defenses[^ref-12].

## Competitive Scene

Starsiege: Tribes spawned a dedicated competitive community, "giving rise to hundreds of clans of 10 to 20 players that competed for position on 10-vs.-10 tournament ladders hosted at sites such as Online Gaming League"[^ref-28].

Dynamix included a "demos" feature that let players record their sessions and upload them for others to play back inside the game, useful both for bragging rights and for demonstrating tactics[^ref-28].

Ladder competition was fierce, and the community later developed individual player ranks based on server records and fantasy draft leagues[^ref-27].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have maintained the game's reputation as a classic. Metacritic shows an 8.7/10 user score based on 28 user ratings[^ref-15], while MobyGames shows a Moby Score of 8.1 and a critic average of 86% from 22 reviews[^ref-4]. ModDB's community rates it 8.9/10[^ref-10]. Long-term players continue to praise the game, with one 2021 review calling it "Still the best online multiplayer game, 23 years later..."[^ref-9].

## Development

### Origins

Starsiege: Tribes was announced by Dynamix, a division of Sierra On-Line, on May 28, 1998[^ref-20]. "After creating Starsiege, we knew the only way we could improve upon computer AI was to tap the ultimate AI: human mind against human mind," said Jeff Tunnell, founder of Dynamix[^ref-20]. Tunnell was executive producer, Tim Gift directed, Scott Youngblood led the design with David Meddish as assistant game designer/writer, and Mark Frohnmayer was lead software engineer; MobyGames credits 68 people in all[^ref-26]. A team of 15 to 20 people took about two and a half years to develop the game[^ref-28].

### Production

The game was built on Dynamix's Darkstar engine[^ref-27]. Voice acting was provided by a cast of ten actors: Mark Berry, Kiamalise Budak, Jason Cottle, Andromeda Dunker, Craig English, Kit Harris, Jay Hopper, Kate Myre, Matt Reidy, and Kelly Wright[^ref-26].

### Technical Achievements

Dynamix's announcement promised terrain stretching for miles, seamless movement between building interiors and exteriors, motion-capture animation, weather systems with snow, rain and fog, a built-in 3D mission editor, and software rendering with support for OpenGL and 3Dfx accelerator cards[^ref-20]. At launch the game was "3Dfx Glide or software 3D only", with OpenGL support still planned[^ref-22]. As Frohnmayer and Gift later described, the network code "support[ed] low-end modem connections and [was] designed to deal with low bandwidth, high latency, and intermittent packet loss"[^ref-28]. The game supported TCP/IP, IPX, modem-to-modem, and direct serial connections[^ref-20].

## Legacy

Starsiege: Tribes won the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences' Online Action/Strategy Game of the Year, Computer Games Strategy Plus' 1998 Online Game of the Year, IGN's Best Online Game of the Year for 1998, and PC Gamer US's 1999 Special Achievement in Innovation award, with PC Gamer writing that "Tribes re-wrote the rules of online combat"[^ref-5]. It sold 98,840 copies during 1999[^ref-5].

The game spawned several sequels including Tribes 2 (2001) and Tribes: Vengeance (2004)[^ref-23], and later the free-to-play Tribes: Ascend (2012)[^ref-29]. Tribes 2 kept the original's core mechanics while adding larger network games and more items, vehicles, and equipment[^ref-2]. A planned expansion pack, Tribes Extreme, was cancelled; it was to add single-player missions, multiplayer maps, and bot AI[^ref-5]. Discussing its bots in 1999, Tribes Extreme lead designer Scott Rudi said that "no AI created with today's technology can be as smart or as effective as a human player"[^ref-21]. The original game was released as freeware in 2004[^ref-24], distributed free from May 4, 2004 on a Computer Gaming World DVD and on FilePlanet to promote Tribes: Vengeance[^ref-5].

The skiing mechanic began as a bug: about halfway through development, software engineer Dave Moore showed lead designer Scott Youngblood a collision bug: jumping repeatedly while descending a slope let players accelerate far faster than the jetpack alone allowed, and Youngblood told him, "Nope, don't fix it. This is now a feature"[^ref-28].

Tribes offered several team-based, non-deathmatch modes that, as The Ringer noted, mirror most of the options in modern equivalents like Overwatch[^ref-28]. "We were the guys that invented the concept of 'capture and hold,' which basically turned into all the point-defense games," Youngblood said[^ref-28]. Writing in 2018, The Ringer credited the game with "cultivating an active online community that persists today"[^ref-28].
