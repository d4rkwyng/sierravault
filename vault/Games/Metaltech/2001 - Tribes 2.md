# Tribes 2

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Tribes 2 is a sci-fi first-person shooter multiplayer video game developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line in 2001 as the sequel to Starsiege: Tribes[^ref-1][^ref-7]. Set in the 40th century on the frontier "wilderzone" of human space, hardy neo-barbarian tribes compete for possession of new worlds[^ref-2]. The game's packaging promised fast-paced team play online across vast worlds[^ref-3], with the Tribes of Man facing a new threat: "The BioDerm Horde. Genetically engineered as a slave race, The Horde is driving toward the heart of the Empire in an explosive frenzy of rage and violence"[^ref-3].

DailyRadar's review of the game said of the original *Tribes*: "Unlike anything before it, the multiplayer, 360 degree, team play-based game set new standards for what a multiplayer game could and possibly should be"[^ref-4]. Tribes 2 gameplay makes extensive use of jet pack-powered flight, which adds a notable vertical element to combat, and players move across expansive maps on foot, by jet pack, or in ground and airborne vehicles[^ref-7]. Players are free to choose their own role and loadout[^ref-7], and the game supports large online or LAN matches: PCGamingWiki lists 64 players[^ref-5], while Wikipedia gives up to 128 (64 vs 64)[^ref-7].

## Story Summary

The game is set in the 40th century where humanity has spread across the galaxy into the frontier 'wilderzone' of human space[^ref-2]. Major factions include the Blood Eagle, Diamond Sword, Harbingers of Phoenix, Starwolf, and BioDerm Hordes[^ref-2]. The central conflict involves The Tribes of Man facing a new existential threat from The BioDerm Horde, a genetically engineered slave race driving toward the heart of the Empire; as the box copy puts it, "Humanity must perish..."[^ref-3]. The story chiefly serves as a backdrop for the multiplayer battles[^ref-25].

## Gameplay

### Armor and Loadouts

Players choose among three armor types: Scout, Assault and Juggernaut[^ref-2][^ref-25]. The Scout armor carries three weapons, the Assault up to four and the Juggernaut a maximum of five; only a Scout with an Energy Pack can carry the Laser Rifle, and only a Juggernaut can carry the Fusion Mortar[^ref-2]. Players respawn in Scout armor and re-equip at their team's Inventory Station[^ref-2].

### Interface and Controls

A GameFAQs beginner's guide opens by warning that "Tribes 2 is unlike any first person shooter you'll encounter," because it is mainly an online game[^ref-11]. Players control jetpack-equipped infantry using keyboard and mouse controls, with gamepad/joystick support also available[^ref-5]. The game supports both first-person and third-person perspectives[^ref-5], allowing players to choose their preferred viewpoint for the fast-paced aerial combat.

### Structure and Progression

Game modes include Capture the Flag, Deathmatch, Rabbit, Arena, Hunters, Siege, Gauntlet and Bounty[^ref-7]; GameSurge also describes Capture and Hold[^ref-25]. GameRevolution wrote that the game "delivers a unique and exciting brand of aerial, online mayhem," but also that it "plays by complex rules and mechanics that are completely its own"[^ref-15]. GameSpot found that "the deep teamplay that you can find in large matches is incredibly addicting"[^ref-16].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Central to gameplay is the jet pack, together with a "skiing" action for sliding down slopes[^ref-7]. Players can also deploy weaponry, vehicles and emplacements, many of which can run unattended or be controlled by players[^ref-7]. Vehicle combat adds another layer, as Eurogamer noted: "This isn't just a little infantry showdown any more, this is war"[^ref-19].

### Modern Assessment

On MyAbandonware, users rate it 4.65/5 from 20 votes[^ref-23], and the game has kept an active community through projects like TribesNext[^ref-24]. Critics noted that "Tribes 2 isn't easy to master, and it won't play well on every gaming PC, but when you're up and running it's a tremendous amount of fun"[^ref-16]. GameSurge called it "the best example of a team based multiplayer game to date"[^ref-25]. GameRevolution listed a steep learning curve among its drawbacks[^ref-15], and Eurogamer called the system requirements "simply over the top"[^ref-19].

## Development

### Origins

Development began as a sequel to Starsiege: Tribes, which came out at the very end of 1998 as the first retail first-person shooter designed specifically for multiplayer competition[^ref-16]. In August 2000, Dynamix took beta applications and planned to choose approximately 1,000 applicants[^ref-27]. The game was then scheduled for a Fall 2000 release[^ref-27]; it launched in North America on March 29, 2001[^ref-7].

### Production

David Georgeson was producer and director, with Mark Frohnmayer as lead programmer and Eric Lanz, David Meddish and Jesse Russell as designers[^ref-29]. The voice talent was John Armstrong, Mark Berry, Kiamalise Budak, Kymberli Colbourne, Craig English, Kit Harris, Jay Hopper, Mike Madeoy, Dex Manley, Kate Myre, Matt Reidy, Gary Schwartz and Jen Taylor[^ref-29]. The musical score was composed by Timothy Steven Clarke[^ref-7].

On August 10, 2001, only months after launch, Slashdot relayed reports that Sierra's parent Vivendi was shutting Dynamix down[^ref-43]. The author of a GameFAQs beginner's guide thanked the team: "While corporate America may not appreciate all you've done, the community always will"[^ref-11].

### Technical Achievements

Tribes 2 ran on an early version of the Torque Game Engine[^ref-7][^ref-34]. It demanded substantial hardware for its time: PC Gamer listed a Pentium II 300, 64MB RAM and a 12MB 3D card as required[^ref-22], and GameSpot recommended at least a 600MHz Pentium III and a GeForce or Radeon graphics card[^ref-16]. Eurogamer noted 512x512 high-quality player models "tucked away on the CD"[^ref-19], and the game supports both Direct3D 7 and OpenGL[^ref-5].

## Legacy

The game's engine technology evolved into the Torque Game Engine, which was later released as open source and became Torque 3D[^ref-38]. Shortly after launch in 2001, DailyRadar counted "over 300 servers hosting games around the world"[^ref-4].

The community created extensive modifications and content, including approximately 65 mods, multiple map packs, and more than 1000 scripts and utilities[^ref-40]. When Sierra discontinued official online support in 2008[^ref-41], the community developed TribesNext, a revival project that brought multiplayer functionality back online[^ref-24]. In October 2015, Hi-Rez Studios made every Tribes game available to download for free[^ref-42].
