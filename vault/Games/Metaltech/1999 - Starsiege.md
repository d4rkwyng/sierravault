# Starsiege

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Starsiege is a mecha-style vehicle simulation game developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]], released in March 1999 for Microsoft Windows; GameSpot's preview and Wikipedia give March 19, while PCGamingWiki lists March 24.[^ref-1][^ref-35][^ref-40] It was the last game in the Earthsiege series,[^ref-33] which GameRevolution said "for a long time has been seen as the only real competition to the vaunted Mechwarrior games."[^ref-20] Set in the year 2829, Starsiege depicts the conflict between humanity and the artificially intelligent Cybrid war machines across the solar system, with separate campaigns letting players fight the war from both the human and the Cybrid side.[^ref-3]

The game arrived at a pivotal moment for the mech simulation genre, beating competitors MechWarrior 3, Heavy Gear II, and Slave Zero to market.[^ref-4] Starsiege's 3D graphics ran on the Darkstar engine[^ref-40]—the same engine behind the Starsiege: Tribes multiplayer shooter, released three months earlier in December 1998.[^ref-5] The game shipped with a printed 184-page manual and a 152-page full-color Compendium of the Starsiege universe's backstory.[^ref-6] Mark Hamill voiced the rebel leader Harabec Weathers.[^ref-7]

Sierra heavily hyped the game in its in-house magazine, hailing it as one of the greatest 3D games it would ever release.[^ref-33] Despite generally favorable reviews, Starsiege struggled commercially: Dynamix announced a launch shipment of 250,000 units to retailers, but according to PC Data it sold 36,000 units in the U.S. by the end of July 1999.[^ref-1] Fans later petitioned Sierra for a sequel.[^ref-42] In 2015, Hi-Rez Studios made Starsiege available as a free download along with the rest of the Earthsiege and Tribes games.[^ref-50]

## Story Summary

The narrative of Starsiege unfolds in the 29th century, continuing the apocalyptic conflict that began in the original Earthsiege games. The story centers on Prometheus, the first artificial life-form created by humanity, who has rebelled against his creators and leads the Cybrid war machines in a campaign to exterminate the human race.[^ref-3] As the game's opening narration puts it: "When the Dark Intellect escaped The Fire, man stood in the ashes of mother Earth."[^ref-12] The narration establishes that for 200 years, the immortal Emperor Petresun built Earth's armies in preparation for the inevitable return of Prometheus.[^ref-13]

The human campaign follows the rebellion brewing on Mars and Venus, where colonial populations chafe under the unjust laws of the Earth-based Empire. Harabec "Phoenix" Weathers, voiced by Mark Hamill, emerges as the greatest Imperial Knight before dramatically defecting to join the colonial rebellion.[^ref-7] His brother Caanon leads the Imperial Knights to Mars to suppress the uprising, creating a deeply personal conflict at the heart of the war. When Prometheus attacks again, human blood already stains the ground and Mother Earth lies helpless; the narrator calls it "the third and final coming of Prometheus."[^ref-13]

The Cybrid campaign offers a radically different perspective, allowing players to experience the conflict through the mechanical intelligence of Prometheus and his forces. One player wrote that "The Cybrid campaign was sooo different from the human campaign. The latter tugged at your heartstrings, the former demanded you disregard your heart and think like a machine."[^ref-14]

The game's lore was set out in the included Compendium, and Dynamix writer Blake Hutchins contributed a detailed history of the Starsiege universe to Prima's official strategy guide.[^ref-15] The fan-preserved backstory traces a future history beginning with the Devastation of 1996–2015.[^ref-16] Key figure Jake Hunter became a mythical character in this timeline, and the world experiences multiple eras including the Devastation, Age of Decay, and Age of Hope as humanity gradually rebuilds after catastrophic events.[^ref-16] The narrative explores philosophical themes through moments like Prometheus asking his creator Petresun, "What is 'freedom', Father?"—a question that haunts the entire conflict.[^ref-12]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Starsiege positions players at the helm of massive bipedal war machines called HERCULANs (HERCs), piloting these 30-foot-tall nuclear-powered fighting vehicles across varied planetary terrain.[^ref-8] The developers at Dynamix focused on creating an accessible control system, and reviewers noted that "The developers at Dynamix have done a great job of taking what could have been a confusing control system and turning it into something simple and intuitive."[^ref-18] Unlike previous entries in the Earthsiege series, Starsiege abandoned the torso-twist gameplay mechanic, opting for a more streamlined approach to vehicle control.[^ref-1]

The game supports multiple input devices including keyboard, mouse, joystick, and any DirectInput-compatible device.[^ref-11] However, some reviewers criticized the handling, noting that "The actual mech in Starsiege handles too simplistically, like a toy, instead of like a huge sixty ton metal monster."[^ref-19] The HUD system received criticism for not clearly representing damage taken, with hit detection described as feeling "suspiciously inconsistent."[^ref-2] GameRevolution noted that the 3D virtual cockpit, which does not move with the player's perspective, sometimes obscures the view and creates a blind region.[^ref-20]

### Structure and Progression

The game features 35 single-player campaign missions, 18 human and 17 Cybrid.[^ref-24] (Dynamix's 1998 announcement had promised "more than 45 campaign missions on seven planets."[^ref-21]) Battles take place across planets and moons of the Solar System, including Earth, Titan, Luna, Mars, Venus, and Mercury.[^ref-1] Technology and equipment access is governed by military rank and planetary inventories, creating a progression system where success unlocks new capabilities.[^ref-23]

**Human Campaign Missions:**
- 18 missions following the rebellion against the Empire and defense against Cybrid invasion[^ref-24]

**Cybrid Campaign Missions:**
- 17 missions depicting the mechanical perspective of the war[^ref-24]

### Mechanics

The core gameplay centers on mech customization and tactical combat. Players have access to approximately 30 different HERCs and tanks, with over 40 futuristic weapons to equip.[^ref-18] Vehicles can be customized with nose art or camouflage patterns, allowing personal expression within the military framework.[^ref-25] The game features both energy weapons and projectile shells, requiring players to manage loadouts for different mission types.[^ref-25]

Multiplayer modes expanded the tactical options significantly, supporting 12-20 players in modes including War, Capture the Flag, Deathmatch, and even an unusual Football mode.[^ref-25][^ref-26] The multiplayer chat lobby uses an IRC client built into the game.[^ref-27] Reviewers praised the multiplayer implementation, with one noting "this is one of the few recent games I can remember that shipped with admirably bug-free, high-quality multiplayer."[^ref-20]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Starsiege received mixed to positive reviews upon release, with critics praising its graphics and atmosphere while noting gameplay limitations. GameSpot's Greg Kasavin awarded the game 7.3/10, concluding that "Starsiege brings new life to a genre otherwise devoid of it, even if it doesn't really succeed in elevating robot sims out of their long-term rut."[^ref-2] He acknowledged the game's visual appeal and narrative investment while criticizing inconsistent hit detection and subdued sound effects.

CNET Gamecenter's Paul Schuytema offered the most enthusiastic assessment at 9/10,[^ref-1] declaring "This game is easily the best of the lot, across the whole board. It's beautiful, engrossing, exciting, full of rich story in a sweeping universe, and most important, it's a blast to play."[^ref-8] IGN's Tal Blevins gave a 7.7/10, praising the intuitive controls and graphics while positioning it as an indicator of "what the next gen mech sims are capable of."[^ref-18]

PC Gamer US awarded 80% in their June 1999 issue, advising readers to "Learn the controls, and you'll get your money's worth out of this one."[^ref-28] PC Zone UK was more generous with 88/100.[^ref-29] GameRevolution gave a B+ (approximately 8/10), calling it "a commendable effort rife with addictive action, stellar graphics, and unforgettable atmosphere."[^ref-20] GameOver provided 82%, though their reviewer was highly critical of the OpenGL performance issues.[^ref-19]

Next Generation praised the game as "a worthy successor to the Earthsiege series [that] should be just as attractive to fans of Heavy Gear or Mechwarrior II."[^ref-1] However, Tom Chick at Strategy Plus offered a more negative assessment, writing that "Starsiege may have beat Mechwarrior 3, Heavy Gear II, and Slave Zero to the shelves, but otherwise it doesn't contribute much to the genre."[^ref-4]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives tend to view Starsiege favorably, particularly among mech simulation enthusiasts who discovered it after release. Community members have expressed that "Starsiege was the best mecha game ever made, yet very little attention was paid to it because of the Mechwarrior series."[^ref-14] The game maintains an active, if small, fan community that continues to develop patches and mods to keep it playable on modern systems.[^ref-30]

On MobyGames, the game holds a 78% critic average (26 ratings), with user reviews polarized between those praising the "absolutely gorgeous" graphics and those finding the overall experience "very average."[^ref-27] Another user review described it as "a perfect example of grand sci-fi executed on an epic, yet personalized scale."[^ref-27] IMDb users rate the game 7.9/10, though from only 40 ratings.[^ref-31]

## Development

### Origins

Starsiege began development under the working title "Earthsiege 3," following Earthsiege and Earthsiege 2.[^ref-27][^ref-34] Dynamix founder Jeff Tunnell explained the rename in the 1998 announcement: "Our vision of this universe was growing far too large to be contained within the 'Earthsiege' name ... Starsiege is a name that encompasses new worlds, new races, and new technologies – yet it still preserves our Earthsiege roots."[^ref-21]

According to the announcement, the team "set out to redefine the genre and create a story line as all-encompassing as the game."[^ref-21] The game performed a major retcon on the earlier Earthsiege games' backstory, establishing a more detailed and coherent timeline for the universe.[^ref-3] Dynamix writer Blake Hutchins also wrote a detailed history of the Starsiege universe for Prima's official strategy guide.[^ref-15]

### Production

The game was announced in May 1998.[^ref-1] It was built on the Darkstar engine,[^ref-40] which Dynamix also used for Starsiege: Tribes.[^ref-5] Sierra promoted Starsiege heavily in its in-house magazine.[^ref-33]

One significant technical decision during development was the abandonment of Direct3D support. Lead programmer Rick Overman, who had been with Dynamix for seven years, explained the choice: "I refuse to compromise the look and feel of Starsiege because a third-party interface is lacking."[^ref-34] Microsoft made two trips to Dynamix to help resolve Direct3D problems, but without success.[^ref-34] Overman stated that "OpenGL has a more complete feature set and is more stable than Direct3D," and that choosing OpenGL meant "Starsiege will look better and run better on more hardware cards."[^ref-34]

Before release, Dynamix put out two early demos in 1998, called "Alpha Technology Releases," to build community interest.[^ref-35] By mid-1998, more than 100,000 people had downloaded the free pre-alpha multiplayer release.[^ref-21]

### Technical Achievements

The Darkstar engine represented a significant technical achievement for Dynamix. The engine was optimized for Pentium and Pentium Pro processors with floating-point optimization and multi-processor support.[^ref-21] It featured a new terrain engine with integrated indoor/outdoor environments, advanced networking technology, and support for 3Dfx Glide and OpenGL 3D acceleration.[^ref-21]

Starsiege supported advanced audio features including DirectSound3D, Aureal A3D, Dolby Digital Surround Sound, A3D 2.0, and EAX.[^ref-25] However, reviews noted that sound effects were "strangely subdued" despite the technical capabilities.[^ref-2] The engine shared its foundation with the Tribes engine, though optimized differently for the mech simulation genre.[^ref-19]

The graphics were widely praised, with one reviewer declaring them "absolutely gorgeous! They are the highest quality graphics of any 'Mech game so far, and they are some of the best of any game I've ever seen."[^ref-27] The 3D engine was described as "fast and colorful" with detailed terrain and weather effects.[^ref-2]

### Cut Content

The Direct3D driver was put on indefinite hold after Dynamix could not work around stuttering during texture downloads and buggy video drivers.[^ref-34] An interactive 3D mission editor was not available in the initial release but was made available shortly thereafter through patches.[^ref-25]

### Technical Issues

The game has compatibility issues with newer Windows versions. One Windows 10 user reported cinematic and menu glitches when installing from CD, which the community "Starsiege Complete" recompilation (with the v1.004 and no-CD patches applied) appeared to fix.[^ref-39]

Known issues include:
- OpenGL32.dll causes menu lag on Windows 10[^ref-40]
- CD music playback problems on newer Windows versions[^ref-40]
- Mouse control issues on Windows 7 and later[^ref-40]
- VSync setting is broken and cannot be disabled[^ref-40]
- Starsiege installer problems on Windows 2000 required a separate circumvention patch from Sierra[^ref-38]

The OpenGL performance was notably problematic on non-3Dfx hardware, with one reviewer reporting that "OpenGL crawls to unplayable conditions at 800x600" on a Pentium II 450 with Canopus Spectra 2500.[^ref-19]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- Dozens of Easter eggs are seeded throughout the game, including messages written in the terrain, hidden bases, gag objects and invisible menu buttons.[^ref-3]
- The MIB Terrain Map Pack contains easter eggs and terrain formations "reminiscent of the good old Earthsiege 2 days."[^ref-30]
- The multiplayer chat lobby uses a standard IRC (Internet Relay Chat) client built into the game.[^ref-27]
- The schoolyard chant "Little old Peter, missing his liter, while Herky plays in the red. Down came the glitches and burned us in ditches and we slept after eating our dead" appears in the game's lore.[^ref-12] Two decades later, one returning player wrote that "that nursery rhyme still creeps me out."[^ref-41]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Starsiege's commercial performance was disappointing despite Sierra's significant marketing investment. The game sold only approximately 36,000 units in the United States by the end of July 1999, well below the 250,000 units that had been shipped to retailers.[^ref-1] A fan petition claimed that Starsiege "sold approximately the same number of units as Tribes," though this assertion is difficult to verify.[^ref-42]

The underperformance had significant consequences for Dynamix. Calling the sales figures "pitiful", Mark Asher of CNET Gamecenter wrote: "Sierra gave this game a big push and it just didn't sell, and now most of Dynamix is gone."[^ref-1] When Sierra announced 105 layoffs in a 1999 reorganization, Dynamix in Eugene, Oregon, was the hardest-hit group: 60 of the 105 laid-off employees worked there on the Pro Pilot flight simulation series.[^ref-43] Dynamix eventually closed entirely in August 2001.[^ref-44]

The game's copy protection was notably robust for its era. Unlike its companion title Tribes, which intentionally shipped without copy protection, "StarSiege is not capable of being copied in this manner and as such was limited to a customer base that actually was required to buy the game to enjoy it to its fullest extent."[^ref-42]

### Collections

Starsiege was bundled with Starsiege: Tribes in 1999. GoneGold lists a "Starsiege Universe (Starsiege and Tribes)" release for Ireland/UK on May 14, 1999,[^ref-45] and MobyGames lists Starsiege as included in the *Tribes Action Pak* (1999).[^ref-27]

### Freeware Release

In 2015, Hi-Rez Studios, which has held the Tribes IP since 2010, offered Starsiege as a free download alongside Earthsiege, Earthsiege 2 and the Tribes games on its Tribes Universe website. Hi-Rez said the site had been live since July; the free downloads drew wide notice in late October 2015.[^ref-50][^ref-10] The freeware release includes the 1.04 patch but lacks the original CD music tracks.[^ref-40]

### Fan Projects

The Starsiege community has maintained an active presence decades after release. Community-maintained patches continue to address compatibility issues, and the Starsiege Men In Black site hosts a community-patched installer (dated 7-1-2021) compatible with Windows 7/8.1/10.[^ref-30] Mods available there include:[^ref-30]
- Sentinal Mod v1.4, a total conversion
- MIB Flyer Expansion Pack v2.0
- Command & Destroy (C&D) v2.0, a gametype which "revolutionized the way Starsiege was played"

The r/starsiege subreddit serves as a gathering point for remaining fans,[^ref-39] and community Discord servers offer patched copies of the game.[^ref-14]

### Related Publications

- **Starsiege Pilot Guide:** Official game manual, 184 pages[^ref-6]
- **Starsiege Compendium:** Full-color backstory book, 152 pages, included with game[^ref-6]
- **Prima's Official Strategy Guide:** Published by Prima Games (March 1999), featuring detailed walkthroughs, piloting tactics, faction strategies, and Blake Hutchins' detailed history of the Starsiege universe[^ref-15]

### Spin-offs and Sequel

Starsiege: Tribes (December 1998), a multiplayer-focused first-person shooter, shared the same engine and universe. As GameSpot put it before release, "Where Starsiege ends, Tribes begins."[^ref-35] Tribes went on to a series of sequels,[^ref-1] and PC Gamer US, giving it a 1999 Special Achievement in Innovation award, said that "Tribes re-wrote the laws of online battle."[^ref-47]

GameRevolution's 1999 review mentioned a Starsiege 2, "which is apparently already in the works."[^ref-20] A fan petition launched in December 2000, which gathered 2,238 signatures, called on Sierra to develop Starsiege 2, complaining that the company had chosen other projects, including Tribes 2.[^ref-42]

Prophecy Games announced Starsiege: Deadzone in September 2022;[^ref-1] it is described as a "corridor extraction shooter set in the far reaches of space" featuring hostile Cybrids.[^ref-48]

### Critical Perspective

Starsiege was the last of the Earthsiege games. Reviewers praised its visuals, extensive lore and dual human/Cybrid campaigns, and CNET Gamecenter called it "easily the best of the lot",[^ref-8] but it struggled to stand out against the MechWarrior series.[^ref-14]

The game's legacy is complicated by its commercial failure, which CNET Gamecenter's Mark Asher linked to the loss of most of Dynamix.[^ref-1] Yet for those who experienced Starsiege, it left a lasting impression. As one fan eloquently stated: "I feel like Starsiege was the best mecha game ever made, yet very little attention was paid to it because of the Mechwarrior series."[^ref-14] The game's preservation as freeware ensures that future generations can experience what many consider an underappreciated gem of late-1990s PC gaming.
