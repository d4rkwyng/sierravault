# Sabotage

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

Sabotage is a fixed shooter arcade game developed by Mark Allen and published by On-Line Systems in 1981 for the Apple II computer.[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-12][^ref-14] In an era when the majority of action titles released for home computers either were inspired by or directly copied popular video arcade games, Sabotage stood apart as a genuinely original concept.[^ref-4][^ref-6] The game places players in command of a gun turret tasked with defending against helicopters, jets, and paratroopers attempting to overwhelm their position.[^ref-3]

The game's scoring system was original for its time: one point is subtracted every time a shell is fired, discouraging the "spray & pray" style of play.[^ref-1][^ref-4] Retro365 notes that this made players "strive to aim and shoot only where it mattered".[^ref-4] Sabotage was well received, placing 16th in the Softtalk Reader's Poll Top-Thirty for 1981.[^ref-4] The game was vastly pirated and became one of the most-played Apple II games of 1981.[^ref-4] It spawned numerous clones across multiple platforms, including the PC title Paratrooper.[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-6]

## Story Summary

Sabotage features minimal narrative framing typical of early 1980s arcade-style games. The player takes command of a powerful gun base with the objective of shooting helicopters and aircraft out of the air.[^ref-6] The opposing forces have plans to sabotage your base through a coordinated assault involving aerial bombardment and paratrooper deployment.[^ref-6]

The gameplay scenario presents a defensive military situation where the player must fend off continuous waves of enemy aircraft. Helicopters fly across the screen dropping paratroopers, and later bombers (jets) arrive to drop bombs on the turret.[^ref-1][^ref-5] If enough paratroopers land, they blow up the emplacement and the game ends.[^ref-3][^ref-5]

The tension escalates as the player must balance shooting down aircraft, eliminating paratroopers mid-descent, and destroying bombs before they reach the turret.[^ref-3]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Sabotage supports several control methods. Players can aim with the D and F keys, a single game paddle, or a single axis of a joystick.[^ref-1][^ref-3] The game was designed around paddle controls on the Apple II.[^ref-7] Retro365 recommends paddles or an analog joystick, noting that keyboard aiming moves in fixed steps and requires repeated taps.[^ref-4]

The gun turret can swivel to cover a large area of the screen but cannot move from its base, making this a classic "fixed shooter" design.[^ref-1][^ref-3] On the keyboard, any key other than D and F fires a shell. With a paddle, the paddle button fires.[^ref-1]

### Structure and Progression

The game follows a continuous wave-based structure. The following can be on screen at once:[^ref-1]

- **Helicopters or bombers:** Up to 4. Helicopters drop paratroopers, and bombers drop bombs.
- **Paratroopers:** Up to 8 falling at any given time.
- **Bombs:** Up to 2 active at once.
- **Shells:** Up to 8 of the player's shells in flight at once.

There are no discrete levels, and difficulty rises over time rather than with score. Helicopters start in two "lanes" near the top of the screen. Every 768 frames (about 40 seconds) more lanes open, allowing lower flights. After the fifth expansion the game switches to a bomber phase, which lasts until (N+1)×4 bombs have been dropped, where N is the difficulty level. The difficulty level starts at 0, can rise to 5, and increases at the end of each bombing phase. Higher levels also bring denser helicopter traffic and more paratrooper drops.[^ref-1] Data Driven Gamer found that the first few minutes of play are "really easy".[^ref-7]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay mechanic revolves around the scoring system. Points are awarded for destroying enemies: helicopters and jets are worth 5 points each, paratroopers are worth 2 points, and bombs are worth 25 points.[^ref-3][^ref-7] However, firing a shell costs the player one point, so every shot carries a cost.[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-4] With paratroopers only worth two points, even perfect accuracy yields only one net point per paratrooper eliminated.[^ref-7] The score never drops below zero.[^ref-3]

Shooting the parachute off a falling paratrooper makes him plummet, and he kills any landed paratrooper beneath him. Shrapnel from destroyed aircraft can also destroy falling paratroopers and other aircraft.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

**Game Over Conditions:**[^ref-1][^ref-3]
- The turret is hit by a bomb
- A single paratrooper lands directly on the turret base
- Three paratroopers land atop each other immediately adjacent to the turret base, allowing the third to jump onto the base
- Four paratroopers land on either the left or right side of the turret, letting them build a human pyramid and climb up to blow up the turret

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Retro365 reports that Sabotage was well received on release and placed 16th in the Softtalk Reader's Poll Top-Thirty for 1981.[^ref-4]

The French magazine *Tilt* (#8, November/December 1983) included Sabotage in its "300 cartouches, cassettes et disquettes" buyer's guide. The guide listed it as a Mark Allen action game for the Apple II on disk at 395 F, gave it five stars for originality, and summed it up as "Défendez un canon antiaérien des attaques d'un commando de parachutistes".[^ref-16] UVList records Tilt's overall rating as 4/6 (66%).[^ref-14]

### Modern Assessment

Retrospective analysis has been mixed. Data Driven Gamer rated Sabotage "Below average" in a June 2020 post, with the reviewer Ahab commenting "This was fun for a few minutes, but got old fast."[^ref-7]

MobyGames user Katakis noted that "Mark Allen did a good job at making a version for the Apple II; the game was remarkable for the time, with its color graphics, smooth animation, and sound effects."[^ref-2] The same reviewer observed that "Like all of their other games, On-Line Systems has done a great job at bringing arcade games to the Apple II. The quality of the game may be crap today, but it was good for its time."[^ref-2]

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 67% (Critics aggregate, 1 rating)[^ref-2]
- **UVList:** 66% (external review average, from Tilt)[^ref-14]
- **My Abandonware:** 5/5 (3 votes)[^ref-9]

## Development

### Origins

Mark Allen developed Sabotage as an original concept during a period when most home computer action games were direct clones of popular arcade titles.[^ref-4][^ref-6] His previous game, *Stellar Invaders*, was a Space Invaders clone that Apple published in 1980 as *Apple Stellar Invaders*.[^ref-4] In 1978 at the University of California, San Diego, Allen and Richard Gleaves had developed a 6502 interpreter for the Pascal programming language, which later became the foundation for Apple Pascal.[^ref-4]

Rather than copying an existing arcade game, Allen created a defensive shooter concept that would itself become widely imitated.[^ref-4][^ref-6]

### Production

Sabotage was written in machine language.[^ref-6] Its exact release date is uncertain. Retro365 says it was released in the summer of 1981, but it also reproduces an On-Line Systems advertisement featuring the game from the May 1981 issue of InfoWorld.[^ref-4] MobyGames gives a release date of October 1, 1981.[^ref-2] The Player/Missile Podcast covered the game in its March 1981 magazine episode, but it does not date the game's release.[^ref-10]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-1][^ref-2][^ref-3]
- **Designer/Programmer:** Mark Allen

### Technical Achievements

Graphics are drawn on hi-res page 1 only, and all graphics are drawn and erased with exclusive-OR operations. Using XOR means the game doesn't have to be careful about the order in which drawing happens.[^ref-1] Collision detection is pixel-accurate.[^ref-1]

For single-pixel horizontal movement, the hi-res screen architecture requires seven bit-shifted versions of each shape. The game uses slightly different images for each position, so the helicopter and bomber propellers twirl as they move.[^ref-1]

Shells move roughly 8 or 12 pixels per frame, depending on whether the optional steerable-shell mode is on (steerable shells are slower).[^ref-1] Up to 8 shells, 4 helicopters or bombers, 2 bombs, 8 falling paratroopers and 14 shrapnel fragments can be active at once.[^ref-1]

### Technical Issues

Andy McFadden's disassembly identified an apparent bug in the collision detection for falling bombs. The code at $6ae0 walks through the list of shell structures but uses the wrong structure size, so every entry after the first reads incorrect data and will likely fail to detect a collision.[^ref-1] Because bombs fall up to 5 pixels per frame, a bomb near the gun can also step over a shell that should hit it.[^ref-1]

MobyGames reviewer Katakis criticised the placement of the score counter inside the turret rather than at the top of the screen. The review asks what would happen when a score passes 100 and spills beyond the edge of the turret.[^ref-2]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game was an original concept at a time when most action games were arcade clones, which made it unusual for the era.[^ref-4][^ref-6]
- Sabotage was vastly pirated and became one of the most-played Apple II games of 1981.[^ref-4]
- The Sierra Chest says the game was not re-released as a SierraVision title.[^ref-6] Retro365 says it was later announced under the SierraVision label but isn't sure that release ever materialized.[^ref-4]
- Each shell fired subtracts a point, a deliberate deterrent to "spray & pray" shooting.[^ref-1]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures have been documented. Sabotage placed 16th in Softtalk's 1981 Reader's Poll Top-Thirty, and like most early titles it was vastly pirated, becoming one of the most-played Apple II games of 1981.[^ref-4]

### Clones and Derivatives

Sabotage's gameplay spawned numerous clones and derivatives across multiple platforms:[^ref-3][^ref-6]

| Title | Platform | Year | Developer/Notes |
|-------|----------|------|-----------------|
| Commando Raid | Atari 2600 | 1982 | Clone[^ref-3] |
| M.A.D. (Missile Attack and Defense) | Atari 2600 | 1982 | Clone[^ref-3] |
| Paratrooper | IBM PC | 1982 | Greg Kuperberg, published by Orion Software. Data Driven Gamer calls it "nearly a carbon copy".[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-7] |
| Airborne! | Macintosh (black and white) | 1985 | Silicon Beach Software[^ref-3] |
| Incoming! | Atari 8-bit | 1986 | Conrad Tatge; ANALOG Computing magazine[^ref-3] |
| ParaShooter! | Apple IIGS | 1989 | Lane Roathe[^ref-3] |
| Night Raid | IBM PC | 1992 | Clone[^ref-3] |
| Parachute | iPod | 2003 | Preloaded on the third-generation iPod and later models[^ref-3] |

The most notable clone, Paratrooper for the IBM PC, was developed by Greg Kuperberg, who also created PC-Man. It uses the CGA's cyan-on-black color scheme.[^ref-7] Data Driven Gamer wrote that "it turns out that this nearly a carbon copy of a lesser-known Apple II game called Sabotage, published in 1981 by On-Line Systems."[^ref-7] Retro365 calls Paratrooper "one of the most important early x86 titles".[^ref-4]

### Collections

No major compilation releases have been documented for the original Sabotage, though the game remains available through abandonware archives and emulation.[^ref-5][^ref-9][^ref-11]

### Fan Projects

Sabotage was unofficially converted to the Atari 8-bit. The Player/Missile Podcast interviewed the converter, AtariAge user Shamus, about the conversion.[^ref-10] Retro365 describes the converter, "Hacker Cracker", recreating the game without the source code and distributing it freely on The Edge Of Reality BBS as S.A.B.O.T.A.G.E.[^ref-4]

### Related Publications

No official hint books or strategy guides have been documented.

### Critical Perspective

Retro365 frames Sabotage as an exception in an era when most home computer action titles "either were inspired by or directly copied" the arcade hits of the day. It also says the game "went on to become a classic" and spawned numerous clones.[^ref-4] The design was still being cloned more than two decades later, when Parachute came preloaded on the third-generation iPod in 2003.[^ref-3]

On the technical side, Andy McFadden's disassembly documents pixel-accurate collision detection, single-pixel horizontal movement using seven pre-shifted copies of each shape, and exclusive-OR drawing on a single hi-res page.[^ref-1]
