# Marauder

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

*Marauder* is a top-down arcade shooter developed by [[On-Line Systems]] and released in 1982 for the Apple II, Atari 2600, and Atari 8-bit computers[^ref-3][^ref-5][^ref-12]. Designed by Eric Hammond and Rorke Weigandt[^ref-17][^ref-23], the game draws heavy inspiration from Stern's *Berzerk*, placing players in the role of an intruder who must navigate robot-infested mazes to destroy an alien power center[^ref-3][^ref-15]. The Atari 2600 version was published by Tigervision, after Tiger Electronics approached On-Line Systems in 1982 with a proposal to convert some of its computer games for the Atari 2600[^ref-3].

The game occupies an interesting position in Sierra's early history, releasing the same year that On-Line Systems formally renamed itself to Sierra On-Line[^ref-3][^ref-7]. While never achieving the commercial success or critical acclaim of the company's adventure titles, *Marauder* represents Sierra's attempts to diversify into the action game market during the golden age of arcade gaming[^ref-3]. According to Retro365, weeks before release a copy was given to a joystick manufacturer to use at a tradeshow, and days later copies showed up on pirate boards[^ref-3].

Despite only modest sales, *Marauder* has gained appreciation among retro gaming enthusiasts as an underrated title in the Atari 2600 library[^ref-8]. Collectors value the Tigervision cartridge highly; as of 2019, loose copies were listed on eBay for $55-130 and boxed copies had sold for $70-400[^ref-9].

## Story Summary

The narrative premise of *Marauder* is straightforward science fiction action fare typical of early 1980s arcade games. The player takes on the role of an unnamed space operative who has landed on an alien planet and successfully infiltrated an enemy base defended by armed attack robots[^ref-5]. The mission objective is simple but deadly: navigate through the hostile facility to locate and destroy the power center of the enemy's weapon system[^ref-5].

According to the game's manual, robots pursue the player through halls and corridors, from room to room, as their sensors detect intrusion into their territory[^ref-8]. The aliens fire from all corners as the player desperately seeks the power center while defending against the robotic onslaught[^ref-8]. The box description dramatically proclaims: "Alien robots have invaded! You must make your way to safety and recapture the cosmic treasure. Maneuver your way through six different mazes. Watch out for the alien robots hidden throughout the mazes. Shoot them or they will shoot you. Time is against you. The ultimate experience in alien invasion on Tigervision"[^ref-4][^ref-8].

Upon successfully destroying the power center, the game starts anew on a harder difficulty level, reflecting the arcade design philosophy of escalating challenge rather than conclusive narrative endings[^ref-5][^ref-15].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

*Marauder* utilizes a digital joystick for single-player control, employing a top-down perspective with a fixed flip-screen visual presentation[^ref-5]. Players control a small character sprite navigating through maze-like rooms while shooting at enemy robots. According to the manual, the Marauder can move in eight directions (up, down, left, right and diagonally) and fires a bullet in the direction he is facing[^ref-14].

### Structure and Progression

The game consists of six distinct screens arranged in a 2x3 grid that players must navigate to locate and destroy the power center[^ref-8][^ref-9]. Unlike *Berzerk*, where all enemies are visible immediately upon entering a screen, *Marauder* features a more sophisticated enemy visibility system where robots only appear on screen when they enter the player's line of sight[^ref-11][^ref-15]. This was considered an advanced feature for the time[^ref-11].

The game features randomized room arrangement and starting positions, adding replay variety[^ref-9]. On the Atari 2600, each cleared round escalates the difficulty (these are 2600 rounds, not the two gameplay phases of the Apple II and Atari 8-bit versions):

- **Round 1:** Four robots per room at base speed[^ref-5]
- **Round 2:** Five robots per room[^ref-5]
- **Round 3:** Six robots per room[^ref-5]
- **Round 4:** Returns to four robots but with increased speed[^ref-5]

Destroyed robots generally stay dead, allowing players to systematically clear rooms[^ref-9][^ref-11]. The power center room is the exception: per the manual, if the player leaves and returns, new robots are called in so that there are always four defending it[^ref-14]. Notably, there is no "Evil Otto" equivalent—the relentless enemy that pursues players in *Berzerk*—giving players more freedom to explore at their own pace[^ref-9].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The primary mechanical challenge involves navigating the maze structure while managing encounters with robot enemies. Players can move freely between rooms to strategically approach the power center[^ref-9]. A key power-up called "magic armor" provides temporary invulnerability, offering crucial assistance during difficult sections[^ref-5][^ref-11].

The Apple II and Atari 8-bit versions feature a two-phase gameplay structure that was condensed to a single phase for the Atari 2600 version due to memory limitations[^ref-3][^ref-6]. The Apple II manual says the first two levels "can be played either separately or simultaneously"; its main menu offers "Play level one only", "Play level two only" and "Play levels one and two", plus an option to enter a starting skill level, and it states that the game "offers 16 skill levels"[^ref-22]. In that version's Level I the player pilots a ship against an alien planet's defenses, and a boost button has a "four second interval" between uses[^ref-22]. *Softline*'s 1982 review of the Apple II original notes that players could choose to play the first part or the second part exclusively, or the full game[^ref-16]. This removed first phase represents significant cut content from the console port[^ref-3].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

*Softline* reviewed the Apple II original in its September 1982 issue, crediting it "By Rorke Weigandt and Eric Hammond" and describing "a two-part space seek-and-destroy game"[^ref-16]. The reviewer noted that the robots cannot be seen until they are in line of sight, and concluded: "All in all, Marauder is as good as any mindless arcade game, and better than most"[^ref-16]. The listing gave "Apple II, Apple II Plus; 48K; disk. $34.95 from Sierra On-Line"[^ref-16].

*Softalk* also reviewed the Apple II original in its September 1982 issue (page 133), crediting "Rorke Weigandt and Eric Hammond" and describing "two completely different games that are thematically linked"[^ref-17]. It says the first scenario has "nine total" levels of difficulty, that the maze's aliens "only appear when you get too close", and that players can choose "the two scenarios separately or sequentially on either keyboard or joystick"[^ref-17].

*Creative Computing* ran a Software Profile in its January 1983 issue, listing the game as an arcade game for a "48K Apple, disk drive, joystick" at $34.95[^ref-18]. The profile calls Level II a "Berzerk-style maze" in which the player "can see only those robots that are within your field of vision", and says "There are 16 levels of play on the disk"[^ref-18]. These 1982 and early-1983 descriptions show that limited robot visibility was already a feature of the Apple II original.

In July 1983 *Electronic Fun with Computers & Games* reviewed the Atari 400/800 version (by Paul Backer)[^ref-19]. It described the first stage as "a hybrid of Space Invaders and Missile Command" and the second as "Berzerk with some very interesting changes", including that "you can only see the robots when they are in the same corridor as you"[^ref-19]. The review found "very little challenge for better-than-average players" and called the game "an absolute must-have" for those just starting out with action games[^ref-19]. Atarimania's transcription of the review lists a rating of 3 out of 4[^ref-26].

*A.N.A.L.O.G. Computing* took the opposite view of the Atari 8-bit version in issue No. 12 (July/August 1983), where the magazine, spelling the title "MAURADER", wrote: "MAURADER is a cheap combination of MISSILE COMMAND and SHAMUS, with poor graphics, sounds and playability"[^ref-27]. The same piece said Sierra On-Line, "while still holding their high standing in the Apple market, is rapidly losing the respect they have earned from ATARI owners" through releases including *Marauder*, *WallWar*, *Lunar Leeper* and the rewritten *Jawbreaker*[^ref-27]. Set beside *Electronic Fun*'s "absolute must-have" for newcomers, the two reviews split on the Atari 8-bit version[^ref-19][^ref-27].

The only online review that Orphaned Games could find (in 2011) was negative: The Video Game Critic gave the Atari 2600 version an **F** in 2002, calling the game a "cakewalk"[^ref-8][^ref-11]. The reviewer later revised this after a reader pointed out he had been overly critical[^ref-11].

Retro365 lists the November 1982 issue of *Softline* magazine among its period sources for the game's history[^ref-3], and the @oldschoolfrp Tumblr archive reposts a 1982 *Marauder* magazine scan tagged \#Softline[^ref-7].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been kinder to *Marauder*, with several reviewers highlighting its underappreciated qualities. The Video Game Critic revised their assessment to a **C+** in November 2016, noting: "Marauder may be a Berzerk clone but there's more than enough innovative elements to set it apart"[^ref-11]. The reviewer specifically praised the line-of-sight enemy visibility system as "a pretty advanced feature"[^ref-11].

Adam Trionfo of Orphaned Computers & Game Systems declared: "Without doubt, this is easily one of the most underrated games in the Atari 2600 collection"[^ref-8]. His co-reviewer Chris Federico concurred, calling it "an extremely well-made Atari VCS game, and certainly one of TigerVision's best"[^ref-8].

The Doctor Spuds review blog offered a more tempered assessment, rating it as "thoroughly 'MEH'" and concluding "Straight to the Collector's Zone for Marauder, the game simply doesn't justify the price"[^ref-9]. The reviewer noted that "this entire game is made up of six screens and six different flavors of blob"[^ref-9].

## Development

### Origins

*Marauder* was created by Eric Hammond and Rorke Weigandt on Weigandt's Apple II computer, and was then picked up by On-Line Systems, which published it for the Apple II in 1982[^ref-3]. Retro365 calls it Hammond and Weigandt's first game[^ref-3]; the same article notes that when Hammond wrote *One-on-One* for Electronic Arts in 1983, he had only a year of experience and two simple commercial releases behind him[^ref-3]. Hammond was self-taught, having learned both BASIC and 6502 Assembly Language independently before contributing to this project[^ref-3]. After *Marauder* he spent eight weeks creating *Maze Craze Construction Set*, a *Pac-Man*-style game with a construction set for players, enemies and mazes, which Data Trek, Inc. published for the Apple II in 1983; he joined Lucasfilm Games in 1989 to work on sound effects and music[^ref-3].

The creative inspiration for *Marauder* drew directly from the most successful arcade games of the era. Retro365 describes the original game's first part as a hybrid of *Space Invaders* and *Missile Command*, and its second part as a variation of *Berzerk*[^ref-3]. Kotaku's game summary calls *Marauder* "a Berzerk-clone with some minor alterations"[^ref-15].

In a Backerkit discussion of NBC Magazine footage of Sierra On-Line from autumn 1982, researcher Lance Ewing identifies Eric Hammond, "Co-author of Marauder", among those on screen[^ref-2].

### Production

The original Apple II version utilized most of the platform's 48KB of memory to accommodate the full two-phase gameplay experience[^ref-3]. When the game was ported to the Atari 400/800 computers, Retro365 says the duo rewrote it and "managed to squeeze the game into only 20 KB"[^ref-3]. Other period sources give different figures. *Electronic Fun*'s July 1983 review says the two games "together they take up only 32K of memory"[^ref-19], while Sierra's own advertisement in *Computer Gaming World* (March-April 1983) lists the game as available for "Apple II/II+ (48K) and Atari 400/800 (40K)"[^ref-20]. The sources do not agree, and this page does not choose between the Atari 8-bit figures.

The Atari 2600 version presented the greatest technical challenge, as the console featured only 128 bytes of internal memory and cartridges were limited to 4KB of data[^ref-3][^ref-13]. This severe constraint necessitated removing the first phase entirely, resulting in a version that contains only the second part of the original game[^ref-3].

### Technical Achievements

The most notable technical innovation in *Marauder* was its line-of-sight enemy visibility system[^ref-11][^ref-15]. Rather than displaying all enemies immediately when entering a room (as in *Berzerk*), robots only appear when they fall within the player's field of view[^ref-15]. This created a more suspenseful gameplay experience and was considered technically impressive for the era[^ref-11]. The feature was not new to the Atari 2600 port: the Apple II manual says "you will not be able to see the robots until they are within your field of vision"[^ref-22], and *Softalk* and *Creative Computing* described it for the Apple II original in 1982-83[^ref-17][^ref-18].

The game also managed to implement room persistence within the constraints of the Atari 2600 hardware, allowing destroyed robots to remain eliminated rather than respawning, except in the power center room[^ref-9][^ref-14]. This feature, combined with the six-room maze structure and magic armor power-up system, demonstrated efficient use of the limited 4KB cartridge space[^ref-5][^ref-8].

### Cut Content

The Atari 2600 version represents the most significant content reduction in the game's release history. Due to the platform's severe memory limitations, the entire first phase of gameplay present in the Apple II and Atari 8-bit versions was removed[^ref-3][^ref-6]. This effectively made the console version a condensed experience compared to its computer counterparts.

### Technical Issues

Several bugs and design issues have been documented in the Atari 2600 version:

- **Spawn Point Issues:** Upon respawning, players are sometimes placed directly in front of converging robots, leading to unavoidable deaths[^ref-8][^ref-11]
- **Wall Clipping:** Robots and magic armor power-ups can occasionally appear inside walls when entering rooms[^ref-8][^ref-9]
- **Robot Pathfinding:** Orphaned Games says robots cannot normally walk through walls, but one that appears inside a wall can walk through the rest of it, which lets it exit the wall and then proceed normally[^ref-8]
- **Starting Position Variance:** The random room generation sometimes places the player in the power cell room first, making for extremely easy rounds[^ref-11]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

Several hidden mechanics and interesting facts about *Marauder* have been documented:

- **Deadly Last Shots:** Players can fire bullets and destroy the power center even after their character has died, allowing for posthumous victory[^ref-8]
- **Robot Collision:** Walking directly through robots allows players to avoid their shots, exploiting collision detection quirks[^ref-8]
- **Friendly Fire:** Robot shots can inadvertently destroy other robots, creating tactical opportunities[^ref-8]
- **Musical Recycling:** The Doctor Spuds reviewer believes, with a "correct me if I'm wrong", that the game shares its entire musical lineup with *Custer's Revenge*, another 1982 Atari 2600 title[^ref-9]
- **Piracy Origins:** Weeks before release, a copy was given to a joystick manufacturer for a tradeshow; days later, copies showed up on pirate boards[^ref-3]
- **Company Transition:** *Marauder* released the same year On-Line Systems officially became Sierra On-Line[^ref-3][^ref-7]

The game's wrap-around box art depicts robots chasing the titular marauder through the facility[^ref-8]. Tigervision only published two other space-related Atari 2600 games: *Espial* and *Threshold*[^ref-8].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

*Marauder* achieved only modest commercial success according to available records[^ref-3]. Retro365 attributes this partly to piracy: the game was "widely pirated from the get-go", after a pre-release copy lent to a joystick manufacturer for a tradeshow reached pirate boards within days[^ref-3]. As one retrospective noted: "The shelf life of games in the early '80s was merely a few weeks, that was all it took before the copy protection was cracked and piracy took over"[^ref-3].

*Softalk*'s September 1982 bestseller column, drawn from a poll of Apple-franchised retail stores on their July sales, said of Sierra On-Line (then On-Line Systems) that "Cannonball Blitz and Marauder joined Screen Writer on the Top Thirty"[^ref-17].

In late 1982 the Atari 400/800 version was released under Sierra's new SierraVision action label, alongside a rerelease of the Apple II version[^ref-3]. A year later, after the North American video game market crash, the SierraVision label was dropped for good[^ref-3].

The Atari 2600 cartridge also reached other markets. Atarimania lists a 1982 Tigervision Europe release plus three Brazilian releases (Atrevision, Canal 3/Intellivision and Robby)[^ref-25]. In its January 1984 issue *Computer & Video Games* reported that "the entire Tigervision range is now available in the UK" through the London-based distributor Prism, describing *Marauder* as a game "where you have to get through six mazes to recover the cosmic treasure" and listing mail order from Prism at £21.95 for *Jawbreaker*, *Marauder*, *Threshold* and *King Kong*[^ref-21].

### Collections

*Marauder* has not been featured in any official Sierra compilation releases. The game remains available primarily through retro gaming preservation sites and the secondary collector's market[^ref-9][^ref-10].

### Collector's Market

The Tigervision Atari 2600 cartridge has become a sought-after collectible:

- **Loose Cartridges:** $55-130 on eBay (listings as of 2019)[^ref-9]
- **Boxed Copies:** $70-400 in historical sales (as of 2019)[^ref-9]

The Doctor Spuds blog notes that "since this is a Tigervision game the prices are going to be a bit ludicrous"[^ref-9].

### Critical Perspective

*Marauder* occupies a curious position in Sierra's history as one of their early action game experiments before the company fully committed to the adventure genre that would define their legacy. While games like *Mystery House* and *Wizard and the Princess* were establishing Sierra as adventure game pioneers, titles like *Marauder* represented attempts to capture the arcade action market that dominated early 1980s gaming[^ref-3].

The game's reception arc—from faint period praise and a dismissive 2002 online review to modern reappraisal—reflects broader trends in retro gaming evaluation. *Softline* judged it "as good as any mindless arcade game"[^ref-16] and The Video Game Critic first called it a "cakewalk"[^ref-11], while later assessments have recognized its technical innovations and solid gameplay within the severe constraints of Atari 2600 hardware[^ref-8][^ref-11]. As of October 2026, MobyGames ranks the Atari 2600 *Marauder* #143 among Atari 2600 titles[^ref-5].

The comparison to *Berzerk*, *Venture*, and *Room of Doom* provides useful context for understanding *Marauder*'s place in the maze shooter genre[^ref-9]. While not revolutionary, the game's line-of-sight mechanics and room persistence represented meaningful iterations on established formulas[^ref-11][^ref-15].
