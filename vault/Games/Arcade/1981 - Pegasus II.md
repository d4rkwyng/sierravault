# Pegasus II

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

Pegasus II is a side-scrolling arcade shooter for the Apple II, written by Olaf Lubeck and published in 1981 by [[On-Line Systems]], the company [[Ken Williams|Ken]] and [[Roberta Williams]] founded and later renamed Sierra On-Line.[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-4] MobyGames classes it as a "Scramble variant": the player flies over mountainous terrain, bombing ground installations and using lasers against rockets that launch from below.[^ref-1] It was Lubeck's second published game for On-Line Systems, after the Pac-Man clone [[1981 - Gobbler|Gobbler]].[^ref-3][^ref-4]

The game shipped with a terrain generator. Players could draw their own landscape as twenty-one separate "frames" that the program then joined together at random.[^ref-5][^ref-1] Creative Computing reviewed it in August 1982 and called it a "challenging, fast moving flying game with the option of user-definable terrain".[^ref-6] Years later John Romero remembered first seeing Pegasus II in the Apple room at Sierra College, and named its terrain generator as one of the earliest level editors he had used.[^ref-7]

Pegasus II entered *Softalk*'s Top Thirty best-seller list for the first time in the magazine's November 1981 issue.[^ref-8] It was sold on disk for 48K Apple II and II Plus machines at $29.95.[^ref-9][^ref-10]

## Story Summary

The game has only a small amount of story, and it comes from On-Line Systems' advertising. The player is "the pilot of the fighter PEGASUS II" and must destroy the base station of the enemy Gebellians, described as "an alien race best known for depositing its eggs into space".[^ref-9] If the Gebellians survive, the advertisement warns, they will take over the player's "alliance of 30 worlds (best known as The Top Thirty)". The line is a nod to *Softalk*'s best-seller chart.[^ref-9][^ref-8] On-Line's catalogue repeated the same premise in shorter form.[^ref-10]

The aliens' name was a tribute. A 1983 *Softline* profile of Lubeck says the Gebellians came from "Lubeck's admiration for the pioneering work of Nasir Gebelli", the Sirius Software programmer.[^ref-11] Retro365 adds that the egg-laying description refers to Gebelli's game *Space Eggs*.[^ref-4]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The game can be played with paddles or a joystick, and it boots under either DOS 3.2 or DOS 3.3.[^ref-5] With paddles, the dial moves the ship up and down, the A key fires the "lazer", the S key drops bombs, and the space bar moves the ship forward or back across the screen.[^ref-5] With a joystick, the stick moves the ship in all four directions.[^ref-5]

Creative Computing's reviewers said the game "must be played with a joystick". In their view the space bar did not give enough control for "the tricky terrain". They also found a mistake in the instructions: in joystick mode the stick's two buttons, not the A and S keys, fire the laser and drop bombs.[^ref-6] Paddle players had their own complaints. In May 1982 a reader wrote to *Softline* calling Pegasus II "a great game except for one thing": with paddles, the bombs and lasers stayed on the A and S keys.[^ref-12]

### Structure and Progression

At start-up the player chooses between playing and generating new terrain. Next come standard or user-made terrain, a Beginner, Intermediate or Advanced tunnel, and paddle or joystick control.[^ref-5] Creative Computing described the cycle of play like this:[^ref-6]

- **Target terrain:** The plane flies over cities, plains, mountains and chasms. On the ground are radar detectors, surface-to-air missiles and targets the reviewers thought looked like alien robots. Each is worth 20 points. Birds (25 points) and flying saucers (50 points) attack from the air.
- **Outer space:** After the bombing run the plane leaves for space and meets dragons with "exceptionally long, lethal tongues". They are worth 100 points, but players can avoid them by staying at the left edge of the screen.
- **Refuelling:** The plane meets a mother ship to refuel, then goes back to the target terrain at a higher speed.
- **The tunnel:** Players who get far enough reach a tunnel guarded by comet-like "tunnel killers", worth 100 points each.

The manual itself warns that "until you have considerable flying experience you will not reach the tunnel anyhow".[^ref-5] The player starts with five lives, and touching an enemy or the ground costs one.[^ref-1]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The ship carries two weapons. The laser fires straight ahead. Bombs keep the plane's forward momentum, so as Creative Computing put it, "they don't drop straight down".[^ref-6]

To use the **terrain generator**, the player connects paddles, chooses (T) at boot, and sets the height of the horizon line frame by frame with the paddle dial. Each frame can be saved or redrawn.[^ref-5] After the twenty-first frame the program asks the player to reboot and choose (U) for user terrain. The manual adds a rule: at least one third of the landscape must be level so the program has room to place the defence units.[^ref-5] Creative Computing noted that a player could make the game trivially easy by drawing 21 flat frames, adding that "although the temptation was great, we did not succumb."[^ref-6]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

*Creative Computing* covered Pegasus II in "Apple Arcade Games", a round-up in its August 1982 issue. Its software profile summed the game up as a "challenging, fast moving flying game with the option of user-definable terrain".[^ref-6] The reviewers praised the terrain option: "Pegasus II adds a unique touch found in very few games". They were less impressed by the presentation, which had "good, hires graphics, some color but not much in the way of sound effects". They still concluded that "Pegasus II is a challenging game and one which had our player panel coming back for hours."[^ref-6]

The game also sold well. *Softalk* reported that five programs reached its Top Thirty for the first time in November 1981, and the first one it named was "Pegasus II from On-Line Systems".[^ref-8] The same list credits the game to "Olaf Lubeck, On-Line Systems".[^ref-8]

### Modern Assessment

Pegasus II has had little modern critical attention. MobyGames shows no Moby Score and one player rating, an average of 3.2 out of 5.[^ref-1] Retro365's 2021 collector's article calls it "an excellent Scramble clone". The author describes it as "one of the first, if not the first Scramble variant available on personal computers" and "one of the very first computer games to feature a built-in level editor". Both are the blog's own judgements and have not been checked against other sources.[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

Olaf Lubeck got his first Apple in 1980, while he was still in the Navy, and wrote his first complete program on it in the spring of 1981.[^ref-11] He came to On-Line Systems through its advertising for programmers: his wife Pam saw one of the ads, and Ken Williams signed his maze game *Gobbler*.[^ref-11][^ref-4] Atari objected that *Gobbler* infringed its Pac-Man rights, and the game was pulled. The dispute carried on over its replacement, *[[1981 - Jawbreaker|Jawbreaker]]*, until the parties settled out of court.[^ref-11]

According to *Softline*, Jawbreaker's royalties are what got Lubeck started on this game. "Jawbreaker did better than I ever expected," he said. "I started getting royalty checks and I got all fired up and started work on Red Alert and Pegasus II."[^ref-11] (*Red Alert* came out through Broderbund.)[^ref-13] Retro365 places the game in its arcade context. Konami's *Scramble* was a hit in 1981 but had no official home computer ports, so clones began to appear. Pegasus II was one of them, released "later that year".[^ref-4]

### Production

Pegasus II was written in machine language. On-Line's advertising promised "Fast Machine Language", "Hi-Res animation and sound", and "Full Ship Movement" with control over the craft's speed and altitude.[^ref-9][^ref-6] By 1983 Lubeck was working with supercomputers at Los Alamos National Laboratory and programming games at home.[^ref-11][^ref-4] By then he had also written *[[1982 - Cannonball Blitz|Cannonball Blitz]]* and an Apple II version of *[[1981 - Frogger|Frogger]]* for Sierra.[^ref-11][^ref-14]

The Sierra Chest places the game in its "SierraVision" group. It records the engine as machine language and the US packaging as a small folder holding a single Apple II disk.[^ref-2][^ref-15]

## Legacy

Pegasus II's longest-lasting influence may be on John Romero. In an interview with David Lightbown about TEd, the tile editor Romero later wrote, Romero recalled first seeing "Pegasus ][" at Sierra College. He said he "thought that it was pretty cool that it has this tool built in to generate gameplay", and added: "It's funny to think now that this is one of the earliest games that let you generate mods."[^ref-7]

The game sits among Olaf Lubeck's early run of arcade-style games for On-Line Systems and Sierra. The Giant List of Classic Game Programmers credits him with *Gobbler*, *Jawbreaker*, *Pegasus II* and *Cannonball Blitz* for On-Line/Sierra, and *Red Alert* for Broderbund.[^ref-14][^ref-13] Wikipedia's list of Sierra Entertainment video games includes Pegasus II as a 1981 Apple II release from On-Line Systems.[^ref-18]

### Collections

Pegasus II has not appeared in any Sierra compilation, and it has never been sold digitally. The Apple II disk and a boot video from the 4am preservation project are on the Internet Archive.[^ref-19][^ref-20] MyAbandonware also lists it as a 1981 Apple II release.[^ref-21]
