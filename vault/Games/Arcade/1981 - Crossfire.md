# Crossfire

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Crossfire is a fast-paced arcade shooter developed by Jay Sullivan and published by Sierra On-Line (then known as On-Line Systems) beginning in 1981.[^ref-1] Inspired by the 1980 arcade game *Targ*, it challenges players to defend a city from waves of alien invaders attacking from all four directions on a grid-based playing field.[^ref-2][^ref-20] Initially released for the Apple II,[^ref-3] the game was later ported to Atari 8-bit computers, the VIC-20, the Commodore 64, the IBM PC and the IBM PCjr.[^ref-1]

The Data Driven Gamer blog singled out its smooth, responsive controls as unusual for an arcade-style Apple II game.[^ref-4] The Atari 8-bit version earned a Certificate of Merit in the "Best Arcade/Action Computer Game" category of the fourth annual Arcade Awards (the "Arkies"), announced in late 1982 and published in *Video* magazine in March 1983.[^ref-1][^ref-17]

## Story Summary

The narrative framework of Crossfire is straightforward but evocative of the era's arcade sensibilities. Aliens have invaded and are systematically taking over a city, destroying everything in their paths as they advance steadily across town.[^ref-6] The town's civilian population has been evacuated, and the player's military regiment has retreated, leaving the player completely alone in the city at the mercy of the extraterrestrial invaders.[^ref-6]

The aliens have surrounded the player's position, and laser fire flies from all directions as the enemies swarm inward with relentless aggression.[^ref-6] Though the player's movements are confined to the grid-like streets of the city, hope remains. Survival depends entirely on concentration—tracking where enemy shots originate and carefully planning escape routes while returning fire.[^ref-6] One moment of lost focus means getting "caught in the Cross Fire."[^ref-6]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Crossfire employs an innovative two-handed keyboard control scheme that anticipated the WASD-style controls that would become standard nearly fifteen years later.[^ref-4] Players use the IJKL keys for movement and ESDF for shooting in four directions, allowing simultaneous navigation and firing in independent directions.[^ref-1] MobyGames' trivia and Trixter's review of the PC booter version say the joystick fires only in the direction of travel, which favors keyboard players.[^ref-2][^ref-9] The Atari version's joystick is more flexible: its manual says "to fire, simultaneously push the button and toggle joystick in direction you want to fire," and the *Addison-Wesley Book of Atari Software 1984* notes that a player can stay stationary at an intersection and aim with the stick.[^ref-19][^ref-20]

*Softline*'s January 1982 review joked about the scheme: "The macho man will be temporarily gratified by the thought that it's a two-fisted game until he realizes that with one fist he'll be manipulating the E-S-D-F pyramid and with the other he'll have to cope with the I-J-K-L pyramid."[^ref-16] The Data Driven Gamer blog later observed: "Finally – an arcade style Apple II game with controls that are smooth and responsive!"[^ref-4]

### Structure and Progression

The game takes place on a 7×6 grid representing city streets, viewed from a top-down perspective.[^ref-8] Players begin each game with three ships and earn a fresh ship for every 5,000 points scored.[^ref-2] Ammunition is limited—players start with 35 shots, and this decreases by 5 each level until reaching a minimum of 15 shots.[^ref-1] When ammunition runs low (10 shots remaining), players must collect a fresh supply that appears on the grid;[^ref-8] *Softalk* likewise says new ammunition appears "when you are ten bullets from empty."[^ref-23] The same review says the aliens come "from three directions — later four."[^ref-23]

Four bonus crystals appear per level, each worth twice as much as the previous one (100, 200, 400, and 800 points).[^ref-8] A bonus object appears for every 12 missiles fired, and the player has six shots in which to run over it before it disappears.[^ref-19][^ref-2] The game employs a restriction where only one shot can be active at a time, requiring precise timing and positioning.[^ref-1]

Four visually distinct types of alien appear in each round; their behavior is the same, but they are worth 10, 20, 40 and 80 points respectively.[^ref-2] *Softalk* described each alien as having four lives, metamorphosing into its next form at its point of origin when shot.[^ref-24][^ref-23]

### Puzzles and Mechanics

While Crossfire is primarily a reflex-based action game rather than a puzzle game, it incorporates significant strategic elements. Players must balance aggressive pursuit of enemies with defensive positioning, managing limited ammunition while maximizing scoring opportunities from bonus crystals.[^ref-2]

"Crossfire starts out as an easy game of reflexes: shoot everything you can in all four directions," observed Trixter in a PC Booter review. "The more insects that get released into the grid, the harder it gets. After about five minutes of playing, you're intensely aware that your life hangs by a thread, and the entire world around you disappears as you concentrate on not merely blowing away the bad guys, but staying alive!"[^ref-9]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Crossfire was generally well received on release. *Softline* magazine called it "a new twist at arcade games with delightfully colorful beasties and smooth animation" and said it "offers hours of challenge and enjoyment for the dedicated and persevering gamester," though it faulted the lack of a pause button.[^ref-16][^ref-1] The review was decidedly mixed: it opened by calling Crossfire "a blight on the hallowed number 6502," found the two-handed controls frustrating, and said that clearing the first screen takes "several playing hours."[^ref-16] *BYTE* magazine called it "one of the most difficult and challenging arcade games to play," adding that "the reflexes take a long time to master, but, once you get the hang of it, it's addictive."[^ref-1][^ref-21]

*Softalk* magazine provided particularly effusive praise: "Critters come at you from four directions on a grid laid out like city blocks. Strategy and intense concentration required. Superb, smooth animation of a dozen pieces simultaneously. One of the great ones."[^ref-22] The French magazine *Tilt* rated the game 5/6 (UVList) in January 1983.[^ref-10]

*Softalk*'s full review in January 1982 called Crossfire "the best home-arcade game to cross our screens since Alien Rain," said it "offers much opportunity for strategy—far more than normal in a shoot-'em-up," and concluded: "With Crossfire and the recent Threshold, On-Line moves firmly into the same league as Sirius, Broderbund, Cavalier, and the like as a purveyor of outstanding home-arcade fun."[^ref-23]

Not all reviews were uniformly positive. *PC Magazine* found the IBM version merely "adequate, but nothing spectacular."[^ref-1] *Ahoy!* magazine was more critical of the VIC-20 version, asking "do we really need a bare bones grid shooting game (the VIC graphics are absolutely starved), with no character or atmosphere?"[^ref-1] *InfoWorld* was more positive about the IBM PCjr version, calling it "an engrossing waste of time."[^ref-1]

The *Addison-Wesley Book of Atari Software 1984* awarded the game a B+ rating, calling it "one of the best strategy shoot-'em-ups to come along in a long while."[^ref-1][^ref-20]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective assessments have been generally favorable. User "dmsph" on Abandonware DOS declared: "I think this game is much better than most of the games today! Agility and strategy are key."[^ref-5] The Data Driven Gamer blog concluded that "for its time, this was arguably the best arcade-at-home experience."[^ref-4]

## Development

### Origins

Crossfire was inspired by the arcade game *Targ*.[^ref-2] The *Addison-Wesley Book of Atari Software 1984* said it "has its roots in the arcade game, Targ," but noted that where *Targ* let players shoot only in their direction of travel, Crossfire let them stop at any intersection and shoot in any of four directions.[^ref-20] Retro365 adds that Sullivan drew on both *Targ* and *Space Invaders*, and that the game was first marketed as "Cross Fire."[^ref-18] On-Line Systems' January 1982 *Softline* advertisement spelled the name "CROSS FIRE" and billed it as "a unique new game by JAY SULLIVAN."[^ref-16] Jay Sullivan designed and programmed the original Apple II version, which Data Driven Gamer identifies as the original and, judging from footage, the most advanced of the three early versions.[^ref-4] The game was originally published under Sierra's earlier name, On-Line Systems, in a small folder before being re-released in a small SierraVision box.[^ref-6]

### Production

According to Retro365, Sullivan co-founded the Calsoft mail-order software business with Ken Williams and wrote Crossfire in machine language while mainly running that business, using the Apple II's Hi-Res graphics mode.[^ref-18] On-Line Systems' own advertising billed it as having "HI-RES graphics and sound, smooth quick animation," for any 48K Apple II at $29.95 on disk, and *Softline* praised its "smooth animation."[^ref-16]

**Development Credits:**[^ref-2][^ref-8]
- **Designer/Programmer (Apple II):** Jay Sullivan
- **Atari 8-bit Port:** Chris Iden[^ref-4][^ref-25]
- **Commodore 64 Port:** [[Chuck Benton]] (who also wrote *Softporn Adventure*) and Jay Sullivan[^ref-2][^ref-8][^ref-11]
- **IBM PC Port:** Jay Sullivan and Frank Randak[^ref-8]
- **VIC-20 Port:** "Gordon" (full name not given)[^ref-4]
- **Cover Artwork:** Chris Dellorco[^ref-2]
- **Commodore 64 Music:** Chuck Benton[^ref-11]

### Technical Achievements

*Softalk* specifically praised the game's "superb, smooth animation of a dozen pieces simultaneously."[^ref-22] The Commodore 64 version has SID background music by Chuck Benton; the High Voltage SID Collection's notes say it "is not a cover, but is definitely inspired" by Henry Mancini's "Peter Gunn" theme.[^ref-11]

### Technical Specifications

**Apple II Version:**[^ref-3][^ref-10]
- **Storage:** 5.25" diskette
- **Memory:** 48K (Apple II / II Plus, DOS 3.2 or 3.3)[^ref-16]
- **Input:** Keyboard (ESDF/IJKL controls), Joystick
- **Display:** Fixed screen 2D, top-down perspective

**Atari 8-bit Versions:**[^ref-1]
- **Cartridge Version:** Runs on 16K RAM, uses character mode graphics
- **Floppy Version:** Requires 32K RAM, uses bitmap mode graphics duplicating Apple II appearance
- **Cassette Version:** *BYTE* (December 1982) noted the Atari 400/800 version was also available on "disk, cassette, or cartridge"; the Atari manual likewise covers disk, ROM cartridge and cassette play[^ref-21][^ref-19][^ref-18]
- **Addison-Wesley listing (1984):** 32K, machine language, disk, $29.95; defeating all the aliens on level one takes 48 kills[^ref-20]

**IBM PC Version:**[^ref-1][^ref-2]
- **Format:** Self-booting disk
- **Display:** Color Graphics Adapter (CGA), composite mode colors match Apple II version
- **Storage:** 5.25" diskette

**IBM PCjr Version:**[^ref-1][^ref-5]
- **Format:** Cartridge
- **Graphics:** "More aesthetically pleasing colors" than PC version

**Commodore 64 Version:**[^ref-11]
- **Control:** Joystick (Port 2)
- **Players:** Lemon64's database lists 1 or 2, simultaneous; Wikipedia describes the game as single-player[^ref-11][^ref-1]
- **Audio:** SID music inspired by the "Peter Gunn" theme

### Technical Issues

One Abandonware DOS commenter, "Andy Panda," recalled a scoring bug: "There was a bug in the scoring when you reach a certain level, and when you reach that certain score you get 99 lives, instead of 4 extra lives, then game over."[^ref-5]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game was one of the very few Sierra titles re-released for the IBM PCjr, alongside the original *King's Quest*.[^ref-6]
- Both PC and PCjr bootable cartridge versions exist, with the PCjr version featuring different, more pleasing colors.[^ref-5]
- The Commodore 64 version's title music was later reused, with slightly different instruments, in the game *Ballonjagd* (1984).[^ref-11]
- One MyAbandonware commenter recalled the Apple II version as a "High priority game back in the software piracy trade game in the 80s."[^ref-9]

## Legacy

### Awards

- **Certificate of Merit** – "Best Arcade/Action Computer Game" at the 4th Annual Arcade Awards (Arkies), announced November 1982 and published March 1983 (Atari 8-bit version)[^ref-1][^ref-17]

### Collections

IBM is credited as a co-publisher of the PC and PCjr versions.[^ref-1][^ref-2] The game has been preserved across multiple abandonware and archival sites, including:
- Internet Archive (Apple II, Atari, MS-DOS versions)[^ref-3][^ref-7][^ref-13]
- MyAbandonware[^ref-9]
- ClassicReload (playable in browser)[^ref-14]

MobyGames lists it as included in the compilations *Familiar Favorites II: Jawbreaker – Crossfire* (1986) and *4 Game Pack: No.1* (1991).[^ref-2]

### Fan Projects

A Windows remake titled *Gridfire* was created for the Retro Remakes competition in 2006, bringing the classic gameplay to modern systems.[^ref-2]

### Related Publications

No dedicated strategy guides or hint books have been documented for Crossfire, though the game was featured in numerous magazine reviews and gaming columns of the era.

### Critical Perspective

User "Robotron 2084" on Lemon64 was cooler on the C64 version: "Crossfire is a no-frills reaction test from the stone age of C64 gaming featuring a groovy SID-cover of the 'Peter Gunn' theme." The High Voltage SID Collection entry is more careful, saying the tune "is not a cover, but is definitely inspired" by the Mancini theme.[^ref-11]

"This game is so simple yet so addictive," noted user bbagnall, while Jan Wiberg observed: "A game that truly lives up to its title! Not nice to be the shooting target of so many enemy ships which take several shots to destroy!"[^ref-11]
