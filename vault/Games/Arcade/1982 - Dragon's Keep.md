# Dragon's Keep

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

Dragon's Keep is a children's educational adventure game originally developed by SunnySide Soft and later published by Sierra On-Line[^ref-1]. SunnySide sold it for the Apple II in 1982; Sierra re-released it for the Apple II in 1983 as a "Hi-Res Learning" title, and ported it to the Atari 8-bit and Commodore 64 in 1984[^ref-19][^ref-14][^ref-20]. The game was designed in the same spirit as Sierra's Hi-Res Adventure series, including games like Mission: Asteroid and Mystery House, but was specifically tailored for young players ages seven and up[^ref-1]. The premise involves a magical dragon who has captured sixteen animals—including a dog, bird, fish, turtle, cat, squirrel, rabbit, pig, bear, monkey, cow, calf, hen, puppy, frog, and raccoon—that the player must locate and free from the dragon's domain[^ref-1].

What makes Dragon's Keep particularly significant in gaming history is its connection to [[Al Lowe]], who would later become famous as the creator of the Leisure Suit Larry series. This was the very first game ever made by Al Lowe, who had purchased an Apple II computer to learn how to program[^ref-4]; Retro365 instead says he bought it "initially to write software that could help make his job easier"[^ref-19]. Developed in the summer of 1982 with his wife Margaret Lowe and friends Mike and Rae Lynn MacChesney through their small software company Sunnyside Soft, the game caught the attention of Sierra On-Line founders Ken and Roberta Williams[^ref-4].

The game was designed to help children develop reading comprehension through a controlled vocabulary[^ref-5], along with map reading skills[^ref-14]. Rather than using a traditional text parser that might frustrate young players, Dragon's Keep employed a simple menu-driven interface where children could select actions from a short list of choices by pressing just two keys[^ref-1].

## Story Summary

In Dragon's Keep, a magical dragon is holding sixteen animals captive in and around its magic house[^ref-14][^ref-4]. The player takes on the role of a young adventurer whose mission is to explore the dragon's territory and rescue all of the captive creatures. The animals have been scattered throughout the dragon's domain, hidden in various locations that the player must discover through careful exploration[^ref-2].

The dragon serves as the primary antagonist, appearing at random throughout the game world. When the dragon is present in a location, the player cannot free any animals in that same picture[^ref-1]. This mechanic teaches young players patience and strategic thinking—they must wait for the dragon to leave before attempting a rescue. Notably, the dragon does not directly harm the player, making the game suitable for young children without frightening content[^ref-16][^ref-7].

The adventure world is described as "very small," and the animals are designed to be easily found by young players who look everywhere[^ref-2]. This deliberate design choice ensures that children experience success and encouragement rather than the frustration that more complex adventure games might cause. The game rewards thorough exploration and reading comprehension, as players must understand the options presented to make progress[^ref-5].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Dragon's Keep features an innovative simplified interface specifically designed for young children who might struggle with traditional adventure game text parsers[^ref-2]. Instead of typing commands, young adventurers simply choose their action from a short list of choices displayed on screen[^ref-2]. The controls are remarkably streamlined—children select options using only the space bar and return key[^ref-7].

Each screen presents just three command lines, making choices clear and manageable for beginning readers[^ref-4]. This choose-your-own-adventure style approach[^ref-8] suited children who could not yet type: Sierra's parent guide notes that "the vocabulary is controlled, the instructions are on the disk, and no typing skills are required"[^ref-14].

### Structure and Progression

The game takes place entirely within the dragon's domain, where players must systematically search all locations to find the sixteen hidden animals[^ref-1]. The adventure world was deliberately designed to be compact so that young players would not become lost or overwhelmed[^ref-2].

The game includes an interactive color and music exercise at startup, providing an engaging introduction for young players before the main adventure begins[^ref-8]. Players navigate through the dragon's house and surrounding areas, encountering each of the sixteen animals in various hiding spots.

- **Dragon's House Interior:** The primary location where several animals are kept hidden
- **Surrounding Areas:** Outdoor locations around the dragon's lair containing additional captive animals

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay mechanic involves locating animals and determining when it is safe to free them. If the dragon appears in the same picture as an animal, the player cannot rescue that creature and must return later when the dragon has moved elsewhere[^ref-1]. This simple mechanic teaches young players about timing and consequences without harsh penalties.

Some situations require careful consideration of safety. For example, when encountering a sleeping bear, the game warns: "That is not a good idea! Bears can hurt you. Maybe you can help him without waking him"[^ref-8]. These moments teach children to think about their choices rather than simply selecting every available option.

The game included a large map with peel-off stickers as part of its packaging, allowing children to track their progress in rescuing animals[^ref-7]. This physical component enhanced the educational value and provided a tangible record of achievement.

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

*Computer Gaming World* reviewed the Apple II version in its July–August 1983 issue. Bob Proctor noted that the game was "originally published by Sunnyside Software but now being marketed by Sierra On-line" at $29.95, and that it was "designed especially for second-graders". He judged it "very well done" and concluded: "It is highly recommended"[^ref-16]. Jay Lucas also reviewed it at length in *InfoWorld* in 1983, opening with a complaint about the limited vocabularies of parser adventures and praising how the game "cleverly avoids" that problem[^ref-18].

Dragon's Keep received attention as part of Sierra's Hi-Res Learning line of educational games that aimed to "sharpen children's thinking by combining the basic skill building exercises they need with the fun they deserve"[^ref-5]. The game was marketed as helping children develop reading comprehension through its controlled vocabulary approach[^ref-5].

A user review on MobyGames titled "Hours of fun at an easy reading level" praised the game for its accessibility to young children[^ref-1]. The reviewer Katakis noted that children are given simple options that can be selected just by pressing two keys, making it ideal for beginning readers and young computer users[^ref-1].

Personal testimonials from players who experienced the game during childhood indicate fond memories. An anonymous commenter on the Gaming After 40 playthrough wrote in 2014: "This was my favorite computer game when I was in elementary school"[^ref-8]. Another user, Max West, reminisced: "I played this game in 1st grade and it was fun!"[^ref-10].

### Modern Assessment

Sierra Chest records Dragon's Keep as the first game ever made by Al Lowe[^ref-4]. The Gaming After 40 blog published a detailed playthrough of the Atari version in 2013, noting that "this isn't a particularly challenging game"[^ref-8]. In the comments, Rowan Lipkovits placed it alongside Troll's Tale, Mickey's Space Adventure and Winnie the Pooh in the Hundred Acre Wood, which he called "basically re-skins of this game"[^ref-8].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **MobyGames:** 3.6/5 (user reviews)[^ref-1]
- **My Abandonware:** 3/5[^ref-10]
- **Glitchwave:** 2.04/5 (3 ratings)[^ref-11]

## Development

### Origins

Dragon's Keep originated in the summer of 1982. Al Lowe, then a music teacher, had purchased an Apple II computer to learn how to program[^ref-4][^ref-18]. Collaborating with his wife Margaret Lowe and friends Mike and Rae Lynn MacChesney, Lowe developed what would become his first published video game through their small software company, Sunnyside Soft[^ref-4]. Lowe programmed it in Applesoft BASIC, and SunnySide sold copies for a few months, out of the Lowes' home, before the deal with Sierra[^ref-19].

The educational game was designed specifically for young children, reflecting the team's interest in creating software that could help kids develop reading and comprehension skills[^ref-4]. Lowe utilized Penguin Software's Graphics Magician tool kit for the game's visual presentation[^ref-8].

### Production

The game was discovered at the San Francisco Applefest in late 1982, where it caught the attention of Sierra On-Line founders Ken and Roberta Williams at SunnySide's small booth[^ref-4][^ref-18]. *Softalk*'s report on the show pictured Margaret Lowe of Sunnyside Soft demonstrating the company's other title, Bop-A-Bet[^ref-17]. This discovery proved pivotal—not only would Sierra agree to rerelease Dragon's Keep, but it also launched Al Lowe's career in the game industry[^ref-4].

It was agreed that Dragon's Keep would be rereleased by Sierra and also converted for the Commodore 64 and Atari 400/800, as well as for the IBM PC/PCjr with enhanced graphics[^ref-4]. This multi-platform approach reflected Sierra's growing commitment to reaching the widest possible audience across the emerging home computer market.

### Technical Achievements

Dragon's Keep's pictures were made with The Graphics Magician, Penguin Software's graphics tool kit[^ref-8][^ref-19]. A Gaming After 40 commenter noted that Troll's Tale, Mickey's Space Adventure, and Winnie the Pooh in the Hundred Acre Wood reuse the same engine[^ref-8].

The Gaming After 40 playthrough observed that "Dragon's Keep does not appear to use any of Sierra's standard engines," though commenter Rowan Lipkovits replied that "Sierra was just in the awkward intermediary stage between Mystery House text parser games and King's Quest SCI parser games"[^ref-8].

### Technical Issues

The IBM PC/PCjr version is unconfirmed. The © 1984 multi-platform manual has IBM PC/PCjr loading instructions and credits[^ref-20], and period mail-order listings and ads mention it, but no disk or box has surfaced[^ref-13][^ref-1].

Forum discussions reveal uncertainty about the PC port. As one researcher noted: "That's not proof of existence; there have been many manuals published with instructions for ports that never surfaced"[^ref-13]. Another contributor suggested: "I wouldn't be surprised if this was only released for the PCjr and died with that system"[^ref-13]. After the original submitter posted 1984–86 evidence (1984 mail-order ads, a 1985 book listing it for the PCjr and 1986 graphics-card ads), approver Sciere restored the PC entry: "So, until further notice, I'm going to let it back in." Self-described PCjr aficionado Trixter added: "Let's hope we eventually find this one"[^ref-13].

The Gaming After 40 blog noted one specific issue: the victory message blinks by too quickly for young readers to fully comprehend, somewhat undermining the game's reading education goals[^ref-8].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Inconsistent Dragon Size:** The dragon changes size dramatically throughout the game, appearing in different scales in various screens[^ref-8]
- **Interactive Startup:** An interactive color and music exercise appears at startup before the main game begins[^ref-8]
- **Al Lowe's First Game:** Dragon's Keep holds the distinction of being the very first game created by Al Lowe, who would later achieve fame with the Leisure Suit Larry series[^ref-4]
- **Discovery Story:** The game was discovered by Ken and Roberta Williams at Applefest 1982, directly leading to Al Lowe's career at Sierra[^ref-4]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

Dragon's Keep became part of Sierra's Hi-Res Learning line, positioning it alongside other educational titles designed to make computing accessible and beneficial for children[^ref-5]. The game's most significant commercial impact was arguably indirect—it served as the launching pad for Al Lowe's career at Sierra On-Line[^ref-4].

Its menu-driven approach carried over into Sierra's next children's adventures: one Gaming After 40 commenter described Troll's Tale, Mickey's Space Adventure and Winnie the Pooh in the Hundred Acre Wood as "basically re-skins of this game"[^ref-8], and Sierra Chest notes the same style of play in Troll's Tale and Winnie the Pooh[^ref-4].

### Collections

Dragon's Keep was originally published independently by Sunnyside Soft before being picked up by Sierra On-Line for wider distribution[^ref-4][^ref-16]. The game was part of Sierra's early 1980s educational software catalog and was included in their Hi-Res Learning series[^ref-11][^ref-14]; Giant Bomb files it under "Sierra's Hi-Res franchise"[^ref-12].

### Fan Projects

The game has been preserved through various retro gaming archives and emulation efforts. Internet Archive hosts several versions of the game, including Apple II and Atari 8-bit disk images[^ref-6][^ref-2]. A 4am crack of the Apple II version, designated crack number 986, was archived in January 2017[^ref-6].

### Related Publications

- **Parent Guide (1983):** Sierra's Apple II parent guide (DKD-001), © 1983[^ref-14]
- **Game Manual (1984):** Sierra's later multi-platform manual (DKD-101), © 1984, with loading instructions and credits for the Apple II, Atari, Commodore 64 and the disputed IBM PC/PCjr version[^ref-20]
- **In-Game Map:** Large map with peel-off stickers included with retail packaging[^ref-7]

### Critical Perspective

The game itself is deliberately small—"the adventure world is very small"[^ref-1]—and its larger significance lies in what it led to: Sierra hiring Al Lowe, who would go on to design the Leisure Suit Larry series[^ref-4].

It came during what a Gaming After 40 commenter called Sierra's intermediary stage between the Mystery House text parser games and the King's Quest parser games[^ref-8]. Its menu interface was noticed at the time as a possible new direction for adventures, though it was not the first menu-driven adventure[^ref-18].
