# Mr. Cool

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

Mr. Cool is a Q*bert-inspired action game designed by Peter Oliphant and published by Sierra On-Line in 1983 for the Atari 8-bit computers and Commodore 64, with Apple II and IBM PC versions by John Redekopp[^ref-10][^ref-36]. Oliphant wanted to make "a game like Q*bert, but better", answering what he saw as the original arcade hit's repetitiveness[^ref-10]. Players control Mr. Cool, an ice cube, who hops diagonally around a seven-tiered pyramid of hot plates, changing every plate to the same color while dodging fireballs and hot springs[^ref-33][^ref-35][^ref-15].

The game was part of Sierra On-Line's early catalog[^ref-32], and the Atari version was issued as a ROM cartridge under Sierra's SierraVision label[^ref-8][^ref-31]. *Softline* called it "the first Q*Bert clone" and "the best new-on-any-machine arcader in a while from this top game publisher"[^ref-33]. Other reviews were more mixed, so its contemporary reception is best described as mixed to positive[^ref-34][^ref-35].

## Story Summary

Mr. Cool has a slight premise. The Atari manual promises that "Adventures have never been so hot or so dangerous!!": the ice cube hero cools off sizzling hot plates while fireballs and "hot and cold running springs" try to dissolve him, and "The everlasting fire is waiting below"[^ref-8]. *Softline* described him as "a cute little (you got it) ice cube with feet and eyes" who drops onto the top plate "from a freezer presumably hidden inside the top of your monitor"[^ref-33].

SierraChest's write-up says Mr. Cool is "so cool, he can make fireballs frosty", while the Hot Springs "resent this uppity ice cube, Mr. Cool, invading their private pyro-playground"[^ref-31]. One Lemon64 user comment sums it up as "Mr. Cool, icecubeman, has gone to hell (or something like that, I quess [sic])!"[^ref-20].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Mr. Cool is a single-player game[^ref-20] controlled with a joystick. The Atari manual says to plug the joystick into Port #1 and use diagonal movements to move Mr. Cool[^ref-8]; the Commodore 64 version uses Port 2[^ref-20]. *Creative Computing* noted that the diagonal joystick action "for some people who tried the game, posed a coordination problem"[^ref-34]. The Addison-Wesley guide found that the game "exhibits good control when played with 'loose' joysticks, but responds poorly with stiff joystick control"[^ref-35].

### Structure and Progression

The object is to match the color of the plates to the color of the top border of the playing field before the round ends, and the player starts with four lives[^ref-8]. At first one landing per plate is enough, but later rounds need several landings per plate[^ref-33][^ref-35]. Each level has 15 rounds, and the levels repeat endlessly at increasing speed[^ref-33][^ref-34][^ref-26]. Each round awards a bonus based on the time left on a countdown clock, and if time runs out Mr. Cool "sweats himself out of existence"[^ref-33]. Extra cubes are awarded every 20,000 points[^ref-26][^ref-33].

### Hazards and Supercool Time

Fireballs patrol every row except the top one[^ref-8][^ref-33]. Four hot springs drop in from the top and bounce downward; the manual has them appearing from Round 2 of Level 1[^ref-8]. Beneath the pyramid is a furnace that is lethal if Mr. Cool misses a plate[^ref-35]. Once per round, pressing the joystick button starts "Super Cool Time", which the manual says "lasts 15 seconds"[^ref-8][^ref-33] (*Softline* estimated "twelve to fifteen seconds"[^ref-33]). During it, fireballs turn into snowballs and the springs lose their tails, so Mr. Cool can absorb them for points or an extra cube[^ref-8]. From the end of the sixth round of Level 1, the plates are invisible until they're hit, and they won't support Mr. Cool in Supercool mode[^ref-33]. The Addison-Wesley guide noted that "It does require some strategy, for like Pac-Man you must take the offensive for brief periods of time"[^ref-35].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| Softline (Sep–Oct 1983) | Positive | "Although Mr. Cool lacks Q*Bert's Escheresque assault on the eyes, it's a no less addicting game"[^ref-33] |
| Creative Computing (Jan 1984) | Mixed | Software Profile summary: "Playable until Q-Bert arrives."[^ref-34] |
| Addison-Wesley Book of Atari Software 1984 | C | "In sum, it is a well-made clone."[^ref-35] |

*Softline* was enthusiastic, closing that "Mr. Cool deserves to be a sizzling hit for Sierra On-Line"[^ref-33]. In *Creative Computing*, Arthur Leyenberger wrote: "This game resembles the popular arcade game Q-Bert, but without the beautifully colored screen. That is not to say that the game is not fun to play or challenging enough." He found it "very difficult above the second level" but concluded, "for some reason I like Mr. Cool"[^ref-34]. The *Addison-Wesley Book of Atari Software 1984* gave it an overall C and a D+ for value for money. It called the graphics "simple lines on a two-dimensional plane, more of what you would expect in a VCS game"[^ref-35].

### Modern Assessment

Lemon64 user ratings range from 4/10 to 10/10. One commenter called it "A weak Q*bert clone that's good for about a minute or two of your time"[^ref-20]. MobyGames user Katakis was kinder: "As Sierra's take on Q*Bert, the game is excellent and it mimics the Gottlieb game very well"[^ref-21]. MobyGames lists a critics' average of 56%, based on two ratings[^ref-21].

## Development

### Origins

Peter Oliphant conceived Mr. Cool after finishing *Wall War*, his first original game for Sierra On-Line[^ref-10][^ref-36]. *Softline* introduced it as Oliphant's follow-up, "Fresh from his creative triumph with Wall War"[^ref-33]. According to Oliphant, he finished the Atari 8-bit version three weeks after he first started thinking about it[^ref-10].

### Production

Oliphant programmed the Atari 8-bit and Commodore 64 versions. John Redekopp wrote the Apple II and IBM PC ports[^ref-10][^ref-26][^ref-21], and the Apple II version needs a 48K Apple ][+ or later[^ref-4]. The game was written in machine language[^ref-35][^ref-31]. The Atari version was an 8K ROM cartridge for the Atari 400, 800 and 1200[^ref-33][^ref-8], although the Addison-Wesley guide lists its requirement as 16K[^ref-35]. The Commodore 64 version was also a cartridge[^ref-33]. *Softline* listed the Apple and Atari versions at $33.33 and the C64 cartridge at $37.78[^ref-33]. Lemon64 credits the box art to Scott Comstock[^ref-20].

### Technical Notes

The IBM PC version was a self-booting disk[^ref-10]. The preserved booter images (CGA and CGA+PCjr) are 180K disks labelled 1984[^ref-5]. Sierra protected it with Formaster Copylock, which it also used on the IBM PC booter versions of King's Quest and on Troll's Tale. That protection "did not work at first with a DMA-less PC like the PCjr. and many early Tandy 1000 models" and was speed-sensitive, "breaking on faster 286 machines"[^ref-25].

## Legacy

Mr. Cool is an early Sierra arcade release and one of the period's Q*bert-style games: James Hague's list of classic game programmers files it as "Q*bert-like"[^ref-36]. It survives on the Internet Archive in Atari 8-bit, Apple II and IBM PC disk images[^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-5][^ref-7], and it is listed on MyAbandonware[^ref-23] and Lemon64[^ref-20].
