# 3D Ultra Lionel Traintown

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 11, 2026</small>

## Overview

3D Ultra Lionel Traintown is a third-person railroading puzzle game developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by Sierra Attractions for Windows PC in 1999[^ref-1][^ref-25]. It was licensed by Lionel, LLC[^ref-1][^ref-25]. A review of the 2000 Deluxe edition describes how the developers, "inspired by the once popular Lionel toy trains," set out to make a place where trains are the main attraction[^ref-2]. Sierra announced it in September 1999 alongside Radio Control Racers and Cool Pool as part of a 3-D Ultra trio priced at around $19.95[^ref-26]. Unlike earlier entries in Sierra's 3D Ultra series that focused on quick bursts of arcade fun, Traintown was a departure into puzzle-oriented gameplay, and one retrospective calls it "a worthy entry into the 3D Ultra series"[^ref-3].

The game features over 70 assignments across seven play environments, including a desert, the arctic, a living room, and the moon[^ref-1][^ref-25]. Players control locomotives to deliver cargo from one place to another, and each level has its own narrated scenario rather than aimless train movement[^ref-2]. The jobs range from simple deliveries to tasks like clearing avalanches off the tracks before a passenger train arrives[^ref-4]. The game also has an educational side, with math, word and memory puzzles built into some of its jobs[^ref-1].

The game won Computer Family Entertainment Title of the Year from the Academy of Interactive Arts and Sciences at the 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards, held on May 11, 2000[^ref-1][^ref-23][^ref-24].

## Story Summary

3D Ultra Lionel Traintown takes place in a whimsical world where the player works their way up the railroad career ladder, "from Caboose Washer to Whistle Blower"[^ref-7]. The game presents various scenarios through narrated missions that challenge players to solve railroad-related problems across themed environments.

The jobs take place in locations including Oak Valley, Cactus Valley, The Backyard, North Pole, Living Room, and Planet X[^ref-8]. Each environment presents its own challenges, from the familiarity of a living room train set to railroading on the moon[^ref-1].

One recurring storyline is the "Tourist Project," an episodic series of jobs where Oak Valley's mayor attempts to build a Roman Colosseum to attract tourists[^ref-9]. Each episode introduces obstacles facing construction, including one-way tracks, construction workers going on strike, and hoodlums scattering cars around the map. This storyline carries across multiple difficulty levels.

The game also features the "School Daze" jobs, where players form math equations using flat cars with giant numbers, progressing from addition and subtraction to multiplication and division; TV Tropes describes the series reaching fourth-grade problems[^ref-9], while Wikipedia gives the overall range of the equation jobs as kindergarten through fifth grade[^ref-1]. Another recurring mission type is the "Little Sister" scenario, where the player's sibling has scattered train cars and players must organize them by type as they come down the track[^ref-9].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

A 2010 forum review of the Deluxe edition notes that the game, though marketed as 3D, technically functions as a 2D side-scroller, and that it controls more like a virtual model railroad than train simulation games like Railroad Tycoon or Train Simulator[^ref-10]. Gamezilla's contemporary review likewise called it "not really a 3D environment"[^ref-22]. Players use the mouse to manage their trains: each engine has three speeds in forward and reverse, set with a throttle, and cars are uncoupled by clicking between them[^ref-22]. The mouse controls can occasionally be awkward, particularly when judging forward and backward directions on curved tracks with long trains[^ref-3].

A 2024 GameFAQs user review of the Deluxe edition praised the largely player-controlled pacing and relaxing atmosphere, but criticized the lack of a checkpoint feature, which makes failing late in a long mission frustrating[^ref-11].

### Structure and Progression

The game features six difficulty levels, known as "job rosters," each containing 11 standard jobs plus a 12th job that unlocks after completing the previous eleven[^ref-1]. Gamezilla counted seventy-two missions in total, including six tutorials[^ref-22].

**Job Rosters (Difficulty Levels):**[^ref-8]
- **Tutorial Jobs**
- **Caboose Washer** – Beginner
- **Hobo Bouncer** – Average
- **Grease Monkey** – Intermediate
- **Fire Stoker** – Very Hard
- **Whistle Blower** – Extreme

Each difficulty level's "final job" typically introduces a unique mechanic specific to that level, such as loads costing money or objectives coming in stages, and usually awards the player with a unique animation upon completion[^ref-9].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The core gameplay involves picking up and delivering loads via different freight and passenger cars[^ref-1]. Players must manage track repairs, coupling and decoupling cars, refueling, and track switching[^ref-14]. More specialized jobs include guiding automated commuter trains with semaphores and fixing train tracks and switches[^ref-9].

Beyond basic railroad operations, the game incorporates various puzzle types[^ref-1]:

- **Math Equations:** Moving numbered freight cars to create valid addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division equations
- **Memory Matching:** Timed matching of cargo pairs[^ref-8]
- **Pick-Up Sticks**
- **Hangman**
- **Tower of Hanoi**
- **Anagram Scramble:** Unscrambling the letters of a word[^ref-8]

The game features over 30 different Lionel model trains with animations[^ref-3][^ref-25], including models such as the Union Pacific EMD SW1500 switcher, Atchison Topeka and Santa Fe Railway F3A diesel locomotive, 2-8-0 steam locomotive, and 1950s passenger railcar[^ref-1].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Gamezilla's Christine and Mike Messersmith gave the game a score of **89** in a review posted on 27 March 2000[^ref-22]. They praised the smoke effects, "ultra smooth" animation, and the "simple, yet intuitive control interface," and wrote that they "struggled with giving this game a score in the 90's, but since it may not appeal to players in all genres, we both happily give this game a score of 89"[^ref-22].

The Academy of Interactive Arts and Sciences honored it as Family Entertainment Title of the Year at the 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards[^ref-6][^ref-23].

A description of the Deluxe edition reproduced on MyAbandonware calls it potentially "everything both a parent and a child could want," and says that "though its approach is low-key, it's also very educational"[^ref-14].

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives have been largely positive. Collection Chamber described it as "insanely addictive. Like the best kind of puzzle games, it's easy to understand but will often stump you on many occasions. A worthy entry into the 3D Ultra series and highly recommended"[^ref-3].

A 2024 GameFAQs user review of the Deluxe edition gave it 6 out of 10, with the reviewer noting "Not what I thought, but I liked it"[^ref-11].

The game keeps nostalgic appeal among those who played it as children. One GOG Dreamlist user described it as "a childhood classic that I never completed. I would love the chance to play it again"[^ref-12]. The author of the IGN walkthrough wrote that it "became my favorite train simulation game"[^ref-8].

**Aggregate Scores:**
- **Gamezilla:** 89 (contemporary review)[^ref-22]
- **Metacritic:** 6.0/10 (4 user ratings)[^ref-7]
- **MyAbandonware (Deluxe edition):** 4.41/5 (46 votes)[^ref-14]
- **MMO Central Forums (Deluxe edition):** 9/10 (single review)[^ref-10]

## Development

### Origins

3D Ultra Lionel Traintown came from [[Dynamix]] during the late 1990s, when the 3D Ultra series had mostly offered arcade-style pinball, pool and minigolf games. Collection Chamber describes Traintown as a departure, with the developers "trying to shake up the formula"[^ref-3].

The game was licensed by Lionel, LLC[^ref-1][^ref-25].

### Production

Development took place at [[Dynamix|Dynamix, Inc.]], with Sierra On-Line publishing under its casual game brand Sierra Attractions[^ref-1].

The game's opening sequence incorporated video clips from existing Lionel video titles: "The History of Lionel Trains," "Toy Trains & Christmas Part 1," and "The Making of the Lionel Scale Hudson"[^ref-15].

**Development Credits:**[^ref-1]
- **Composer:** Christopher Stevens

### Technical Issues

- An installation error with track01.cue invalid data (21FC) has been reported by multiple users[^ref-3]
- MyAbandonware's install notes for the Deluxe edition recommend Windows 2000 or Windows XP compatibility mode[^ref-14]

CodeWeavers lists the Deluxe edition as "Runs Great" under CrossOver on both macOS (last tested with CrossOver 24.0.3) and Linux (last tested with CrossOver 25.1.0)[^ref-17].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- The game was known as simply "TrainTown" in European releases, according to the Sound Effects Wiki[^ref-16]; MobyGames lists "Mini Train" as an alternate (French) title[^ref-25]
- The user-submitted GOG Dreamlist entry states that a Sega Dreamcast port was planned but cancelled; no other source found confirms this[^ref-12]
- Cheat codes can be activated using the pipe key; using cheats results in a red check instead of a blue ribbon in the Deluxe version[^ref-8]
- The phrase "Cheaters don't prosper, ya know!" appears in relation to the cheat code system[^ref-8]
- The Deluxe edition let players post finished layouts on the Internet for others to download; the layout-swapping website is no longer active[^ref-6][^ref-10]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures are publicly available. Sierra announced the game at a price of around $19.95[^ref-26]. A 2010 forum reviewer said used copies of the Deluxe edition sold for about $60, calling it "probably the rarest game I have"[^ref-10]. One O Gauge Forum member recalls buying the Deluxe edition at Best Buy in 2000[^ref-18].

### Awards

- **Computer Family Entertainment Title of the Year** – Academy of Interactive Arts and Sciences, 3rd Annual Interactive Achievement Awards (May 11, 2000)[^ref-1][^ref-23][^ref-24]

### Collections

The game was released under the Sierra Attractions casual gaming label, announced together with 3-D Ultra Radio Control Racers and 3-D Ultra Cool Pool[^ref-26].

### Sequels and Related Games

The Deluxe version, released in 2000, was an enhanced release adding 50 new missions and a new track creation system[^ref-6]. Lionel later licensed other train games, including Lionel Trains: On Track for the Nintendo DS[^ref-1].

### Critical Perspective

3D Ultra Lionel Traintown combined puzzle gameplay, educational jobs and the Lionel license, and showed that the 3D Ultra line could move beyond arcade games into puzzle gaming[^ref-3]. Its Family Entertainment award[^ref-23] and the fond memories players still share online[^ref-12][^ref-18] suggest it left a lasting impression on the families who played it.
