# Maximum Pool

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

Maximum Pool is a pool simulation video game developed by Dynamix and published by Sierra On-Line under their Sierra Sports label in 2000[^ref-1]. It was released for Windows, Macintosh and Sega Dreamcast[^ref-5]. The game is part of the 3D Ultra series and serves as the successor to the 1999 game Cool Pool[^ref-1]. GameSpot's Stephen Poole wrote that "Dynamix's 3D Ultra Group clearly set out to make Maximum Pool a fast, friendly, and inviting pool simulation"[^ref-16], and GameZone found it "an entertaining and challenging experience for newbie and casual gamers alike"[^ref-3].

The game features an entertaining mix of classic and novel pool games, offering a total of 12 types with several table variations[^ref-4]. At the Dreamcast launch, Senior Producer Randy Thompson said: "We're extremely excited to be one of the first video games in history to support online play between Dreamcast, Windows, and Macintosh players"[^ref-5]. He added that the game "shows the flexibility of the SegaNet network and how well designed it is for multiple platforms and a variety of game types"[^ref-5].

The game includes seven classic pool games and five unlockable "unique pool games" that add a twist to the standard fare[^ref-5]. IGN wrote that despite its "colorful, family oriented presentation, you won't find a better simulation of the table sport on shelves, with the possible exception of Interplay's soon to be released Virtual Pool 3"[^ref-7].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

According to Sierra, "artfully rendered tables and excellent ball physics create accurate ball motion, jump shots and realistic angles"[^ref-5]. The game offers multiple levels of difficulty and options such as auto-aim to ease players into the experience[^ref-2]. Players can adjust the viewing angle by holding the Alt key for hidden views or right-clicking anywhere on the table to focus the view on specific areas[^ref-9].

The cue control system offers two primary methods: a pull-and-release mechanism or manual pushing to control shot power[^ref-9]. The target ball system features a red bull's-eye indicator that assists players in aiming[^ref-9]. The difficulty menu offers Easy, Medium and Hard, which control how far the target ball can move from the cue ball; a new Expert mode, enabled in the preferences, removes the target ball and guidelines entirely[^ref-11]. The manual cue stick control method was noted to cause crashes on Windows XP and Windows 7 systems[^ref-9].

### Traditional Pool Games

Maximum Pool offers seven classic games: Basic Pocket Billiards, Carom Billiards, Cutthroat, Eight Ball, Nine Ball, Snooker and Rotation[^ref-11]. The snooker and carom billiards tables differ because of the nature of those games, but the same blue table is used for the other five classic games, and all the tables are apparently the same size[^ref-16].

### Maximum Cool Games

Beyond traditional pool, Maximum Pool features five "cool games," which can be played on standard or "cool" tables and feature unique rules and often-unorthodox ball physics[^ref-16]:

**24 Cents**: The balls are worth coin values, and the winner is the first player to reach exactly 24 cents or the first to go over 50 cents[^ref-3].

**Chameleon Ball**: Balls change colour when struck by the cue ball; the goal is to sink balls of your own colour, and you must hit an opponent's ball twice to turn it to your colour[^ref-16].

**Mad Bomber**: Played on a cross-shaped table with a centre pocket[^ref-3]. A struck ball loses value until it becomes a time bomb that must be pocketed within five seconds, or it explodes[^ref-16].

**Poker Pool**: Two racks of balls are marked as playing cards, and players gather and discard the contents of their hand as they sink the balls[^ref-3].

**Rocket Ball**: Several "turbo-injected" balls zoom around the table when struck; if a rocket ball drops, you lose your turn and your opponents earn double points on their next shot[^ref-16].

These modes drew praise, with GameSpot noting that "these quirky diversions potentially serve to give Maximum Pool a long life on your hard drive"[^ref-16].

### AI Opponents and Multiplayer

Maximum Pool features five computer opponents (two women, two men and a dog), seen and heard in video clips that play in circular windows[^ref-16]. GameZone noted that the five opponents included "one dog, named Buddy"[^ref-3]. The others are Amy, Jim, Brad and Linda, and each opponent's experience level can be set from Novice to Expert[^ref-11].

Multiplayer options include:
- **Hot Seat**: 1–4 players on the same computer[^ref-2]
- **Internet Play**: Cross-platform play against Dreamcast, PC and Macintosh players[^ref-5]
- **Same/Split-Screen**: Listed by MobyGames among the Windows version's multiplayer options[^ref-2]

GameZone called the Internet-play options "smooth and well-designed (there's already ladder support for rankings)"[^ref-3].

## Development

### Origins

Maximum Pool was developed by Dynamix's 3D Ultra Group[^ref-16] as the successor to Cool Pool[^ref-1]. IGN described it as "an overall enhanced version of the undeservedly overlooked 3D Ultra Cool Pool"[^ref-7]. GameZone noted that the developers "could have settled with making a game whose only major selling point was that it allowed for online play, but they chose to add features and make every feature count"[^ref-3].

### Production

The game credits 66 people[^ref-2]. Key roles included:

- **Executive Producer**: Susan Folden
- **Producer**: Randy Thompson (described as senior producer for Dynamix in Sierra's press release[^ref-5])
- **Lead Programmer**: Jon Milnes
- **Game Design**: Rhett Anderson, who is also credited for the physics and graphics engine[^ref-2]

### Technical Achievements

IGN praised its "brilliantly modeled physics"[^ref-7].

The game supported Internet play between PC, Macintosh and Dreamcast players, using Sega's SegaNet network for the Dreamcast version[^ref-5].

The Macintosh version supports Mac OS 8.5.1 to 9.2.2; OS X compatibility was added by the v1.06 update, running up to Mac OS X 10.5[^ref-19]. The game is compatible with G5 Macintosh computers and Mac OS X 10.5.8 Leopard, but not Lion[^ref-30].

### System Requirements

**Windows Version**:
- Operating System: Windows 95/98[^ref-5]
- Processor: 166 MHz[^ref-4]
- RAM: 16 MB[^ref-4]
- Video: 4 MB[^ref-4]
- Suggested Retail Price: $29.99[^ref-5] (GameSpot's review cited a "modest $19.99 price tag"[^ref-16])

**Macintosh Version**[^ref-19]:
- Processor: 233 MHz G3
- RAM: 32 MB available
- Operating System: Mac OS 8.5.1 to 9.2.2 (OS X compatibility added by v1.06)
- Storage: 130 MB of hard drive space
- Display: 800x600, 16-bit colour; 4x CD-ROM drive

**Dreamcast Version**:
- Online play by modem, up to four players[^ref-26]

### Version History

- **v1.06 (Macintosh)**: update that added Mac OS X compatibility[^ref-19][^ref-30]

### Critical Analysis

IGN's Scott Steinberg was enthusiastic, writing that "Sharp graphics, brilliantly modeled physics, and a pick up and play interface come together in a wonderful package that'll evoke more oohs, ahhs, and pearly white grins than a behind the back masse shot"[^ref-7].

GameZone's Gil Alexander Shif called Maximum Pool "surprisingly good," emphasizing that the developers "chose to add features and make every feature count"[^ref-3]. The review highlighted the game's appeal to both "newbie and casual gamers," and noted that the AI "runs on a variety of settings wide enough to keep most players racking 'em up game after game"[^ref-3].

GameSpot's Stephen Poole offered a more measured assessment, acknowledging that "Maximum Pool's interface can prove to be very cumbersome, but if you can learn to tolerate how it plays, it's a great deal"[^ref-16]. The review criticized the shot-setup interface but praised the multiplayer options and the cool games[^ref-16].

More critical voices included GameSpot's Jeff Gerstmann, reviewing the Dreamcast version, who stated that "Maximum Pool does the job, albeit without any bells or whistles" and described it as "a standard pool game with overly simplified control"[^ref-24].

The critic site Antagonist, which scored the Dreamcast version 87, captured the game's polarizing nature: "Simplistic, annoying, and suffers from quirky controls and poor audio. It's also a hell of a lot of fun to play"[^ref-13].

### Modern Assessment

DreamcastLive.net user reviewers called it "something of a hidden gem in the Dreamcast's online lineup" and "the perfect chill online multiplayer game"[^ref-26].

The game holds a 4.3/5 rating on DreamcastLive.net from three reviews; the site lists its online servers as hosted by Dreamcast-Talk[^ref-26]. On Glitchwave, it holds a 2.04/5.0 rating from only three ratings[^ref-27].

Some Amazon reviewers were particularly enthusiastic, with one declaring it the "Best Pool Game Ever!"[^ref-25].

## Legacy

### Cross-Platform Play

Sierra promoted Maximum Pool as "one of the first products in the console marketplace" to let PC, Macintosh and Dreamcast players challenge one another via online Internet play[^ref-5].

### Server Shutdown and Community Preservation

Sierra terminated multiplayer support for Maximum Pool on August 16, 2007, along with other legacy titles including 3D Ultra Cool Pool[^ref-29].

Fans set up privately run servers, and a registry patch points the game at them[^ref-29][^ref-11]. DreamcastLive.net lists the Dreamcast version's servers as hosted by Dreamcast-Talk[^ref-26].

### Platform Preservation

Maximum Pool remains accessible through various preservation channels:

- **PC Version**: Available through abandonware archives and Internet Archive
- **Dreamcast Version**: Online play is hosted by community servers[^ref-26]
- **Macintosh Version**: Available through Macintosh Repository; the v1.06 update supports Mac OS X up to 10.5 (Leopard)[^ref-19]

Modern players can run the Windows version, though manual cue control may cause crashes on Windows XP and Windows 7[^ref-9].
