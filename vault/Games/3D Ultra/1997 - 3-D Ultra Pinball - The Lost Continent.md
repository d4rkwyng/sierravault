# 3-D Ultra Pinball: The Lost Continent

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 27, 2026</small>

## Overview

3-D Ultra Pinball: The Lost Continent is an arcade-style pinball game underscored by an action-adventure story line[^ref-1] that was released in 1997 by Sierra Entertainment's Dynamix studio[^ref-2]. The third installment of the popular pinball series, presenting an innovative approach to this classic genre[^ref-3], it departed significantly from traditional pinball by featuring a narrative-driven experience across multiple interconnected tables. As one reviewer noted, "This is not a regular pinball game, it has a storyline that takes players through the different tables in the game"[^ref-4].

The game is essentially a combination of Jurassic Park, Indiana Jones, and pinball[^ref-5], featuring a dinosaur theme that was inspired by the popularity of the Jurassic Park film[^ref-6]. The product description preserved with the archive.org copy promises "all the sights and sounds you have come to expect from the bestselling pinball series of all time"[^ref-1], while the game itself attempts to tell a cohesive story through its 15 ultra-wide, interlinked pinball tables[^ref-7]. The series had sold more than half a million copies by 1998[^ref-2].

## Story Summary

The Lost Continent follows an adventure story involving crash-landed explorers on a mysterious island. An airplane carrying three explorers is hit by lightning and crashes on an island somewhere in Africa[^ref-10]. The island is full of dinosaurs, long thought extinct[^ref-10], and is controlled by the diabolical Doktor Hekla, who has been transforming the local inhabitants into dinosaurs[^ref-2]. Your task is to lead Rex Hunter, Professor Spector, Mary, and Neeka to a safe place[^ref-4] while battling savage prehistoric tribes and surviving dinosaur stampedes[^ref-11].

The story, although very short, involves crash landing on an island taken over by a mad scientist, who is turning the inhabitants into dinosaurs[^ref-9]. The Collection Chamber reviewer describes the plot as "a rip-roaring yarn inspired by the serials of the 1930s and the B-movie schlock of the 1950s"[^ref-7], and one MobyGames user found the character dialogue "straight from a 'B' movie" and fun[^ref-16]. As the game progresses, players must discover the hidden laboratory of Doktor Hekla and ultimately earn the ultimate game payoff - a fully animated escape from the Lost Continent[^ref-11].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The Lost Continent utilizes Sierra's physics-based game engine, with the company boasting that "Nothing comes close to our physics-based game engine. The pinball moves, banks and ricochets just like the real thing"[^ref-7]. However, the Collection Chamber reviewer found that "the physics are still a little iffy"[^ref-7]. The game supports the mouse, keyboard and analog joysticks[^ref-27], with compatibility for specialized pinball controllers like the Thrustmaster Wizard pinball controller, Microsoft Sidewinder game pad, and Gravis Gripp game pad[^ref-1].

A contemporary NewTechReview review said the game "provides the most sophisticated play that I have seen so far in a video pinball game"[^ref-14]. Despite some control issues during slowdown periods[^ref-15], the ball reacts to objects according to actual laws of physics, upping the realism factor[^ref-15].

### Structure and Progression

Unlike traditional pinball games, The Lost Continent features a unique progression system across 15 tables that are shorter than levels in earlier games in the series[^ref-16]. The game is divided into three major sectors: Jungle, Temple, and Chambers[^ref-2], with 16 tables total but only 3 being full tables[^ref-15]. The two counts agree: 15 tables carry the story and a secret 16th is the final level, reachable only after the others are beaten[^ref-10]; NewTechReview likewise describes "15 inter-linking tables of play, along with one bonus table"[^ref-14]. Each table represents part of the characters' journey crossing the Lost Continent[^ref-16]. Hardcore Gaming 101 observes that it "seems to be the only game in the series to attempt to have a story"[^ref-5].

The game includes a unique "save or sacrifice" feature that allows players to either drop back a level or sacrifice a ball when facing difficult situations[^ref-16]. Characters are affected by the table being played[^ref-16], offering tips during gameplay and allowing players to see characters firing guns at enemies[^ref-4].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The Lost Continent incorporates multi-ball scoring and animated targets throughout its tables[^ref-11]. Players must battle savage prehistoric tribes and survive dinosaur stampedes while navigating the complex table layouts[^ref-11]. The game features temporary targets such as dinosaurs appearing throughout the table[^ref-2], adding dynamic elements that differentiate it from traditional static pinball tables.

Dinosaurs interact with the ball occasionally, and notably, the T. Rex can be killed by hitting it with the ball multiple times[^ref-17]. The game uses a continues system where replays can be used to continue at Level 1[^ref-5]. Progress can also be saved mid-game; NewTechReview noted that "as in the last version, you are able to save your game along the way"[^ref-14].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

The Lost Continent received mixed to positive ratings from critics and players over the years. GameSpot users rated the game 8.1 out of 10[^ref-4], while IMDb users gave it a score of 6.8/10[^ref-19]. Critics on MobyGames provided an aggregate score of 68% from 17 ratings[^ref-18], and MyAbandonware users rated it 4.79/5[^ref-20].

Contemporary reviewers praised various aspects of the game, with GameRevolution's Nebojsa Radakovic noting that "Sierra really has a good grasp on smooth and colorful design"[^ref-15] and praising the soundtrack as "one of the best I've heard" as far as game soundtracks are concerned[^ref-15]. However, the same reviewer noted that "Despite some of its annoying quirks, it's a solid game"[^ref-15], acknowledging both strengths and weaknesses.

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospectives are split. The Collection Chamber reviewer concluded that "3D Ultra Pinball: The Lost Continent remains one of the most unique ball-flippers ever released. It all comes together in what I believe to be the best of the series. Highly recommended"[^ref-7]. However, other modern reviewers have been less forgiving, with one noting that "The intro cut scene looks like something designed by my high school buddies using Microsoft Movie Maker"[^ref-8].

Hardcore Gaming 101's retrospective assessment noted that "The Lost Continent is quite an ambitious pinball title, and it would have been a fitting end to the trilogy"[^ref-5], recognizing the game's scope while acknowledging its technical limitations. Modern compatibility testing has shown the game runs on Windows 10[^ref-8], though with some issues including balls sometimes unfairly falling out of play field immediately after being released[^ref-8].

## Development

### Origins

The Lost Continent was developed by Dynamix in cooperation with Sierra[^ref-3], with Dynamix getting far more adventurous for their third game in the series[^ref-7]. The credits list Jerry Carathers as pinball design consultant and Dariusz Lukaszuk on physics programming[^ref-12]. GamePressure lists Jeff Tunnell Productions alongside Dynamix[^ref-3]; MobyGames credits Jeffrey Tunnell as executive producer[^ref-12].

The game's development was influenced by the popularity of the Jurassic Park film[^ref-6]. The dinosaur theme replaced the space and horror motives of the previous games[^ref-9] in the series.

### Production

The voice cast included Bill Barrett, Jane Chase, Lani Minella, Mike Shapiro, and Ed Ragozzino[^ref-19]. The credits name Robert Terrell as director for Terrell Enterprises, which handled 3D art and animation, and Kevin Lamb for on-line manual writing[^ref-12]. The production utilized the same engine series as previous versions[^ref-3], though Sierra emphasized that nothing comes close to their physics-based game engine[^ref-7].

The technical specifications included 640x480 resolution with 256 colors[^ref-5], delivered on CD-ROM format[^ref-5]. GamePressure lists the Windows requirements as a 486/DX2 66 MHz, 8 MB RAM, 25 MB of hard disk space and Windows 3.x/95[^ref-3]. MobyGames lists a PowerPC 601, System 7.5 and 12 MB RAM for the Macintosh version[^ref-27]. Both versions shipped on one hybrid CD-ROM, which NewTechReview noted was new for the series[^ref-14].

### Technical Achievements

From the technical point of view, the third part of the game does not bring any revolution[^ref-6], using established engine technology from previous series entries. However, the game did feature innovations in narrative integration with pinball gameplay, dedicating half the screen to theatrics or mini-games[^ref-7] to support the story elements.

The game ran on Windows 3.1, Windows 95 and the Macintosh[^ref-27]. Collection Chamber's modern repack (a 332 MB download, 530 MB installed) runs it under DOSBox with Windows 3.1[^ref-7]. Like all Sierra games of the period, it was covered by Sierra's 100% money-back satisfaction guarantee[^ref-8].

## Legacy

Hardcore Gaming 101 called The Lost Continent "quite an ambitious pinball title"[^ref-5]. Bunny Gamer, moving on from it to the 2000 sequel ThrillRide, wrote that "every complaint I had regarding the previous game has been corrected"[^ref-21].

MobyGames also places the game in Sierra's wider 3-D Ultra series, between 3-D Ultra Minigolf (1997) and 3-D Ultra NASCAR Pinball (1998)[^ref-9]. NewTechReview's contemporary review concluded: "If you are going to buy only one video pinball game to add to your arsenal, let it be 3D Ultra Pinball: The Lost Continent"[^ref-14].

The title remains available through preservation efforts, with multiple archive versions maintaining access to this unique gaming experience[^ref-23]. Its combination of B-movie aesthetics, dinosaur themes, and innovative pinball mechanics continues to attract retro gaming enthusiasts, with one user noting it brings back great memories of a misspent youth as a pin ball wizard[^ref-20].
