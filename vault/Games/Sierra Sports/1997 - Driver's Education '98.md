# Driver's Education '98

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 29, 2026</small>

## Overview

Driver's Education '98 is an educational driving simulator developed by [[Dynamix]] and published by [[Sierra On-Line]] for Windows in 1998.[^ref-1][^ref-11][^ref-12] It was among the titles on Sierra's E3 1997 press CD (dated June 10, 1997), where it was listed as "Drivers Education".[^ref-25] The software was designed to help prepare aspiring drivers for both the written examination and the practical driving test.[^ref-1] Its box described it as "TWO-PRODUCTS-IN-ONE", with an interactive test-prep Driving Academy and a 3D Virtual City with real-world physics.[^ref-3]

The game was one of the last titles published under Sierra's "Discovery Series" brand.[^ref-4] Players drive around a virtual city with a talkative virtual driving instructor. The MobyGames credits name him "Driver Ed", voiced by Jim Waid, while abandonware descriptions call him "Ed".[^ref-23][^ref-4] The program took into account the driving laws of different U.S. states, offering lessons and tests appropriate to the state the user selected at the start.[^ref-4]

Driver's Education was sold with a money-back guarantee: if users didn't pass their driving test after using the program, they could get their money back.[^ref-4][^ref-7] An abandonware description of the game calls it "an outstanding educational program that does exactly what it sets out to do: teaches driving in a safe environment without any risk of an accident."[^ref-4][^ref-7]

## Program Summary

Driver's Education '98 was a driving preparation tool rather than a narrative-driven game. It included driving manuals for all 50 United States and more than 30 lessons set on all sorts of roads and freeways in the virtual city.[^ref-1][^ref-8] Lessons and tests were tailored to the state the user named at the start of the program.[^ref-4]

Playing it was described as "very much like a real driving school experience except for the virtual cars." Users went through the lessons in order, and each lesson was followed by a 3D driving lesson with the instructor as a passenger.[^ref-4] The software allowed free-roaming in the virtual city with the virtual instructor, completing objectives such as parking, changing lanes, freeway driving, and driving in bad weather.[^ref-8]

The instruction covered the rules of the road, traffic laws, spotting and understanding road signs, and safety precautions.[^ref-4][^ref-10] The software also came with diagrams of how to change lanes, and short flash animations showing how each driving maneuver is done.[^ref-8]

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

MobyGames classifies the game's perspective as both first-person and third-person.[^ref-1] An abandonware description notes that users "can switch the view to different camera angles, much like a typical racing game."[^ref-4]

The car could be controlled with a keyboard, a joystick, or a driving/racing wheel with pedals.[^ref-4] Sierra's 1998 system requirements page listed support for ThrustMaster and Windows 95-compatible wheels, pedals and joysticks, as well as 3Dfx accelerators.[^ref-24] The virtual car came equipped with brake lights, an automatic transmission, and rearview and sideview mirrors.[^ref-4]

Mouse and force-feedback support belong to the follow-up, Driver's Education '99. Its CodeWeavers listing describes "driving via a mouse, keyboard, standard and force feedback joysticks, steering wheels, and pedals".[^ref-9]

### Structure and Progression

The program was divided into two main components:[^ref-3]

- **Driving Academy:** an interactive test-preparation module.[^ref-3]

- **3D Virtual City:** a driving simulation with real-world physics, where users practised behind the virtual wheel.[^ref-3]

While driving, users lost points both for breaking the rules and for not taking enough safety precautions. Point-losing events included speeding, pulling out into traffic without signalling, and not buckling the seatbelt. After the lessons, users eventually took a review test.[^ref-4]

The 1998 edition featured more than 30 lessons.[^ref-8] The later 1999 edition grew this to 65 lessons, with testing for all 50 states and the District of Columbia.[^ref-9]

### Lessons and Scenarios

The lesson objectives named in the game's database descriptions include:

- **Parking**[^ref-8]
- **Lane Changing:** with accompanying diagrams[^ref-8]
- **Freeway Driving**[^ref-8]
- **Bad Weather Driving**[^ref-8]
- **Street Symbol Recognition:** recognizing street symbols and what to do for a given sign or signal[^ref-10]

An abandonware description adds: "Anyone familiar with Dynamix's superb flight simulations will feel at home with sharp, detailed SVGA graphics."[^ref-4]

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

No contemporary press reviews of Driver's Education '98 were located for this page. MobyGames lists no Moby Score for the game, with no user votes recorded.[^ref-1]

Amazon's listing shows an aggregate of 2.4 out of 5 stars from four ratings. Its written reviews date from 2010 to 2016.[^ref-3] One reviewer, Jack Rella, noted that "This is a great tool in some ways, but the graphics are limited; it feels like a video game as opposed to an educational tool."[^ref-3] Another reviewer, Patricia, was more enthusiastic, calling it "Great for "private driving lessons", 365 days a year....with the most nominal of costs!"[^ref-3] A 2016 reviewer reported being unable to play it at all.[^ref-3]

### Modern Assessment

GameFAQs users rate the game "Great" on the strength of 8 user ratings.[^ref-11]

The driving-instruction website Driver's Ed Guru said the game "may actually be a fantastic game, but unfortunately, we don't know". It chose not to review it "because it's only available by paid download (and because we're not THAT anxious to see it)".[^ref-13] The site still highlighted it as "the only online driving game that lets you play from the perspective of an actual driver (as opposed to the "eagle flying overhead" perspective)."[^ref-13]

## Development

### Origins

Driver's Education '98 was developed by Dynamix, a studio known for its flight simulations.[^ref-1][^ref-4] It was one of the last titles published under Sierra's "Discovery Series" brand.[^ref-4]

The game appeared on Sierra's E3 1997 screenshot CD, dated June 10, 1997. The disc listed it alongside other Sierra titles, such as Front Page Sports: Baseball Pro '98 and Front Page Sports: Ski Racing.[^ref-25] In February 1998, Sierra's official Driver's Education website offered a tour of the Virtual City, driving education and safety articles, and the laws and legal exceptions for all 50 states and the District of Columbia.[^ref-26]

### Production

Development was led by Director Bill Money, with Jeffrey Tunnell as Executive Producer and Ken Embery as Producer.[^ref-23] Glenn Wallace was Lead Programmer, with sim programmer James Poelke and shell programmer Nancy Hamilton.[^ref-23] The AI programming was credited to Jon Milnes, Brian Apgar, Art Peregrine and Damon Mitchell. Dan White handled vehicle physics.[^ref-23]

The digital artists were Robert Borth, Lael Salaets and Anthony Lewis, and Justin Holman was Assistant Director.[^ref-23] Audio and sound effects came from Loudmouth, Inc.[^ref-1][^ref-23] Jim Waid voiced Driver Ed and Jane Chase voiced the classroom instructor.[^ref-23]

### Technical Achievements

The game used SVGA graphics for its 3D virtual city, which one description calls "sharp, detailed".[^ref-4] The box promoted the Virtual City's "real-world physics".[^ref-3] One description says the car physics was "realistic enough to give kids a feel for driving, without overwhelming them with technical minutiae."[^ref-4]

Lessons and tests were customized to the user's state.[^ref-4] The in-car scoring system deducted points for rule violations and missed safety precautions.[^ref-4]

### Technical Specifications

**Sierra's published system requirements (1998):**[^ref-24]
- **Minimum:** Pentium with 16 MB RAM, 4x CD-ROM, SVGA 256-color monitor, 1 MB PCI video card, Windows-compatible sound card with DAC, Windows 95, 50 MB free hard drive space
- **Recommended:** Pentium 133 with 32 MB RAM, 8x CD-ROM, 2 MB PCI video card, Windows-compatible sound card, Windows 95
- **Controllers:** ThrustMaster and Windows 95-compatible wheels, pedals and joysticks
- **3D acceleration:** 3Dfx accelerators supported

**Other specifications:**
- **Perspective:** First-person and third-person[^ref-1]
- **Media:** CD-ROM[^ref-1]
- **Players:** 1 player[^ref-1]

### Technical Issues

Like many Windows 98-era programs, Driver's Education '98 has compatibility problems on later operating systems. One Windows XP user reported that the program loaded the virtual city, but there was no sound and the car could not be driven.[^ref-14] A reply suggested trying Windows 95/98 compatibility mode and listed XP's generic display options, including 256 colors and 640×480.[^ref-14]

For modern systems, one Steam Community reply recommends emulating a Windows 98 PC in PCem or 86box. The same reply says wine/proton should be used only "for Windows XP era games and higher".[^ref-15] In the same thread, a Steam Deck user reported being unable to install the game's .bin/.cue files through Lutris.[^ref-15] The uploader of the Internet Archive copy, by contrast, reported that it "Works perfect in W10".[^ref-16]

**Known Compatibility Issues:**
- Windows XP: no sound in the virtual city, unable to drive (one user report)[^ref-14]
- Modern systems: emulation (PCem or 86box) recommended by one forum user[^ref-15]
- File format: preserved copies may be .bin/.cue disc images that need mounting or conversion[^ref-15]
- One Amazon customer reported being unable to play the game at all[^ref-3]

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

- **Money-Back Guarantee:** The program was sold with a money-back guarantee for users who didn't pass their driving test after using it.[^ref-4][^ref-7]

- **Middle School Memories:** One Reddit user recalled that the game "was installed on our middle school library computers back in the day."[^ref-18]

- **Younger Drivers:** One abandonware description suggests it as "a great alternative to putting them behind the wheel" for "younger kids in the house (say, 11-13 years old) who can't wait to drive".[^ref-4]

- **Alternate Names:** CodeWeavers' entry for the '99 edition notes that the program "also goes by the name driversed or drivered".[^ref-9] The Internet Archive's copy of the '98 edition is catalogued as "DRIVERED".[^ref-16]

- **Driver's Perspective:** Driver's Ed Guru called it "the only online driving game that lets you play from the perspective of an actual driver". It was comparing the game with the online driving games it covered.[^ref-13]

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

No sales figures for Driver's Education '98 were located for this page. It was followed by Driver's Education 99, which MobyGames lists as also released in 1998.[^ref-1][^ref-5]

### Cultural Presence

A 2023 Reddit thread asked whether anyone remembered the game. The original poster recalled using it on middle school library computers and noted that they "just called it, "Driver's Ed"".[^ref-18]

### Preservation Status

The game is preserved through online archives. The Internet Archive hosts a copy of the original 1998 version, uploaded in November 2019 by a user who wrote, "I couldn't seem to find it anywhere."[^ref-16]

The game is not available on Steam.[^ref-19] Several abandonware sites offer downloads of it.[^ref-4][^ref-7]

### Series Continuation

Driver's Education '98 was followed by Driver's Education 99, which offered improved graphics and more driving simulations.[^ref-1][^ref-7] The 1999 edition expanded the lesson count to 65. It listed testing for all 50 states and the District of Columbia, and added force-feedback support.[^ref-9]

The MobyGames entry for the sequel notes that, similar to the original game, "the player can drive around in a virtual world with a virtual driving instructor (named "Driver's Ed"). There is also virtual driving instruction and the ability to take sample written tests from each state."[^ref-5]

### Critical Perspective

Driver's Education '98 is an example of educational software built by a studio better known for entertainment simulations. It put Dynamix's 3D simulation work to practical use in a driver-training product.[^ref-1][^ref-4] An abandonware description concludes that "it will never replace driving lessons behind the real wheel". The same description still calls it an "outstanding educational program".[^ref-4]
