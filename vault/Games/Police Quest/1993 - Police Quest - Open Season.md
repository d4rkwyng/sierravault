# Police Quest: Open Season

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

Police Quest: Open Season, released in November 1993, marked a radical departure from the established Police Quest series, with a new plot, new protagonist, new location and new look[^ref-11]. Produced by Sierra On-Line in cooperation with former LAPD chief [[Daryl Gates|Daryl F. Gates]][^ref-18], this fourth installment abandoned the story arc of the previous three games[^ref-15], introducing Los Angeles as its setting and a new protagonist, homicide detective John Carey of the Los Angeles Police Department[^ref-11].

As part of Ken Williams' ambitious Reality Role-Playing series, the game represented Sierra's attempt to create serious simulations of real-world events rather than traditional games[^ref-4]. Williams explained his vision: "I want to find the top cop, lawyer, airline pilot, fireman, race-car driver, politician, military hero, schoolteacher, white-water rafter, mountain climber, etc., and have them work with us on a simulation of their world. Chief Gates gives us the cop game"[^ref-4]. The retro blog Retro365 describes the result as Sierra's "first fully photo-realistic game", with realistic settings and digitally captured actors[^ref-47].

## Story Summary

The game opens at a crime scene described by the narrator: "The tortured and tormented body of Bob Hickman, LAPD, lies lifeless among the alley debris. An obvious case of felonious homicide"[^ref-6]. Detective John Carey must investigate the murder of his best friend Officer Bob Hickman and 8-year-old Bobby Washington[^ref-15], whose body is found in a dumpster at the start of the game[^ref-7]. As one reviewer noted, "the game's gritty realism greatly depicts the gory life of a homicide detective. This in turn made some scenarios down-right gruesome to investigate"[^ref-7].

The story involves Carey tracking down a mass murderer, which takes elements from several books, such as Silence of the Lambs[^ref-7]. According to IMDb's trivia page, the killer is believed to be based on the serial murderer Jeffrey Dahmer[^ref-8]. Throughout the investigation, players are expected to follow LAPD procedure[^ref-18].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Police Quest: Open Season utilized Sierra's new SCI2 engine and featured a point-and-click interface[^ref-9]. Unlike most Sierra games, the maximum score does not appear in either version, making it difficult for players to track their progress traditionally[^ref-8].

Like earlier Police Quest games, Open Season emphasizes following "realistic" police procedure and penalizes the player for violating protocol, failing to file paperwork, or committing sexual harassment[^ref-18]. Instead of a procedure handbook with gameplay information, it shipped with an actual LAPD policy handbook covering radio codes, uniform dress and legal regulations, which largely lacked information relevant to the game itself[^ref-18].

### Structure and Progression

Points are awarded for finding key information and clues, and PC Gamer notes that the final score "is determined by how many clues you gathered and how closely you followed procedure"[^ref-12]. Breaking protocol can end the game outright, with game-over messages like "Deadly use of force was not tactically correct!" when the player, for example, draws a gun on a grieving widow[^ref-12]. This realistic approach meant that "the sheer level of realism in this game is groundbreaking, if not a bit annoying at times"[^ref-13].

The investigation took players through multiple floors of Parker Center (LAPD headquarters), which were recreated in the game[^ref-22]. Chief Gates wanted to keep the game as realistic as possible and insisted on using actual LA locations; some were only available to the police, and the designers could photograph them only thanks to Gates' connections[^ref-15]. Just Games Retro's reviewer supposed that having Gates attached "opened a lot of doors" and won cooperation from the department and the mayor's office, and lists apparently authentic locations from the county morgue, civic center and police academy[^ref-22].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

The puzzles mix police procedure with conventional adventure-game logic. MobyGames reviewer ETJB wrote that "some puzzles are entirely logical, but do require a basic understanding of professional police procedures", while "other puzzles are so illogical and bizarre, that you can only solve them by trial and error repetition or cheating"[^ref-34].

The game's dialogue has drawn criticism for its stereotyped portrayal of Asian and African American characters[^ref-12]. Daryl Gates told Vibe magazine in 1994 that he had warned Sierra's writers this was a bad idea: "I told them that these people people [sic] use the same language as you and I." PC Gamer's Andy Kelly concluded that "it seems this advice fell on deaf ears"[^ref-12].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

| Publication | Score | Notes |
|-------------|-------|-------|
| Computer Gaming World (Mar 1994) | — | Dennis Owens: "Open Season tells that story magnificently"[^ref-49] |
| Electronic Games (Oct 1994) | B+ | Clayton Walnum: "a rewarding and eye-opening experience"[^ref-50] |
| White Wolf (May 1994) | Very Good | James V. Trunzo[^ref-18] |
| Next Generation #9 (Sept 1995) | 2/5 | Macintosh version[^ref-51] |

Open Season received mixed reviews on release[^ref-18]. Computer Gaming World's Dennis Owens called the photo-realistic graphics a source of "grimness and sense of pressure", described Neal Grandstaff's score as "moody and evocative", and wrote that the contemporary detective story is "about class warfare and poverty" and that "Open Season tells that story magnificently"[^ref-49]. Electronic Games (B+) warned that "the subject matter can be disturbing" but called it "a rewarding and eye-opening experience" for tougher players[^ref-50]; Next Generation (2/5) concluded that "the ultimate Sierra police-based game is still a fond dream to look forward to"[^ref-51]; White Wolf rated it Very Good.[^ref-18] Writing in 1997, Randolph G. Potts called it a "particularly disturbing example" of stereotyping of Black men, and a 2018 Vice retrospective by Duncan Fyfe called it "the most reactionary game of the 90s".[^ref-18] Dargan defended the dialogue as based on Fab Five Freddy's rap dictionary *Fresh Fly Flavor*.[^ref-18]

The game carries an ESRB rating of Teen.[^ref-15]

### Modern Assessment

Modern retrospective reviews have been largely critical of the game[^ref-18].

| Aggregate | Score | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| MobyGames Critics | 73% | 23 ratings, Oct 2026[^ref-26] |
| IMDb | 6.8/10 | 93 votes, Oct 2026[^ref-20] |

In a 2013 review, Scott Bruner of Adventure Gamers gave it 2 stars ("There are some good elements that shine through occasionally, but generally the game is not a positive experience")[^ref-23]. The review criticized the "voice acting Ed Wood would love" and noted that "with a conclusion that borders on incomprehensible, Police Quest 4 offers impressive technological advances from previous games in the series, but loses their simpler charms"[^ref-23].

Alex Bevilacqua was even harsher in his 2024 retrospective, giving the game 40% and stating "The graphics are blocky and just overall look shitty" and "Sierra should have left this series alone after Police Quest 3"[^ref-24]. Space Quest Historian described the game as a "depressingly goofy trainwreck" with "mushy, pixelated" graphics and an "absolute slog" of gameplay[^ref-25].

However, some reviewers found merit in the game's ambitious approach. MobyGames user ETJB noted that it was "very much ahead of its time. It attempts to provide an interactive and realistic experience as to what a police detective actually does during a murder investigation. This is years before, 'Grand Theft Auto' would attempt a similar interactive and realistic experience"[^ref-34].

## Development

### Origins

The game's development began after Jim Walls, creator of the original Police Quest series, left Sierra. As Walls explained on his personal website: "after PQ 3, circumstances developed causing me to decide it was time that I leave"[^ref-16]. Ken Williams wanted the police games to transition into tactical simulations more than just being interactive stories, leading him to seek out Daryl Gates, who had knowledge of police procedures and tactics that were well beyond what any one field officer could bring to the table[^ref-14].

Sierra brought long-time LA Police Chief [[Daryl Gates|Daryl F. Gates]], who had recently resigned following the 1992 LA riots, into the team "as author of the game"; he joined "to show gamers the true face of police work"[^ref-11]. MobyGames credits Gates as "Author" and [[Tammy Dargan]] as "Producer \ Director" and "Designer \ Writer"[^ref-15]. According to Wikipedia, although Gates' name was on the cover, the game was largely written and designed by Dargan, who received some notes from Gates; Gates claims not to have written the game's storyline and said its depictions were not his idea[^ref-18]. Dargan, a former producer on the TV show America's Most Wanted, had previously produced the remake of the first game[^ref-30].

### Production

The development team utilized significant technology for its time. They digitally photographed each scene and scanned them into the computer[^ref-30], producing what Retro365 calls Sierra's "first fully photo-realistic game"[^ref-47]. Character imagery was based on video recordings of actors (one retrospective calls it motion capture) and on real photographs of Los Angeles locations[^ref-18][^ref-14].

The credited cast included Brandon Massey as Detective John Carey, Andrew Witches as Mitchell Thurman, and Jeff Bianchi as Sam Nobles[^ref-20]. Several Sierra On-Line employees also appeared as actors, including Dana Dean (animator), Barry T. Smith (animator and writer), Rod Fung (cinematographer), and Neal Grandstaff (music)[^ref-8]. The game featured extensive voice acting, with the CD version containing 10,000 lines of dialogue[^ref-22].

### Technical Achievements

Open Season runs on Sierra's SCI2 engine[^ref-9]; MobyGames reviewer Katakis believes it was the first game to use SCI2, whose main features include support for 640×480 resolution, and calls it "the first Sierra game to feature photo-realistic backgrounds, long before the likes of Phantasmagoria and its sequel"[^ref-30].

The game came with an abridged LAPD manual of about 50 pages[^ref-14], which contained detailed regulations such as "the prescribed trousers belt shall be worn under the Police Equipment Belt. It shall be adjusted so that no part other than the top edge is visible"[^ref-14]. This manual served as both a reference guide and an attempt to add authenticity to the gaming experience.

## Legacy

Police Quest: Open Season marked the end of the traditional Police Quest adventure game series, though the franchise would continue with the SWAT tactical series[^ref-11]. By 1995, the first four Police Quest games had sold a combined 850,000 copies[^ref-18].

PC Gamer's Andy Kelly summed up the game in a retrospective review: "There's no doubt about it: this is a bad game. But it's an interesting one too, and one of the few detective games on PC that deals with the profession in a realistic manner"[^ref-12].

The game also serves as a historical artifact of early 1990s attitudes toward policing and urban crime. As Jimmy Maher noted in The Digital Antiquarian: "the jingoistic Gates game is a prime case in point" when discussing games that failed to handle complex social questions with appropriate nuance[^ref-48].
