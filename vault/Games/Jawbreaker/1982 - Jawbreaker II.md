# Jawbreaker II

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 13, 2026</small>

## Overview

Jawbreaker II is an arcade game published by Sierra On-Line in 1982 for the Apple II and Atari 8-bit computers, programmed by Chuck Bueche (credited as "Chuckles")[^ref-1][^ref-3]. Versions for the Commodore 64, VIC-20 and TI-99/4A followed in 1983[^ref-1]. Unlike the first computer Jawbreaker, Jawbreaker II is not a Pac-Man clone; it is closer to the Atari 2600 version of the first game[^ref-1].

Legal issues with the original made John Harris's Atari 2600 design the template for these later versions, which were sold, confusingly, as both Jawbreaker and Jawbreaker II[^ref-5]. Sierra released them under its SierraVision arcade label[^ref-7][^ref-9]. According to Wikipedia, the new game "was not as successful as the original"[^ref-5].

## Story Summary

There is no real story; the game keeps the candy-and-teeth theme of the original. Sierra's description reads: "A trip to the candy shop was never so exciting - nor so dangerous! While you're gobbling up treats, a swarm of happy faces is trying to catch you to pull those teeth"[^ref-7].

The TI-99/4A manual describes the player character as "the Jawbreaker, a powerful set of teeth", racing "through a maze of shifting walls and doors"[^ref-4]. The enemies are the happy faces, which the manual calls "Grinning Gobblers"[^ref-1][^ref-4].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

Jawbreaker II is a one-player game, controlled by joystick or keyboard[^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-4]. On the Atari, keyboard players pick their own keys for up, down, left, right and stop. A pressed "up" or "down" is held in memory and carried out at the next door in that direction[^ref-7].

Before play, the player sets a level from 0 to 9. The Commodore 64 manual says this skill level "controls the speed of the action", and level 0 is a "teddy bear" level for young children[^ref-19][^ref-23]. The TI-99/4A version offers five levels instead, from Teddy Bear (the slowest) to Jawbreaker[^ref-4].

MobyGames classifies the game as side view[^ref-1]. Sierra Chest classifies it as a mono-screen arcade game[^ref-7].

### Core Mechanics

The screen holds five horizontal levels lined with dots ("candies"). The player must eat all of them to earn a teeth brushing and move on to the next, harder level[^ref-1]. Moving openings in each level let the player pass between levels. Happy faces patrol every level and knock the player's teeth out on contact, costing a life[^ref-1].

Special dots in the four corners briefly let the player eat the happy faces, and a lollipop sometimes appears in the center for extra points[^ref-1]. In the Atari version, a small dot is worth 5 points, an energizer 20 and a special treat 200. Successive faces score 100, 200, 400 and 800[^ref-7]. An extra set of teeth comes every 10,000 points[^ref-19][^ref-23].

On Lemon64, one commenter wrote that the player's "death" is memorable: "you're a set of false teeth and, when hit by a Jawbreaker, all of its teeth fall out!"[^ref-2]. An Atarimania commenter described the game as "like a cross between Pacman and Turmoil"[^ref-3].

## Reception

Contemporary reviews specific to Jawbreaker II have not been found. The well-known contemporary praise and awards belong to the original 1981 Jawbreaker (see [[1981 - Jawbreaker|Jawbreaker]]). Wikipedia says the later game "was not as successful as the original"[^ref-5].

Ratings on modern databases are modest:

| MobyGames | 40% | Critic average from a single rating[^ref-1] |

User comments are mixed. One Lemon64 commenter called the C64 version "Well executed but terribly tedious"[^ref-2]. A MyAbandonware commenter found it "Good for the occasional, casual play. A variation on the Pacman style of play"[^ref-10].

## Development

### Origins

The original Jawbreaker, John Harris's Pac-Man-style game for the Atari 8-bit, drew Atari into court. Atari sought a preliminary injunction against On-Line. The judge applied the "ten-foot rule" from Midway v. Dirkschneider and found that Jawbreaker looked different enough from Pac-Man to refuse Atari's motion[^ref-13].

Harris then wrote a very different Jawbreaker for the Atari 2600[^ref-14]. Legal issues made that 2600 design the template for the later computer versions[^ref-5].

### Production

Harris did not write Jawbreaker II. Asked about its connection to his 2600 game, he told Halcyon Days[^ref-14]:

> "Jawbreaker II" came later, written by someone else. The programmer had never seen the 2600 version of "Jawbreaker," and so Ken Williams drew a quick sketch to show him what to do, but didn't explain it very well. This rough sketch had only five horizontal pathways on it, and so that is all that were programmed into the game.

Harris added that the open passageways at the edges of each line "weren't drawn in Ken's sketch, and so they didn't get included in the game"[^ref-14]. Retro365 says the computer versions were reinvented by Doug Whittaker and Chuck Bueche from Harris's 2600 game. It describes the number of horizontal pathways (5 instead of 9) as "lost in translation", and says Harris was "appalled" when he saw them[^ref-9].

### Versions

- **Atari 8-bit:** programmed by Chuck Bueche, serials JBD-201 / SRL-212. Atarimania notes that this is Jawbreaker II "despite what the title screen says"[^ref-3]. An Atarimania commenter reports that the full disk version calls itself "Jawbreaker IV"[^ref-3].
- **Commodore 64:** credited to Chuck Bueche and Doug Whittaker, who are also credited for the music[^ref-2].
- **TI-99/4A:** a Solid State Software Command Module, part number PHM 3194, released in 1983 at $39.95. It was programmed by Dan Drew and converted by John M. Phillips[^ref-4].

Commenters identify some of the music as Mozart's Turkish March (Atari 8-bit)[^ref-3], "Gay Paris" and "Hall of the Mountain King"[^ref-17].

## Legacy

Jawbreaker II was included in the 1986 compilation *Familiar Favorites II: Jawbreaker - Crossfire*[^ref-1].

In 2014, Maggoo and Makinavaja released an MSX adaptation of the TI-99/4A version. It was the second entry in the MSXdev'14 competition[^ref-17].
