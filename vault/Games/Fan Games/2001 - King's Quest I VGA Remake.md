# King's Quest I: Quest for the Crown VGA Remake

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 28, 2026</small>

## Overview

King's Quest I: Quest for the Crown VGA Remake is a fan-made reimagining of [[Roberta Williams]]' 1984 adventure game, released on August 7, 2001[^ref-1] by Tierra Entertainment (renamed AGD Interactive on December 10, 2003)[^ref-7]. The remake was created using the free Adventure Game Studio engine developed by Chris Jones; AGDI describes it as "the first complete fan-made adventure remake ever released, and the first to ever include a voice pack"[^ref-1]. According to AGDI, the team had "only intended to remake the game for our own practice and never really intended to show it to anyone else"[^ref-37]. AGDI reports that over 5,000 copies were downloaded in the first month and that "in the first year, that number jumped to nearly half a million"[^ref-2].

The remake upgrades the original game with VGA graphics, a point-and-click interface that mimics that of King's Quest V and VI, and full speech for all characters[^ref-44]; for version 2.0 Tom Lewandowski re-recorded the musical score digitally[^ref-46]. Erpy's official game guide on the AGDI site claims that "you'd be hard pressed to tell that it wasn't created by Sierra On-Line in their golden days"[^ref-4]. AGDI worked under a fan license from the rights holders of the time (Vivendi Universal), allowing the game to be distributed free of charge[^ref-44][^ref-35][^ref-5]. An Enhanced Edition (version 4.0) released on January 17, 2009 re-edited all backgrounds to remove collaged artwork, added narrator speech and lip-synced dialogue portraits, and introduced an optional "No Dead-Ends" gameplay mode[^ref-1][^ref-37].

The remake directly addresses shortcomings of Sierra's own 1990 SCI remake, which featured only 16-color EGA graphics and retained the text parser interface—a version that was considered a "total market failure"[^ref-2]. AGD Interactive's goal was to create the VGA point-and-click version of King's Quest I that Sierra never made, bringing the classic game to the same quality standard as King's Quest V[^ref-2].

## Story Summary

The Kingdom of Daventry is in decline, and King Edward the Benevolent, who has no heir, fears he has little time left to live[^ref-44][^ref-13]. Three magical treasures that once protected the kingdom have been lost: a future-telling Magic Mirror, a Magic Chest that is forever full of gold, and a Magic Shield that protects its user from all attacks[^ref-13]. The aging king needs a champion to restore them before his death.

The king summons Sir Graham, his most loyal knight, for this quest[^ref-3]. The king promises that once Graham has collected all three treasures, he will become the new King of Daventry[^ref-13]. Graham accepts and sets off into the land of Daventry to find them[^ref-3].

Throughout his journey, Graham encounters a cast of characters drawn from classic fairy tales, including the three Billy Goats Gruff, a giant atop a beanstalk reminiscent of Jack's adventure, a witch in a gingerbread house, and a mysterious gnome whose name must be guessed—an obvious nod to Rumpelstiltskin[^ref-11]. Many obstacles can be overcome by force or by cleverness, and the cleverer solution usually scores better; as one player review on the AGS database put it, "you'll get a much higher final score if you use more cunning means to achieve your aims"[^ref-19].

When Graham returns to Castle Daventry with all three treasures, King Edward dies and Graham inherits the throne, setting the stage for the subsequent King's Quest titles[^ref-44][^ref-13]. The maximum score achievable in the game is 158 points[^ref-13].

## Gameplay

### Interface and Controls

The VGA remake drops the original game's text parser in favor of an interface that mimics that of King's Quest V and VI[^ref-44]. Tom Hayes's walkthrough describes the change as moving from a text-based system "where commands had to be typed" to "a newer icon based system that was first seen in King's Quest V"[^ref-3], and Erpy's guide calls the game "similar in gameplay style to Sierra's Point-and-Click titles like King's Quest V"[^ref-4].

Collected items are kept in an inventory where they can be examined and manipulated—opening the walnut with the Hand icon, for example, reveals that it is solid gold[^ref-13]. Erpy's guide notes that keypad control was a feature of version 3.0[^ref-4].

### Structure and Progression

The remake keeps the non-linear structure of the 1984 original, which AGDI calls "revolutionary" for its time[^ref-2]. Daventry has few boundaries and appears to wrap around, except near certain rivers and islands[^ref-44]; Retro Freak Reviews, writing about the original game, counts "about 48 screens" without interiors[^ref-16]. Players can explore freely and tackle the three treasure quests in any order they choose[^ref-16].

- **The Magic Mirror:** Guarded by a dragon in a lair reached from the bottom of the old well[^ref-38]
- **The Magic Chest:** Held by a giant in Cloudland, at the top of the beanstalk[^ref-38]
- **The Magic Shield:** Kept in the throne room of the Leprechaun King, reached by grabbing the condor's talons and descending a hole[^ref-38]

The game's open structure means players can discover and collect various helpful items throughout the world before determining which treasure to pursue first. Retro Freak Reviews called the original's design "probably the beginning of the open-world mechanic we know nowadays"[^ref-16].

### Puzzles and Mechanics

Puzzles in King's Quest I draw heavily from fairy tale logic and classic adventure game conventions[^ref-11]. Many solutions require applying knowledge from well-known stories—crossing a bridge requires dealing with a troll (echoing the Three Billy Goats Gruff), while escaping the giant involves tactics similar to Jack and the Beanstalk[^ref-11].

One infamous puzzle involves guessing a gnome's name. A note found in the witch's house hints that "Sometimes it is wise to think backwards," and the answer the remake accepts is "Ifnkovhgroghprm," worth 9 points; failing three guesses earns a key instead[^ref-13]. Players who guess correctly receive magic beans that grow into the beanstalk to Cloudland[^ref-13][^ref-38]. Version 4.2 RC1 made the name entry no longer case-sensitive[^ref-6].

The remake introduces an optional "No Dead-Ends" gameplay mode that eliminates situations where players could become permanently stuck[^ref-37]. In the original game, using certain items incorrectly or missing them entirely could render the game unwinnable without the player knowing until much later. With this new mode enabled, "no longer will you need to restore to an earlier save-game and retrace your steps just to perform one minor action differently"[^ref-17].

Random hazards roam parts of Daventry, including a dwarf who can steal items (even the Magic Mirror or Chest) from Graham's inventory, a sorcerer, an ogre, a wolf and the witch[^ref-39]. A condor can be grabbed by its talons to carry Graham to an otherwise unreachable area[^ref-38]. The dragon can be driven off by throwing water at it (5 points) or killed by throwing the dagger (3 points)[^ref-38][^ref-13]. The Fairy Godmother sometimes grants a protection spell that wards off most enemies for about a minute[^ref-13].

## Reception

### Contemporary Reviews

Adventure Gamers reviewed the remake in January 2004. Eivind Hagerup wrote that "it's amazing that such a small team (Tierra basically consists of two people) could create a gaming experience that brings memories right back to Sierra's heydays," while finding fault with a save-game list that could "randomly rearrange" itself, and concluded, "I can recommend this free download to any dedicated adventure gamer"[^ref-45]. Tom Hayes's FAQ/walkthrough for the game, hosted on Adventure Gamers, called it a "superb VGA remake" whose "graphics have been given a noteable upgrade and look absolutely stunning in places," adding, "I highly recommended it to all fans of adventure games"[^ref-3]. MobyGames lists a critic average of 86% (from 7 ratings), and a 2004 MobyGames player review by Silverblade called it "a truly professional effort, the graphics on par with Sierra's own Quest for Glory I remake"[^ref-18].

User comments on the Adventure Game Studio database were largely positive; one player called it "amazing for a remake," praising it for keeping the same puzzles, dialogue and screens as Sierra's own remake[^ref-19]. Another credited the game with getting them "hooked to point and click adventures"[^ref-19]. The AGS Panel noted that while it "suffers from the same poor storyline and unexpected deaths" as the original, "it does feature original artwork and soundtrack"[^ref-19].

### Modern Assessment

The game maintains strong ratings across multiple platforms. GamesNostalgia gave the remake an 81/100 editorial score, calling it "a must have for all the old school adventures lovers"[^ref-20]. MyAbandonware users rate it 4.63 out of 5 (8 votes)[^ref-21], while Metacritic shows a user score of 6.3/10 based on 9 ratings[^ref-22].

Home of the Underdogs (user rating 8.23 from 243 votes) praised the remake: "They have done this with an amazing professionalism. The game has improved in every aspect. If Sierra Studios were to have made this themselves, I doubt they'd have done it much differently"[^ref-43].

## Development

### Origins

The remake began as a practice project by two anonymous developers who would later be known as Anonymous Game Developer #1 and Anonymous Game Developer #2[^ref-24]. Developer #1, a female from the Southwest US living on the West Coast who had previously worked at a European Internet startup, chose the company name "Tierra" because it was "the name of a mythical land that I'd created and written about as a kid. In Spanish, it meant Earth"[^ref-24].

Alongside the remake, the team was developing a parody called "Royal Quest: Retrieving Lost Shit," which MobyGames describes as featuring a "bad-ass" Graham more interested in fighting than solving puzzles[^ref-7][^ref-18]. The two were developed simultaneously; Royal Quest was still in progress after the remake's release and was cancelled in late 2001 because the developers "believed that it did not promote the values and tone they were trying to preserve in remaking the old Sierra classics"[^ref-7][^ref-40]. Developer #2 lived in Melbourne, Australia, and the pair worked together remotely, preferring anonymity as "it goes against our purpose of releasing these games for a cause rather than for recognition"[^ref-24].

The developers aimed to create the VGA point-and-click version that Sierra never made. Erpy's guide notes that, unlike the other games Sierra remade, King's Quest I was never remade in VGA with a point-and-click interface[^ref-4]; Sierra's 1990 remake used 16-color EGA graphics[^ref-2] and still required players to type phrases[^ref-4]. AGDI's stated goal was to "remake King's Quest I: Quest for the Crown to the same level of quality as seen in Sierra's King's Quest V"[^ref-2].

### Production

Development was undertaken entirely by volunteers working without pay, which AGDI describes as "months of unpaid hard work", calling the remake and its future projects "our gift to the fans of the adventure gaming community"[^ref-2]. The initial release was built by just the two founding members, with the broader team expanding over subsequent versions[^ref-25].

The team used the free Adventure Game Studio engine, which proved capable of recreating the Sierra adventure game experience faithfully[^ref-4]. Early versions used modified artwork with elements taken from other Sierra games, including King's Quest V and Quest for Glory IV and V, combined with artwork from Sierra's 1990 SCI remake[^ref-44]. AGDI later wrote that what it "found particularly jarring were the collaged and ripped elements of some backgrounds that had been taken from other Sierra games"[^ref-37].

For the Enhanced Edition (version 4.0), artists John Paul Selwood, Emily Selwood, and Johan Botes re-edited all of the original backgrounds; AGDI says "Every collaged area has been removed and re-edited with unique artwork"[^ref-37]. Britney Brimhall also redid the character dialogue pictures, which gained full lip-syncing[^ref-37].

### Technical Achievements

The AGS database lists the game's graphics as 320x200 in 32-bit colour, with full narrator and character speech and digital music included in the download[^ref-19].

Version 4.0 introduced several technical improvements including Windows Vista support, native Ogg Theora movies for cutscenes, and full lip-syncing for character dialogue[^ref-37]. AGDI's own announcement said the redone backgrounds made it "really does feel like playing a genuine Sierra game all the more"[^ref-37].

The game's statistics reveal its scope: 2,865 sprites out of a 30,000 maximum capacity, 55 sprite folders, 122 views, 20 GUIs, 26 inventory items, 39 characters, and 3 dialog topics[^ref-1].

### Cut Content

The remake, while faithful to the original, omits some obscure elements from Sierra's 1990 SCI version due to the simplified interface lacking verbs like "smell" and "taste"[^ref-27]. Some detailed descriptions present in the original are missing, including the "vanilla buttercream frosting" description and detailed leaf descriptions on the gnome's island[^ref-27].

Certain Easter eggs from the original were not implemented, such as the response when trying to "pick lock" on the Door into Mountain[^ref-44]. Some death scenes were also changed, "lacking some of the death animation, instead having a close up death description/graphic"[^ref-27].

### Technical Issues

Several bugs were identified and addressed across versions:
- Save-game files getting misnumbered and overwriting existing saves (fixed in 4.1b)[^ref-36]
- GUIs not properly centered on screen (fixed in 4.1b)[^ref-36]
- Fairy Godmother's spell expiring while flying with the Condor (fixed in 4.1b)[^ref-36]
- Graham's reflection in moat water not updating correctly (fixed in 4.1b)[^ref-36]
- Missing confirm overwrite GUI when saving (added in 4.1b)[^ref-36]
- Graphics filters in winsetup.exe crashing the game (fixed in 4.1)[^ref-37]
- One AGS database player reported frequent crashes at the "restart/restore/quit" dialog in an earlier version[^ref-19]
- Display problems and screen stretching on widescreen monitors (addressed in 4.2 RC1)[^ref-6]

Version 4.2 RC1 was released in July 2024 specifically to address "compatibility issues on modern operating systems and hardware" that had accumulated over the decade since the last update[^ref-6].

### Easter Eggs and Trivia

The remake includes numerous hidden references and secrets[^ref-13]:

- **Cauldron Monster:** Using the Eye icon to look at smoke in the witch's cauldron reveals a monster's face
- **King's Quest V Reference:** Talking to yourself repeatedly (1:15 chance) triggers a reference about an owl from KQ5
- **Invisible Guard Dialogue:** Wearing the Magic Ring while talking to castle guards produces unique dialogue
- **All Your Base:** Reading the note repeatedly (1:15 chance) displays "All your base are belong to us!" message
- **Mirror Invisibility:** Using both Magic Ring and Mirror on Graham after obtaining the Magic Mirror creates a special effect
- **Missing Quail:** Examining a bush near the stump shows a message about the absence of a quail
- **Mountain Door:** Knocking on the Mountain Door triggers a very rare Easter Egg
- **Underwater Voice:** With the voice pack installed, the FAQ suggests trying to quit while underwater in the well and listening to Graham's voice
- **EGA Reference:** Examining a rock at Beautiful Lake mentions the EGA version
- **Mario Sound Effect:** The mushroom eating sound effect is taken from Super Mario Brothers[^ref-13]
- **Goat Revenge:** The goat will push Graham off a cliff if he eats the carrot in a specific location
- **Troll Food:** Using food items on the troll (1:4 chance) produces a hidden message

The company name change from Tierra Entertainment to AGD Interactive on December 10, 2003[^ref-7] was partly motivated by the original name being "a tad too similar to Sierra"[^ref-30].

## Legacy

### Sales and Commercial Impact

As a free fan project, the remake generated no direct revenue. According to AGDI, the game was downloaded over 5,000 times in its first month of August 2001, with "nearly half a million" in its first year[^ref-2]; AGDI also states that by September 2010 it had been downloaded over 494,000 times[^ref-1].

Just Adventure's October 2001 "State of Adventure Gaming" column noted that the nearly three-year-old King's Quest VIII: Mask of Eternity was the top-selling adventure game of August 2001 (19,484 units), and offered only as a theory ("what if...") that players of the remake, which had passed 20,000 downloads through Just Adventure, might have gone on to buy it[^ref-41]. Wikipedia summarizes this as KQ8 sales having "skyrocketed" that month[^ref-7]. The project won multiple AGS Awards in 2001, including Best AGS Documentation, Best Animation in an AGS Game, and Best Room Art in an AGS Game[^ref-19].

### Fan Projects

AGD Interactive followed this remake with increasingly ambitious projects:
- **King's Quest II: Romancing the Stones** – A complete reimagining of King's Quest II that was described as transforming it into "a completely new game only in part based on the original, with far superior graphics, mouse control, music and voices"[^ref-32]
- **King's Quest III Redux: To Heir Is Human** – Released February 2011, praised by Destructoid as potentially "the definitive version of this timeless adventure game"[^ref-33]
- **Quest for Glory II: Trial by Fire** – VGA remake released August 24, 2008[^ref-7]

The development team later founded Himalaya Studios to create original commercial games, including the "adventure/RPG hybrid akin to Quest for Glory called Mage's Initiation"[^ref-34].

However, after Activision acquired Vivendi and the Sierra intellectual property, AGD Interactive reported that it had been informed "that we will not be granted permission to start any new Sierra remakes in the future"[^ref-42].

### Critical Perspective

The remake was AGDI's first release and led directly to its later King's Quest II, King's Quest III and Quest for Glory II remakes[^ref-7]. The 2012 Wikipedia article on the remake says other community members were inspired by AGDI to work on their own remakes, such as Infamous Adventures with its King's Quest III and Space Quest II remakes[^ref-44].

AGDI frames the project as a way to keep playable the game that, in its words, "can be credited as the game that started graphic adventure gaming on the PC"[^ref-2]. Like other AGDI, Phoenix Online and Infamous Adventures projects, it was released for free under a fan license from the rights holders[^ref-35].

## Purchase

This fan remake is not sold anywhere; AGDI states that "the game is not available in stores or for sale" and distributes it as a free download[^ref-4]. Sierra's own King's Quest I versions are sold separately.
