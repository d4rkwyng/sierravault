# John Cutter

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

John Cutter is an American video game designer whose career stretches from the mid-1980s to the 2010s.[^ref-1][^ref-34] He was the first employee of the Amiga developer Cinemaware, recruited by founder Bob Jacob, and produced or designed many of its best-known games.[^ref-1][^ref-5][^ref-10][^ref-2] Later, at Dynamix, he was lead designer and director of [[1993 - Betrayal at Krondor|Betrayal at Krondor]], which won Computer Gaming World's Role-Playing Game of the Year award and was named overall Game of the Year by Computer Games Strategy Plus.[^ref-1][^ref-3]

According to MobyGames, Cutter attended Pepperdine University from 1980 to 1983. He told the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences that he studied Radio & Television broadcasting there, with a minor in advertising.[^ref-1][^ref-4] He has said that most of his career was spent on core PC and console titles, and that his first ten years in the industry were "a bit like the Wild West".[^ref-4] In 2006 he moved to casual games at Big Fish Games, where his best-known title is Fairway Solitaire.[^ref-4][^ref-1] His LinkedIn profile opens: "I have worn many hats in my 40 years as a game developer".[^ref-6]

His own bio says five of his games appear in Computer Gaming World's list of the 150 best games of all time, naming Betrayal at Krondor, Defender of the Crown, Rocket Ranger, TV Sports: Football and The Three Stooges.[^ref-34] The list itself, in CGW's November 1996 issue, includes five games he worked on: Betrayal at Krondor (#43), Rocket Ranger (#45), Wings (#65), Defender of the Crown (#92) and TV Sports: Football (#112). The Three Stooges does not appear on it.[^ref-35] His LinkedIn lists Fairway Solitaire's honors as including an IGN award for Best Mobile Board Game (2012) and the IMGA Excellence in Gameplay Award (2013).[^ref-6] LinkedIn lists Wooga as his employer.[^ref-6]

## Career

### Early Career and Gamestar (1984–1986)

Cutter grew up making games: he reinvented Dungeons & Dragons with his own rules, then taught himself BASIC on his first computer. After buying a Commodore 64 he learned machine language, which led to his first industry job as a junior coder.[^ref-4][^ref-34] He worked as a designer and programmer for Gamestar from 1984 to 1986, mainly on sports games.[^ref-1] His credits there include directing Star Rank Boxing (1985), creating On-Track: Computer Model Car Racing (1985), game design and graphics for GBA Championship Basketball: Two-on-Two (1986).[^ref-9] MobyGames also credits him with animation on Howard the Duck (1986, Commodore 64) and lists him under "Created by" on its 1987 MSX and Amstrad CPC versions.[^ref-9]

Watching his boss struggle to learn assembly on the Macintosh, he realized that "programming is just a means to an end for me. I just like seeing my ideas come to life on the screen. I've been a designer ever since."[^ref-4]

### Cinemaware Years (1986–1991)

In 1986, Cutter joined Cinemaware, where he became employee #1.[^ref-1][^ref-10] The Legendary Games podcast introduced its 2022 interview with him with his line: "After we released 'Defender of the Crown', we at Cinemaware were the rock stars of the industry for a while." It added that, as creative director, he managed almost all of the company's 15 published games, and that the company grew to more than 60 permanent employees.[^ref-5] Legendary Games also wrote that he regards his Cinemaware work as among the most fulfilling of his career.[^ref-5]

His first Cinemaware project was Defender of the Crown (1986), on which he is credited as associate producer and artist on the Amiga version.[^ref-9][^ref-12] He was associate producer, associate director and manual writer on The King of Chicago (1987).[^ref-13][^ref-9] On S.D.I. (1987) he was associate producer of the Amiga original and producer of the DOS version, and he was associate producer on Sinbad and the Throne of the Falcon (1987).[^ref-14][^ref-9] On The Three Stooges (1987) he is credited as producer, director, and concept and game designer.[^ref-15][^ref-9]

Cutter produced Rocket Ranger (1988), was its director on the Commodore 64 version, and is credited with its original design on the Atari ST and Apple IIgs versions.[^ref-16][^ref-9] He produced and designed TV Sports: Football (1988) and was associate designer on TV Sports: Basketball (1989).[^ref-17][^ref-9] He produced Lords of the Rising Sun (1989).[^ref-18]

His final Cinemaware design credits were Wings (1990), on which he was game designer, and ABC Wide World of Sports Boxing (1991).[^ref-19][^ref-9]

### New World Computing and Dynamix (1991–1994)

After Cinemaware, Cutter had a short stay at New World Computing, and he joined Dynamix the same year.[^ref-1] He has listed Might and Magic IV among his core PC titles.[^ref-4] At Dynamix he was designer and director of [[1993 - Betrayal at Krondor|Betrayal at Krondor]], a role-playing game set in Raymond E. Feist's Riftwar universe.[^ref-3][^ref-9]

Cutter and writer Neal Hallford created the game's plot, text and new characters. Feist later credited both as co-authors of the original story for his novelization *Krondor: The Betrayal*, and dedicated the book to them.[^ref-3] The game was designed to resemble a book, with chapters and third-person narration.[^ref-3]

Cutter pitched a sequel and its budget to Dynamix CEO Tony Reyneke, who, Cutter said, "didn't like how much the sequel was going to cost, and he fired me."[^ref-10] Computer Gaming World reported in April 1994 that "Dynamix elected to terminate both the project and the project's guiding force on the computer side, John Cutter. Cutter and his game design talents are currently looking for home."[^ref-36][^ref-21] Sierra later released Return to Krondor (1998), which credits him under "Special Thanks for their vision in creating Betrayal at Krondor".[^ref-22]

### Middle Career (1994–2006)

Cutter went on to work as a designer for Cavedog Entertainment, and later for Amaze Entertainment and Gas Powered Games.[^ref-1] At Cavedog he hired Neal Hallford again for Elysian, an episodic game set in a dream world that was cancelled before release.[^ref-10] At Gas Powered Games he is credited with additional design on Dungeon Siege II (2005), and he said Chris Taylor made him design director.[^ref-23][^ref-10] He is also credited under Adrenium Games' senior management on Samurai Jack: The Shadow of Aku (2004).[^ref-24]

During this period, Cutter observed significant changes in the industry. As he later reflected: "Publishers became a critical cog in the machine and as production costs increased many became risk averse, refusing [to] take chances on new concepts or ideas. I still loved my job, but I missed the old days."[^ref-4]

### Big Fish Games Era (2006–2014)

In 2004, Cutter's wife discovered Big Fish Games' website and became addicted to the casual games there.[^ref-4] Cutter saw in the casual gaming space "glimpses of the old Wild West".[^ref-4] After a few months at Secret Lair Studios, an MMO startup, he was recruited by Big Fish studio VP Pat Wylie. MobyGames dates his move to Big Fish to 2006.[^ref-4][^ref-1]

By 2014 he had been at Big Fish "for over 8 years" and had "designed nearly a dozen games", many of them early titles for an online community site that no longer exists.[^ref-4] He was Creative Director and Lead Designer of Fairway Solitaire.[^ref-25] His early credits include additional design on Mystic Inn (2006), Travelogue 360: Paris (2006) and Azada (2007), plus Azada's story.[^ref-26][^ref-27][^ref-9]

His most successful casual game was Fairway Solitaire (2007), a combination of golf and solitaire. As Cutter put it: "Mixing golf and solitaire doesn't sound great -- like getting sardines in your peanut butter -- but most people really enjoy the game when they play it."[^ref-4] A former junior golf champion who played two years on Pepperdine's golf team, he called it "a real labor of love". The game began as a free Big Fish community title before becoming a downloadable game.[^ref-33] By 2014 it was available on "PC, iOS, Android, Win 8 and more".[^ref-4][^ref-25]

Other Big Fish projects included Plunder! (2007), Azada: Ancient Magic (2008), Unwell Mel (2009) and Diana Fortune in the Lost Temple of Gold (2009).[^ref-9][^ref-29] Fairway Solitaire Blast followed for the iPhone in 2013.[^ref-30][^ref-9]

Fairway Solitaire was a finalist for both Casual Game of the Year and Mobile Game of the Year at the Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences' 16th D.I.C.E. Awards, which honored games of 2012 and were held on February 7, 2013.[^ref-37][^ref-6] His LinkedIn also lists the IMGA Excellence in Gameplay Award (2013), IGN's Best Mobile Board Game and Pixelated Sausage's Game of the Year (both 2012) among its honors.[^ref-6]

### Later Work (2014–Present)

MobyGames credits Cutter with game design on Wings!: Remastered Edition (2014), a re-release of his Cinemaware game.[^ref-1][^ref-9] He later joined Wooga.[^ref-6]

## Notable Works

### Defender of the Crown (1986)

Cutter was associate producer and one of the artists on Defender of the Crown, Cinemaware's first game.[^ref-12][^ref-9] Computer Gaming World ranked it #92 on its 1996 list of the 150 best games of all time.[^ref-35] Of its release he said: "After we released 'Defender of the Crown', we at Cinemaware were the rock stars of the industry for a while."[^ref-5]

### The Three Stooges (1987)

On The Three Stooges, Cutter is credited as producer, director, and concept and game designer.[^ref-15][^ref-9] Cinemaware founder Bob Jacob recalled that "It was John's idea to design it basically as a board game that we brought to the computer," and that its arcade scenes "came directly from their movies".[^ref-38]

### [[1993 - Betrayal at Krondor|Betrayal at Krondor]] (1993)

Cutter was designer and director of Betrayal at Krondor, based on Raymond E. Feist's Riftwar universe.[^ref-3][^ref-9] It was named 1993's best role-playing game and Game of the Year by Computer Games Strategy Plus and won Computer Gaming World's Role-Playing Game of the Year award in June 1994. CGW ranked it #43 on its 1996 list of the 150 best games of all time.[^ref-3][^ref-35]

### Fairway Solitaire (2007)

Cutter designed and produced Fairway Solitaire.[^ref-33] For the downloadable version, Big Fish worked with "a very talented programmer in England named Jake Birkett and Matt Laverty", an artist.[^ref-33] It was later released on Android, Windows 8, iOS and PC.[^ref-25]

## Design Philosophy

Cutter has said he is "easily inspired", naming his family, his co-workers, walks through Seattle, films, television, books and great games as sources. His favorite game of all time is M.U.L.E.[^ref-4]

His relationship with exceptional games is particularly noteworthy. Cutter explains: "When I play a really phenomenal game I don't want to play it anymore... I want to start creating MY masterwork!"[^ref-4]

For aspiring designers, he notes that "Game design is a creative skill that is difficult to show off," and encourages designers "to become proficient with a good prototyping tool."[^ref-4] He recommends a broad education: besides computer science, courses such as psychology, history, business, English and creative writing, anthropology and political science.[^ref-4]

## Legacy

As Cinemaware's first employee, Cutter worked on the company's games from Defender of the Crown through Wings.[^ref-1][^ref-9] Five games he worked on, Betrayal at Krondor, Rocket Ranger, Wings, Defender of the Crown and TV Sports: Football, are on Computer Gaming World's 1996 list of the 150 best games of all time.[^ref-35] Betrayal at Krondor, which he designed and directed with Neal Hallford, won Role-Playing Game of the Year and Game of the Year awards and was later novelized by Feist.[^ref-3] In casual games, Fairway Solitaire was a double finalist at the 16th D.I.C.E. Awards.[^ref-37]
