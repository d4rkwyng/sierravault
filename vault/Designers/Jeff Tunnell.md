# Jeff Tunnell

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 15, 2026</small>

## Overview

Jeffrey "Jeff" Tunnell is an American game designer, producer and entrepreneur. He co-founded Dynamix, designed or directed its cinematic adventure games of the early 1990s, and co-created The Incredible Machine with Kevin Ryan.[^ref-6][^ref-8][^ref-19] Before games he ran a computer store in downtown Eugene, Oregon.[^ref-6] Sega-16, introducing a 2005 interview, said he had "been involved in over seventy titles over the course of his career" and called him "a legend in the adventure gaming genre".[^ref-3]

Tunnell co-founded Dynamix with Damon Slye in 1984, set up Jeff Tunnell Productions in early 1992, and co-founded GarageGames in 2000.[^ref-6][^ref-19][^ref-13] Sega-16 wrote that his adventure games "brought gamers engrossing experiences that allowed them to get closer to their characters", and that "PC gamers have been enjoying the fruits of his labors for over twenty years".[^ref-3]

Beyond adventure games, Tunnell has said he is "credited with pioneering new game genres such as Outdoor Sports with Trophy Bass and Family Entertainment with the 3-D Ultra line of products". On those titles his credited role was usually Executive Producer. The line includes mass-market titles like [[1995 - 3-D Ultra Pinball|3-D Ultra Pinball]], [[1997 - 3-D Ultra MiniGolf|3-D Ultra MiniGolf]], and [[1999 - 3-D Ultra Cool Pool|3-D Ultra Cool Pool]].[^ref-5]

## Career

### Early Career and Dynamix Founding (1984–1989)

Jeff Tunnell's entry into the video game industry began in the mid-1980s when he co-founded Dynamix with Damon Slye in Eugene, Oregon.[^ref-6] As Kevin Ryan described the company's origins, "One day Damon came by the shop, showed Jeff the game, and a computer game publishing company was born."[^ref-6] Tunnell was operating a computer store in downtown Eugene when Slye, a University of Oregon student who had just completed [[1990 - Stellar 7|Stellar 7]] for the Apple II, arrived to show him the game.[^ref-6][^ref-7]

The company was initially called Software Entertainment Corporation, but within a year it changed its name to Dynamix and shifted its focus from publishing to game development.[^ref-6] Tunnell, Slye, Kevin Ryan, and Richard Hicks became the four original owner-partners, all around 23 years old and former University of Oregon students.[^ref-6] MobyGames lists Tunnell among the programmers of Arcticfox (1986), published by Electronic Arts, and among the creators of Skyfox II: The Cygnus Conflict (1987).[^ref-8]

Throughout these early years, Dynamix created original games and ported titles for various computer systems. Project Firestart (1989), designed by Tunnell and Damon Slye, was a horror game set in space with adventure game elements; Retro365 notes that it "is considered the first survival horror game".[^ref-7] MobyGames credits Tunnell as director of Caveman Ugh-lympics and F-14 Tomcat (both 1988), and of Project Firestart, Ghostbusters II and Deathtrack (1989). He was executive producer of A-10 Tank Killer and [[1989 - David Wolf - Secret Agent|David Wolf: Secret Agent]] (1989).[^ref-8]

### Sierra Acquisition and Adventure Game Era (1990–1991)

In late 1989, Sierra On-Line expressed interest in acquiring Dynamix. Ken Williams initially sought to license Dynamix's 3Space technology but quickly recognized the greater potential in acquiring the entire company.[^ref-7] The acquisition, valued at $1.5 million, was completed in August 1990, providing Dynamix with the financial stability to realize ambitious adventure game projects while maintaining nearly full creative autonomy.[^ref-7][^ref-6]

During his tenure at Dynamix, Tunnell was deeply intrigued by the potential for games to tell stories in a more cinematic way.[^ref-7] He admired adventure games for their narrative possibilities but found the text-parser-driven gameplay of the era cumbersome, believing that there had to be better ways to integrate storytelling with interactive gameplay.[^ref-7]

His influences during this period included the first two Alien films, Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), William Gibson's cyberpunk works, and adventure films like Raiders of the Lost Ark, Romancing the Stone, and High Road to China.[^ref-7] These cinematic influences shaped [[1990 - Rise of the Dragon|Rise of the Dragon]] (1990), [[1991 - Heart of China|Heart of China]] (1991), and [[1991 - The Adventures of Willy Beamish|The Adventures of Willy Beamish]] (1991).[^ref-7] MobyGames credits him with design on Rise of the Dragon, design, direction and the original story on Heart of China, and direction on Willy Beamish.[^ref-8]

Working on console adaptations presented unique challenges. Regarding the Sega CD development, Tunnell recalled, "Dynamix was anxious to get into the console market, but all of our products were too big for cartridges. We jumped on the chance to work on the Sega CD. Of course, we still had to get clearance from Sega before we could get dev kits, etc. I remember them being extremely arrogant and difficult to work with."[^ref-3] The technical limitations proved frustrating: "After working on the much more powerful PC platform, working on the Sega system was a nightmare. This was a difficult project."[^ref-3]

### Jeff Tunnell Productions (1992–1995)

By the time Willy Beamish was released in 1991, Dynamix had expanded to over 100 employees. Feeling burned out from large-scale productions, Tunnell yearned for the days of working with smaller teams.[^ref-7] With Ken Williams's support, he established Jeff Tunnell Productions (JTP) in early 1992, a smaller development studio located a few blocks away from Dynamix's main offices.[^ref-5][^ref-7][^ref-19][^ref-4]

At JTP, Tunnell and his team focused on casual puzzles and educational games.[^ref-7] Their output included [[1992 - The Incredible Machine|The Incredible Machine]] (1992), [[1992 - Quarky & Quaysoo's Turbo Science|Quarky & Quaysoo's Turbo Science]] (1992), [[1993 - Turbo Learning - Mega Math|Turbo Learning: Mega Math]] (1993), and [[1993 - Sid & Al's Incredible Toons|Sid & Al's Incredible Toons]] (1993).[^ref-8][^ref-19] In November 1994 Dynamix filed a patent, naming Tunnell and Chris Cole as inventors, for a "Computerized puzzle gaming method and apparatus"; it was granted in 1996 as US 5,577,185.[^ref-14]

The period also saw the creation of [[1995 - Trophy Bass|Trophy Bass]] (1995), whose credits list Tunnell as executive producer, and [[1995 - 3-D Ultra Pinball|3-D Ultra Pinball]] (1995).[^ref-20][^ref-5] As Tunnell later noted, "These products ended up being the most successful ever released from Dynamix."[^ref-5]

### Return to Dynamix Leadership (1995–2000)

"Circumstances drew me back into a leadership role at Dynamix in 1995," Tunnell said.[^ref-5] Once back, he continued his role as the product and technology visionary and creative spark, directing development of new game genres and on new platforms.[^ref-5] This period saw the expansion of the 3-D Ultra franchise with titles like [[1996 - 3-D Ultra Pinball - Creep Night|3-D Ultra Pinball: Creep Night]] (1996), [[1997 - 3-D Ultra MiniGolf|3-D Ultra MiniGolf]] (1997), on which he was co-designer and executive producer, and [[1997 - 3-D Ultra Pinball - The Lost Continent|3-D Ultra Pinball: The Lost Continent]] (1997).[^ref-8]

During this era, Tunnell served as Executive Producer on [[1998 - Starsiege - Tribes|Starsiege: Tribes]] (1998), which he lists as "Online Game of the Year, 1998".[^ref-5][^ref-8] He was also executive producer of [[1996 - Hunter Hunted|Hunter Hunted]] (1996) and [[1999 - Starsiege|Starsiege]] (1999). MobyGames credits him with the original concept of [[2000 - Return of The Incredible Machine - Contraptions|Return of The Incredible Machine: Contraptions]] (2000) and additional design on its 2001 follow-up.[^ref-8]

Looking back on Dynamix's closure, he said: "As you can probably tell, I am not a fan of greedy CEO's and big corporations. A couple of individuals at the top of a crumbling empire tore down what Ken Williams and hundreds of impassioned employees built over a fifteen-year period in a manner of months."[^ref-5]

### Post-Sierra Career (2000–Present)

Tunnell left Dynamix in early 2000.[^ref-5] In July 2000, Game Developer reported that Rick Overman, Tunnell and Tim Gift were starting GarageGames, quoting "Garage Games CEO Jeff Tunnell".[^ref-13] Its founders were Tunnell, Tim Gift, Rick Overman and Mark Frohnmayer, all of whom had worked together at Dynamix.[^ref-5] MobyGames' biography also says he founded Monster Studios with long-time collaborator Chris Cole in 1999.[^ref-8] The company was driven by his belief that "getting back to the fun of making games is my passion, and building a new segment of the gaming industry, the independent game development movement, is just a means to that end."[^ref-5]

GarageGames built "a large and vibrant development community of over 30,000 members working on over 1,000 games", and began selling games near the end of December 2002 with original titles such as Marble Blast, Chain Reaction, Orbz, Tennis Critters and Think Tanks.[^ref-5] The company was acquired by InterActiveCorp in 2007, with Tunnell remaining as Chief Creative Officer before leaving in 2008.[^ref-2]

In 2009, Tunnell founded PushButton Labs along with former partners from GarageGames and Dynamix. The company led development on Playdom's Social City (2010), which reached more than 10 million monthly active users.[^ref-2] Tunnell joined Playdom in 2010.[^ref-17] Disney published The Incredible Machine for iOS in 2011. TouchArcade wrote that its roughly 75 puzzles were "designed, in part, by Jeff Tunnel".[^ref-17][^ref-18] PushButton Labs' IP was acquired by Disney in 2011.[^ref-2]

Tunnell founded Spotkin in 2012, which released Contraption Maker in 2014.[^ref-2] On March 22, 2017, Tunnell announced his retirement from game development, citing market saturation as a deciding factor.[^ref-9] In June 2020 he announced his return to game making with a new company, Monster Ideas, Inc.[^ref-10]

## Notable Works

### The Incredible Machine (1992)

[[1992 - The Incredible Machine|The Incredible Machine]] was the machine-construction-set idea Tunnell had long carried. After he founded Jeff Tunnell Productions in early 1992, he judged it the right time to make it, with Kevin Ryan building the engine.[^ref-19] Players build Rube Goldberg-style contraptions from interchangeable parts. MobyGames credits Tunnell with design and as producer.[^ref-8] The game led to several sequels, on which his roles ranged from designer and producer to executive producer (see the Games list).[^ref-8][^ref-15][^ref-16]

Tunnell produced [[1993 - Sid & Al's Incredible Toons|Sid & Al's Incredible Toons]] (1993).[^ref-8] A patent naming Tunnell and Chris Cole as inventors was filed by Dynamix on November 10, 1994 and granted on November 19, 1996 (US 5,577,185, "Computerized puzzle gaming method and apparatus").[^ref-14]

### Rise of the Dragon (1990)

[[1990 - Rise of the Dragon|Rise of the Dragon]] drew on Tunnell's cinematic influences, particularly Blade Runner and William Gibson's cyberpunk works.[^ref-7] MobyGames credits him with its design.[^ref-8]

Tunnell had mixed feelings about the work: "The funny thing is, I never considered RoD my best work. After Willy Beamish, I moved on to other game types at JTP. I felt frustrated with the adventure game as a method of making fun games and telling great stories. Years later, I truly miss adventure games, and realize that the Dynamix adventure games were pretty good."[^ref-3]

### The Adventures of Willy Beamish (1991)

Tunnell directed [[1991 - The Adventures of Willy Beamish|The Adventures of Willy Beamish]].[^ref-8] He said it "pushed the technical envelope at the time with many industry firsts such as cell animations".[^ref-5] Retro365 reports that it sold about 80,000 copies in its first six months.[^ref-7]

A sequel, The Further Adventures of Willy Beamish, was in development but was quietly cancelled in 1992 following Tunnell's transition to Jeff Tunnell Productions.[^ref-7]

### Starsiege: Tribes (1998)

Tunnell was executive producer of [[1998 - Starsiege - Tribes|Starsiege: Tribes]], which he cites as "Online Game of the Year, 1998".[^ref-5][^ref-8]

## Design Philosophy

At Dynamix, Tunnell wanted games to tell stories in a more cinematic way, and he found the text parsers of the era cumbersome.[^ref-7] After Willy Beamish he "felt frustrated with the adventure game as a method of making fun games and telling great stories" and moved on to other game types.[^ref-3]

His philosophy on modern game development challenged conventional wisdom: "I actually call bullshit on the conventional wisdom! Games are easier to create than in any time in history and they will get easier." He compared game development to forming a rock band: "Making a game is a lot like being in a rock band. Get together with a couple of like-minded people, learn your different crafts (programming, art, audio), and make a wildly innovative and fun game."[^ref-5]

He described GarageGames' purpose in the same spirit: "Getting back to the fun of making games is my passion".[^ref-5]

## Legacy

Tunnell's best-known creation, The Incredible Machine, has continued in sequels, a 2011 iOS version and Spotkin's Contraption Maker.[^ref-2][^ref-17] The Digital Antiquarian notes that the founding ethos of Jeff Tunnell Productions, "to focus on small games that did one thing really, really well", could stand for "that of countless independent game studios working in the mobile and casual spaces today".[^ref-19]

GarageGames billed itself in 2000 as "the first independent label for independent games".[^ref-13] Tunnell's view that games are "easier to create than in any time in history" anticipated the small-team development he championed.[^ref-5]
