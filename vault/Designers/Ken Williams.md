# Ken Williams

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Kenneth "Ken" Williams, born on October 30, 1954, in Evansville, Indiana, co-founded On-Line Systems, later Sierra On-Line, with his wife [[Roberta Williams]].[^ref-17][^ref-16] After a nine-month programming course at the Control Data Institute in Los Angeles, from which he "graduated top of my class," he spent the 1970s as a programmer and contractor in Los Angeles.[^ref-17] He was Sierra's chairman and CEO from 1979 until its sale to CUC International in 1996, by which time the company was "a public company with 1,000 employees."[^ref-17]

Williams programmed several of the company's earliest games, but his main job at Sierra was running the business while Roberta and other designers made the games.[^ref-15][^ref-6] In his own words: "I'm not a game designer, so I tend to focus on the technology, the marketing, and the economic side of things with gaming."[^ref-6]

## Career

### Early Career

Williams took a computer programming course in college in 1973, then left college for the nine-month Control Data Institute course.[^ref-17] From then until 1979 he "worked for about anyone who was doing anything with computers in Los Angeles," with full-time programming jobs by day and contract work at night; his last full-time job was at a company doing compiler development.[^ref-17] He introduced Roberta to William Crowther's text game *Colossal Cave Adventure*, which "consumed her";[^ref-7] it helped inspire their first game, Mystery House.[^ref-6]

### Sierra Years (1979–1997)

Williams and Roberta founded On-Line Systems in 1979; in 1982 it was renamed Sierra On-Line and moved to Oakhurst, California.[^ref-16][^ref-1] Their first game, [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 1 - Mystery House|Mystery House]] (1980), is considered the first graphic adventure.[^ref-1] Williams was "Sierra's Chairman and CEO throughout the 18 year period from 1979 to 1996."[^ref-17] He described the division of labour in a 2022 interview: "I ran the company while she shipped all of her hits, but other than occasionally being involved in decisions that affected her products, I didn't really work directly with her."[^ref-6] Franchises developed under his supervision included [[1984 - King's Quest - Quest for the Crown|King's Quest]], [[1987 - Leisure Suit Larry in the Land of the Lounge Lizards|Leisure Suit Larry]], [[1986 - Space Quest - The Sarien Encounter|Space Quest]], Gabriel Knight, Laura Bow and Phantasmagoria.[^ref-1]

Sierra took venture capital in the early 1980s. Williams wrote in his memoir: "Once we had accepted venture capital, it became like any other drug. No one stops after the first hit. ... We brought in a second round of venture capital."[^ref-7]

Sierra was sold to CUC International in July 1996. Williams said in 2005: "After 18 years running Sierra I was getting burnt out."[^ref-1][^ref-16] He stayed on to guide Sierra's strategic direction until November 1997,[^ref-16] and while at CUC he built one of the first internet shopping systems, NetMarket.[^ref-13] CUC merged with HFS in December 1997 to form Cendant, and on April 15, 1998, Cendant announced that it had found accounting irregularities in CUC business units.[^ref-20][^ref-24] Williams later said of CUC's former chief executive Walter Forbes and president Kirk Shelton, both indicted: "To this day, I have trouble believing they were crooks. Walter was on Sierra's board for many years, and there was never any hint of it."[^ref-1] He has also called the sale "the absolute worst thing that could have happened," adding: "if I could go back and not sell the company, I would happily do so."[^ref-2] He blamed "the lack of leadership immediately after the acquisition."[^ref-2]

From 1988 to 1997 Williams was credited as Executive Producer on many Sierra releases (listed below); MobyGames lists him on 90 games in all.[^ref-15] He said of the title: "The Executive Producer title didn't really mean anything. I forget if it was me, or someone else, who made it up." But, he added, "I was the one who decided if a game got made, who would make it, how much money would be spent on developing the game, and how much would be spent to promote the game."[^ref-17]

### Post-Sierra Career

After retiring in 1998, Williams started Talkspot, a dot-com for entertainment broadcasting on the web, which became WorldStream before he retired again.[^ref-17] In 2003 he created SierraGamers, a site for Sierra fans.[^ref-13] He and Roberta spent the following years cruising on their Nordhavn trawler *Sans Souci*, and he wrote books about the trips.[^ref-13] A noncompete agreement with Sierra's buyer, which he has given as five and perhaps ten years, kept them out of games; "by the time it expired," he said, "we had gotten deeply into our cruising around the world and were not interested in going back into the business."[^ref-7] His memoir, *Not All Fairy Tales Have Happy Endings: The Rise and Fall of Sierra On-Line*, was released on July 24, 2020.[^ref-16]

Roberta says the idea for a new game came in January or February 2021, when Ken "needed a new project" after his book and she suggested *Colossal Cave*.[^ref-6] The result was [[2023 - Colossal Cave 3D Adventure|Colossal Cave 3D Adventure]], made by their company Cygnus Entertainment, with Ken as managing partner and Roberta as lead creative director.[^ref-6] It was their first new game in about 25 years.[^ref-7][^ref-18] Ken compared the pair to Rip Van Winkle: "It's like we awakened nearly 30 years later to a very different world."[^ref-6] On working together, he said: "We're each accustomed to being the final decision maker on everything we touch. We've mapped out territories where I make the decisions (implementation, finance, marketing) and where she makes the decisions (game design, art)."[^ref-6] The project grew past his plans: "The original vision for the game was that I'd do 100% of everything myself. I wanted it to be a nice, small, simple project," he wrote in March 2022, when the team had reached 15 people.[^ref-3]

## Notable Works

### Hi-Res Adventure 1: Mystery House (1980)

On [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 1 - Mystery House|Mystery House]], Roberta's first game, MobyGames lists Ken's credit for the Apple II original ("Written by") under programming.[^ref-15] Roberta dates the game's release to May 1980.[^ref-6] Ken also has design credits on [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 0 - Mission Asteroid|Mission Asteroid]], [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 2 - The Wizard and the Princess|The Wizard and the Princess]] and [[1981 - Hi-Res Adventure 4 - Ulysses and the Golden Fleece|Ulysses and the Golden Fleece]].[^ref-15]

### Sierra's engines

Williams explained that "Sierra had a seperate technology group, from the games groups. The technology group built the code that wasn't game specific (mostly AGI, SCI and various animation tools)." His own part was defining the technology for products and holding a project review with each team every 90 days.[^ref-17]

### Colossal Cave 3D Adventure (2023)

On [[2023 - Colossal Cave 3D Adventure|Colossal Cave]] (2023), Williams is credited as Lead Engineer and Executive Producer.[^ref-15] He built it in Unity after teaching himself the engine.[^ref-22]

## Design Philosophy

Williams was famous inside Sierra for what he called "Kens Laws," principles he repeated so consistently that "almost everyone in every meeting knew what I was going to say before I said it."[^ref-17] He believed that "Sierra succeeded because we managed the balance between creative needs and schedule needs better than our competitors."[^ref-17] In 2005 he said: "Sierra certainly made our share of mistakes, but the one thing we always did right, was that we were never boring. We believed strongly in pushing the envelope, and trying new things. We were cool-driven, not 'me too'-driven."[^ref-1]

On returning to games in 2022, his first reaction was that "Software development is tougher now than it has ever been," though in the same post he found that free engines and asset stores had made much of the work easier.[^ref-22] He told Kotaku: "The competition is much more challenging. There are tens of thousands of great game developers. The market is larger, but building a competitive product is not easy."[^ref-6]

## Legacy

Sierra launched The Sierra Network, a graphical online service, in 1991; it later became the ImagiNation Network and was bought by AT&T in 1994.[^ref-1] Williams called it "our games network" and named giving up on it as a decision he would change: "It would have been a billion-dollar business today if we'd just stuck with it."[^ref-2]

In 2014 Ken and Roberta received the inaugural Industry Icon award at The Game Awards.[^ref-16][^ref-21]
