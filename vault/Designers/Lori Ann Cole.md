# Lori Ann Cole

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Lori Ann Cole is an American game designer, director and writer best known for co-designing Sierra On-Line's [[Quest for Glory Series|Quest for Glory]] series with her husband [[Corey Cole]].[^ref-21] Adventure Classic Gaming calls the series "among the earliest hybrid role-playing/adventure game series ever created."[^ref-13] Her background includes elementary education, film animation and writing.[^ref-14] In 2008 Strange Horizons credited her with "twenty years' experience in the design, direction, and scriptwriting of computer games."[^ref-34] Jimmy Maher quotes her aim in designing computer games: "to bring storytelling and [the] interesting plot lines of books and tabletop role-playing into the hack-and-slash thrill of a computer game."[^ref-12]

## Career

### Early Career

Lori met Corey Cole at Westercon in San Francisco in the summer of 1979, at a Dungeons & Dragons game he was running; at the time Corey was programming computers in Southern California and Lori taught school in Arizona.[^ref-12] Within a few years they married.[^ref-12] Corey later said that the Quest for Glory rules borrowed partly from the "Arizona D&D" variant Lori played with her friends in the late '70s.[^ref-34] Their first joint project was *The Spellbook*, a newsletter for tabletop-RPG players that they edited and self-published.[^ref-12]

In a 2003 interview she described herself: "I have been a reader and a lover of fantasy and mythology most of my life. I've also been a game player and a role player."[^ref-2] She also said: "As a child, I always wanted to be a teacher, and I have taught school on an Indian Reservation."[^ref-2] In 2008 she called herself "a teacher by profession and inclination."[^ref-8]

### Sierra Years (1988–1998)

A science-fiction fan friend introduced the Coles to Ken Williams in 1988,[^ref-36][^ref-15] and Corey was hired first, "as a programmer, not as a designer."[^ref-31] Ken Williams then invited Lori to propose a role-playing game;[^ref-35] in her own account she proposed *Hero's Quest* herself while she "worked in house at Sierra", first conceiving it "as a fairly serious Role-playing Game with Adventure Game aspects."[^ref-31] The Coles' 2024 account says general manager Rick Cavin and Ken Williams were interested in her background as a teacher, and that she "was about to start directing a game project with one assigned programmer, one artist, and no team leads."[^ref-35] Adventure Classic Gaming says she was first hired by Sierra "only as a freelance contractor" and later became a full-time employee for her work on Quest for Glory.[^ref-13] Jimmy Maher writes: "Like many dedicated players of tabletop RPGs, she always felt the computerized variety to be lacking in texture, story, and most of all freedom."[^ref-12] In her own words: "I really enjoyed RPG games, and had never really played an adventure game before working for Sierra. Unfortunately, most computer RPG's at the time were all monster crawls and very little real role-playing. I wanted to play a game with story and characters."[^ref-2]

In 1989 Sierra released *Hero's Quest: So You Want to Be a Hero*, which MobyGames credits her with directing and writing.[^ref-9] Getting it to work in Sierra's engine was hard at first: "I had to rewrite the entire game because I had no clue of how to correctly do it with our engines and things," she told PC Gamer.[^ref-32] The series was later renamed Quest for Glory. Choicest Games says the original name sounded very similar to the HeroQuest board game,[^ref-3] PC Gamer says a videogame version of HeroQuest "necessitated a renaming",[^ref-32] and Corey Cole's MobyGames biography cites "trademark issues".[^ref-36] Computer Gaming World named *Hero's Quest* its 1990 Adventure Game of the Year, listing "Lori Ann and Corey Cole (designers)" and praising it for "adding a twist of role-playing and a sprinkle of humor."[^ref-28] Of the Paladin she said: "My concept of Paladin was slightly different. A Paladin was someone who does what is right regardless of the laws or other people's opinion."[^ref-2]

[[1990 - Quest for Glory II - Trial by Fire|Quest for Glory II: Trial by Fire]] (1990) was made under Sierra's new development processes, which slowed the work.[^ref-12] Lori recalled: "We got the worst of both worlds. We got a new [development] system that had never been tried before, and all the bugs that went with that. And we were doing it under the old-school technology where the colors weren't as good and all that. We were under a new administration with a different way of treating people."[^ref-12] The series continued with Quest for Glory III: Wages of War (1992) and Quest for Glory IV: Shadows of Darkness (1993).[^ref-21] Of the Quest for Glory games, Corey recalled that "Lori came up with most of the characters and wrote the lion's share of their dialogue."[^ref-33] She also designed and directed the educational game [[1991 - Mixed-Up Fairy Tales|Mixed-Up Fairy Tales]] (1991).[^ref-9][^ref-21]

Corey Cole recalled that "Sierra broke our contract after QfG4 shipped";[^ref-33] Wikipedia places the break in Sierra's 1994 downsizing.[^ref-21] The Coles formed FAR Productions and, after Legend Entertainment's president Bob Bates suggested they make a game for Legend, made [[1995 - Shannara|Shannara]] (1995).[^ref-33][^ref-21][^ref-14][^ref-13] According to PC Gamer, it took "fans staging a mail-in campaign" to make Sierra bring the Coles back;[^ref-32] Wikipedia says Lori returned "the following year" after the 1994 downsizing, as the sole designer of [[1998 - Quest for Glory V - Dragon Fire|Quest for Glory V: Dragon Fire]].[^ref-21] The game's credits list her as Designer and thank the teams "at the Yosemite Entertainment development site" who helped in the final stretch.[^ref-37] By Corey's account it "took almost 4 years to develop, versus the original 18 month schedule";[^ref-33] it was released in 1998, and Sierra closed its [[Yosemite Entertainment]] office in 1999.[^ref-21][^ref-36] In 1998 a photograph of her face was also used for a character in Police Quest: SWAT 2.[^ref-21][^ref-9]

### Later Career

Asked in October 2003 whether she missed working for Sierra, she answered: "Not in the least. ;-)"[^ref-2] In the same interview, asked about the Quest for Glory rights then held by Vivendi, she said: "We'd love to acquire the rights. Unfortunately, we haven't been able to negotiate with Vivendi for them."[^ref-2] By 2008 she said, "Corey and I do not have the rights to QFG."[^ref-8]

In 2003 the Coles began a project based on *How to Be a Hero*, a book Lori was co-writing that was never published, with a website of the same name.[^ref-21][^ref-30] After running that site for about three years they lost it and renamed the idea School for Heroes, an email-based web game.[^ref-21][^ref-25] In late 2011 they stopped taking assignments there and turned the idea into a game; their Kickstarter for Hero-U launched in October 2012.[^ref-21][^ref-25] Their company [[Transolar Games]] released [[2018 - Hero-U - Rogue to Redemption|Hero-U: Rogue to Redemption]] in 2018, followed by [[2023 - Summer Daze - Tilly's Tale|Summer Daze: Tilly's Tale]] in 2023.[^ref-26][^ref-21][^ref-36]

## Notable Works

### Quest for Glory I: So You Want to Be a Hero (1989)

Players chose a fighter, magic user or thief, each with its own approach to problems, so the Coles in effect had to design the game three times.[^ref-12] Computer Gaming World's 1990 Adventure Game of the Year citation credited Lori Ann and Corey Cole as designers.[^ref-28] MobyGames lists her as director and writer of the 1989 release.[^ref-9]

### Quest for Glory II: Trial by Fire (1990)

The sequel was the game made under Sierra's new development system that Lori described as "the worst of both worlds."[^ref-12] She also wrote its FACS manual.[^ref-9]

### Quest for Glory III: Wages of War (1992)

MobyGames credits her with design, direction, writing, the "Field Guide" and a voice role on Quest for Glory III.[^ref-9]

## Design Philosophy

Cole has said that the series was meant to make players think: "The QFG series requires that a player think about what he is doing and have to make moral decisions. The player really becomes a Hero through the course of play, and hopefully carries some of the concepts of heroism onward in real life."[^ref-2] In 2008 she added that she "was happy to do educational games" and that the series "actually had underlying lessons in leadership."[^ref-8]

## Legacy

Computer Gaming World's 1990 award recognised the series' mix of adventure and role-playing from the start.[^ref-28] The Quest for Glory II VGA Remake (2008) by AGD Interactive carries the Coles' original "Written & Designed by" credit,[^ref-9] and Lori and Corey returned to the adventure-RPG hybrid with Hero-U.[^ref-21][^ref-26]
