# Roberta Williams

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 16, 2026</small>

## Overview

Roberta Williams, born in 1953, is widely recognized as the pioneer of graphic adventure games; HistoryLink notes she became known as the "Mother" and "Queen" of video adventure games, and IEEE Spectrum calls her "The Queen of Graphic Adventure Video Games."[^ref-4][^ref-5] She met Ken Williams in 1970, when she was 17, at John H. Francis Polytechnic High School in Sun Valley, a suburb of Los Angeles.[^ref-4] According to the Lemelson-MIT program, "Williams had no experience in gaming or computers – only in simple reading and storytelling" when she designed her first game, Mystery House, which is considered the first graphic adventure game.[^ref-3][^ref-4]

Williams co-founded On-Line Systems (later Sierra On-Line) with her husband Ken Williams in 1979.[^ref-4][^ref-19] Over nearly two decades she designed games including the King's Quest series, the first in what IEEE Spectrum calls "a series of eight."[^ref-5] Looking back in 2006, she said: "The experience of creating my adventure games was, other than marrying my husband and bringing into the world my two sons, the most fulfilling, wonderful experience I could ever had."[^ref-12]

Williams left Sierra On-Line in 1999 to travel.[^ref-22][^ref-4] She and Ken received the Industry Icon Award at The Game Awards 2014, and in 2020 she was named the recipient of the Pioneer Award at the 20th Game Developers Choice Awards.[^ref-1][^ref-24][^ref-23] She returned to writing and game development in 2021, with a historical novel, *Farewell to Tara*, and the game that became [[2023 - Colossal Cave 3D Adventure|Colossal Cave 3D Adventure]].[^ref-25][^ref-20][^ref-7]

## Career

### Early Career

Roberta Williams came to adventure games through her husband's work. In 1979 Ken Williams was working from home on an income tax program for an IBM mainframe, using a teletype machine, when he found a program called "Adventure": Colossal Cave Adventure. Roberta began playing it on the teletype in their home and "was hooked."[^ref-4] For Christmas 1979 the couple bought an Apple II.[^ref-4] Looking for more games of the kind, she found few: "If this was all there was, she thought, then there was a need there, and she thought she might be the one who could fill it," as Lemelson-MIT's profile puts it.[^ref-3]

Drawing inspiration from Agatha Christie's *And Then There Were None* and the board game Clue, Williams designed her first game, Mystery House, in 1980.[^ref-4][^ref-5] Ken supplied the programming on their Apple II.[^ref-4] The game opens: "You are in the front yard of a large abandoned Victorian house. Stone steps lead up to a wide porch."[^ref-1] Its graphics were simple black-and-white line drawings, with no animation, no sound and no colour; HistoryLink calls it "the first graphic adventure game ever created."[^ref-4]

Mystery House was released on May 5, 1980, advertised in the May issue of *MICRO*.[^ref-4] Accounts of its sales differ:
- Steven Levy's *Hackers*, as quoted by Laine Nooney, says the couple "made eleven thousand dollars that May. In June, they made twenty thousand dollars. July was thirty thousand."[^ref-28] HistoryLink likewise gives $11,000 by the end of the first month.[^ref-4]
- AIGA Eye on Design and Williams' Wikipedia article give 10,000 copies sold.[^ref-13][^ref-25]
- Wikipedia's Sierra Entertainment article (and the vault's [[Sierra History|Sierra On-Line History]] guide) give about 15,000 copies and US$167,000 in revenue.[^ref-29] Neither source that article cites for the passage (Nooney 2017; Jimmy Maher's *Digital Antiquarian*) contains those two figures, so treat them as unconfirmed.

### Sierra Years (1979–1999)

The game was sold under the name of Ken's consulting company, On-Line Systems.[^ref-25] AGH (2023) dates the founding of On-Line Systems to 1979, matching HistoryLink's account of how the business began; MobyGames' biography says 1980, the year Mystery House shipped.[^ref-19][^ref-4][^ref-15] The company was soon renamed Sierra On-Line.[^ref-4]

Mystery House was the first of six Hi-Res Adventures. IEEE Spectrum says that "between 1980 and 1982" the couple created [[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 2 - The Wizard and the Princess|The Wizard and the Princess]], "the first adventure game with color graphics," and Time Zone; the last of the six, The Dark Crystal, followed in 1983.[^ref-5] HistoryLink says The Wizard and the Princess was released in September 1980 and sold more than 60,000 copies.[^ref-4]

Her breakthrough came with [[1984 - King's Quest - Quest for the Crown|King's Quest]]. In 1983 IBM approached On-Line Systems for a game for its new PCjr home computer; Williams recalled in 1984 that "IBM came to me a long time ago and asked me to write an adventure-type game for the forthcoming PCjr."[^ref-4][^ref-5] IEEE Spectrum calls the result "the first animated 3D adventure game."[^ref-5] She explained her approach: "The first King's Quest was really a compendium of many of the most common fairy tales, and, really was nothing but a big fairy tale that someone could directly experience in a very interactive way instead of in the old passive way of books, movies, or oral tales."[^ref-12]

King's Quest became Sierra's flagship franchise, with Williams designing all eight main entries through [[1998 - King's Quest - Mask of Eternity|Mask of Eternity]] (1998).[^ref-5][^ref-15] Her approach was personal: "As a young girl, I always had enjoyed the old fairy tales of yore. I read them and re-read them. Therefore, when thinking about designing a game, I naturally gravitated to what I liked and felt comfortable with."[^ref-12]

### Post-Sierra (1999–Present)

Ars Technica reported in 2006 that she "in 1999 left Sierra On-Line to pursue some real-life adventures in travel."[^ref-22] During the COVID-19 pandemic she wrote a historical novel set in mid-1800s Ireland, *Farewell to Tara*, which she self-published in 2021.[^ref-27][^ref-25][^ref-26]

She dates the idea for a new game to January or February 2021, when Ken "needed a new project" after finishing his book.[^ref-7] On June 1, 2021, their company Cygnus Entertainment announced that Ken and Roberta Williams would release a new, "Sierra flavored" game, then titled *The Secret*.[^ref-20] It became Colossal Cave 3D Adventure (sold as *Colossal Cave*), released in 2023, with Roberta as creative director; Ars Technica called it their "first video game in 25 years."[^ref-15][^ref-27]

Williams was careful to clarify her role: "I'm not the designer, and this is not an original game. I call myself the transmuter. I am taking an old historical adventure game, arguably the first adventure computer game in existence, and bringing it into the modern era."[^ref-7] Of the renewed attention, she said: "I have been appreciative, humbled, honored, and kind of sorry that I haven't been as responsive for too long."[^ref-7]

## Notable Works

### Mystery House (1980)

[[1980 - Hi-Res Adventure 1 - Mystery House|Mystery House]] is the game Smithsonian calls "the first home computer game ever to include graphics as well as text," a murder mystery in which seven people in a house are killed off one by one.[^ref-1][^ref-5] Its success launched the couple's business.[^ref-4]

### King's Quest (1984)

[[1984 - King's Quest - Quest for the Crown|King's Quest: Quest for the Crown]] was made at IBM's request for the PCjr and introduced an animated, player-controlled character in a fairy-tale world.[^ref-4][^ref-5] Williams described it as "a big fairy tale that someone could directly experience in a very interactive way."[^ref-12]

### Phantasmagoria (1995)

[[1995 - Phantasmagoria|Phantasmagoria]] was Williams' most ambitious and most controversial project, a live-action horror game. AIGA Eye on Design reports that it cost $4.5 million to produce, "occupied a whopping seven CD-ROMs," and was banned from some retailers; it made $12 million in its first week and remained "Sierra's top-seller to this day."[^ref-13]

## Design Philosophy

Williams' games grew out of her love of fairy tales and storytelling, as her own account of King's Quest shows.[^ref-12] She resisted being categorized by gender: "I prefer being thought of as a computer game designer rather than a woman computer game designer; I don't put myself into gender mode when designing a game."[^ref-6]

Ken Williams, in a January 2021 Wired interview, described her as "a perfectionist, and extremely smart, intuitive and usually right. She can't be managed."[^ref-21]

## Legacy

Roberta Williams is credited with pioneering the graphic adventure genre; HistoryLink calls Mystery House "the first graphic adventure game ever created."[^ref-4] Ars Technica described her in 2006 as "one of the more iconic figures in adventure gaming."[^ref-22] According to AIGA Eye on Design, the King's Quest series had sold 7 million copies by 1997.[^ref-13]

In 2014 she and Ken received the inaugural Industry Icon Award at The Game Awards, held in Las Vegas on December 5, 2014.[^ref-1][^ref-24] In 2020 the organizers of the 20th Game Developers Choice Awards chose her for the Pioneer Award, "which recognizes breakthrough tech and game design milestones."[^ref-23]

On modern games, she has said: "it just seems to me that I don't see a lot of originality anymore. It seems that there are a lot of the same games. They just make the same kind of game but bigger and more complex."[^ref-7]
