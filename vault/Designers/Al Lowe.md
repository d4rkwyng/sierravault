# Al Lowe

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: November 12, 2026</small>

## Overview

Albert William "Al" Lowe was born on July 24, 1946, and grew up in Chesterfield, Missouri, a suburb of St. Louis.[^ref-4] He has long called himself "the world's oldest game designer."[^ref-4][^ref-17] He holds a BS and an MS in music education from the University of Missouri-Columbia, was a professional musician from the age of 13, and taught music in public schools for 15 years before he joined Sierra On-Line in 1982.[^ref-5][^ref-24][^ref-4][^ref-40] He is best known as the creator of the Leisure Suit Larry series, but his Sierra credits also include Disney adventures for children, lead programming on King's Quest III, King's Quest IV and the original Police Quest, and music for King's Quest II and Space Quest II.[^ref-7][^ref-25] His own credit list names 26 products he worked on "in various capacities" during his 16 years with the company.[^ref-25]

Lowe has said he deliberately aimed Larry at the mainstream adventure audience rather than at an adult niche: "I didn't want to produce a pornographic game, I wanted to make a comedy. I thought a pornographic game wouldn't sell as well as a broad-based adventure game. I went after the people who bought King's Quest, Space Quest and all those other 'quest' games. I just gave them a different kind of quest."[^ref-12]

## Career

### Early Career

Lowe bought an Apple II around 1978–79, taught himself to program with no formal training, and wrote three games in three months while still teaching full-time. The first was an educational game written for his four-year-old son.[^ref-5] He published those first games himself. When Ken and Roberta Williams saw them, they suggested he leave the publishing to Sierra and concentrate on making more games.[^ref-5] According to a biography on his website, when Sierra bought the rights to his titles in 1982, "it took the man as well."[^ref-7] A trade newsletter dated April 10, 1983 reported the deal as news, saying Sierra "has acquired the product line originally developed by Sunnyside Soft".[^ref-30]

Lowe's own credit list dates Dragon's Keep, Bop-A-Bet and Troll's Tale to 1982.[^ref-25] MobyGames lists the Apple II release of Troll's Tale as 1983.[^ref-19] He then programmed Gelfling Adventure, a children's game based on The Dark Crystal that is "controlled with the space bar and return key," so players don't need to type. Its manual reads "Programmed by: Al Lowe," with text and educational concepts by Michael and Rae Lynn MacChesney.[^ref-28] He also did interface design on Sierra's HomeWord Speller and HomeWord Filer.[^ref-25]

### Sierra Years

Sierra bought Texas Instruments' existing Disney licence rather than paying Disney's usual upfront fee, and most of the activity under it stopped well before the licence expired.[^ref-29] Under it Lowe worked on Winnie the Pooh in the Hundred Acre Wood, Mickey's Space Adventure and Donald Duck's Playground.[^ref-7][^ref-24] Gamasutra describes him as "the lead programmer for parts of King's Quest and Police Quest."[^ref-12] He was lead programmer on both King's Quest III and the original Police Quest, and his credit list adds King's Quest IV.[^ref-24][^ref-25] He also wrote music for King's Quest II, Mickey's Space Adventure and Space Quest II.[^ref-7][^ref-25] Sierra's tools were primitive. Art was built up in a vector drawing program, and as Lowe recalled, "When you look at those early Larry games, remember that everything you see was drawn one tiny line at a time."[^ref-12]

Lowe says the animation process improved once Sierra borrowed a trick Disney animators had used in the 1920s: alignment with registration marks. He hedges on whose idea that was ("well, I think it may have been me!"), but he does describe suggesting that a registration mark be placed inside each animation cel, so that the animator could tell the programmer where to align it.[^ref-12] He also wrote macros for an early Logitech three-button mouse that, he said, "floated around Sierra for years."[^ref-12] Finding artists was hard: "There was no such thing as a computer artist. In fact, we had difficulty hiring any artists to create the games, because back then art majors and those with artistic bent wanted to stay away from computers."[^ref-12]

His first 3-D animated graphic adventure was The Black Cauldron, based on the 1985 Disney film.[^ref-24] He has said the game was built in the game room of Ken Williams's house during a lean period at Sierra, with Mark Crowe, Scott Murphy and Ken himself writing code around a big table.[^ref-5] Of Disney's oversight he said: "We realized quickly that they insisted on having input. But the longer we could delay them seeing the product, the less changes they could make! They never made improvements, just made things different."[^ref-12] In a February 2026 interview with Spillhistorie.no he described the same approach to his Disney work: he told Disney's staff he was too busy to talk until a game was done, and when they then sent a long list of changes (a red leaf instead of a green one, for example), Ken Williams's answer was to "ship it." He also named Goofy's Word Factory as another Disney title he worked on that was never released.[^ref-33]

Accounts differ on how Leisure Suit Larry began. IGN's 2014 account says Ken Williams asked Lowe whether he wanted to do a graphical remake of Chuck Benton's adult text adventure Softporn Adventure.[^ref-26][^ref-12] In Lowe's own telling to Adventure Classic Gaming, it was he who "remembered Softporn's success" after Sierra lost its Disney rights.[^ref-5] Both accounts agree that he found the game badly dated. In the ACG version he told Ken, "There's no way I can do this as a serious game. It's so out of it that it should be wearing a leisure suit," and added, "But if you let me mock it, I might be able to do a spoof of it."[^ref-5] Benton, in a December 2025 interview with Spillhistorie.no, said Ken Williams offered him either a one percent royalty or a check for $5,000 for the story, and that he took the $5,000.[^ref-34] Leisure Suit Larry in the Land of the Lounge Lizards followed in 1987.[^ref-25]

Lowe and his family moved to the Seattle area in 1994.[^ref-24] He was laid off in February 1999 (his own bio says 1998), one of several veteran Sierra designers let go in a mass layoff.[^ref-11][^ref-24][^ref-7] Lowe's own later account of his status is more complicated. In the February 2026 interview he said that after he resigned from teaching to work for Williams full-time, the company cut its staff from 120 to 40 in a single day about six months in, himself included. Williams then asked him to keep working on advances against future royalties, and "for the next 15 and a half years I was an outside contractor who only worked on a contract basis."[^ref-33]

### Post-Sierra Career

Lowe was not involved in Vivendi's Leisure Suit Larry: Magna Cum Laude, and he had to buy a copy at full price when it came out. His reaction: "it was like receiving a ransom video from my son's kidnappers: on one hand, it was nice to know he was still alive, but oh God, look what they've done to him!"[^ref-5]

By his own later account he was not retired after leaving Sierra. He told FiringSquad on December 1, 2006 that he was a part owner of iBase Games, developer of the action-comedy Sam Suede: Undercover Exposure; that his last day there was September 11, when most of the team of about 20 learned the company was shutting down; and that the game had been in deep production for about a year. He said publishers had told the team a PC-only release would not sell enough copies to be worthwhile, and that he doubted he would try to get into the game industry again.[^ref-35] In 2010 the Binary Mill released Al Lowe's Comedy Club, a 99-cent joke app for iPhone, iPod and iPad. The developer's page described it as made by "the man behind the original Leisure Suit Larry series" with comedian Chuck Myers, who performed the recorded jokes.[^ref-36]

He has turned down offers to collaborate on fan games: "Once you have been at the top of the hill, had the best tools, the best artists, and the best programmers at your disposal, it doesn't sound fun to start over with nothing."[^ref-5] He did return to the series for Replay Games' Kickstarter-funded Leisure Suit Larry: Reloaded (2013).[^ref-17] He left Replay on December 11, 2013. Replay called the departure amicable, but Lowe disputed this, saying the parting did not happen on good terms.[^ref-4][^ref-17]

In November 2018 Lowe showed his personal archive of Sierra floppies, boxes, manuals and source code in a video with former Sierra employee MetalJesusRocks, then began selling it on eBay.[^ref-8][^ref-18][^ref-4] In 2026 he told Spillhistorie.no that Activision, which he called Sierra's current owner, sent him a cease-and-desist letter warning that the disks might hold material it owned, and that he pulled the sale.[^ref-33] He explained, "I'm 72 years old, and none of my kids want this junk! Does anybody?"[^ref-8] He also said, "I backed everything up because I knew Sierra didn't."[^ref-8] In January 2024 he gave a three-hour video interview about his career to Daniel Albu's Tech Talk, by which time he was a nominee for the inaugural Adventure Game Hall of Fame.[^ref-1] Outside games, he has said he plays lead alto saxophone in two big bands and has built N-scale model railways for about 35 years.[^ref-33]

## Notable Works

### Leisure Suit Larry in the Land of the Lounge Lizards (1987)

Lowe kept Softporn Adventure's puzzles and locations but, as he told Adventure Classic Gaming, threw out "every line of text";[^ref-5] in another interview he said he kept Benton's puzzles "and one sentence of dialog."[^ref-12] Because the original had no central character, he invented Larry as the butt of the jokes.[^ref-5] He is not Larry, he has said, but he is in the games: "I'm the narrator. When Larry asks some dumb question and the narrator responds with some smart ass remark, that's me."[^ref-5] The game was also the first Sierra title to use beta-testers from outside the company.[^ref-5] Lowe said that, with no Radio Shack distribution and no marketing money, sales "started out really bad and grew month after month," spread entirely by word of mouth. A chart he kept from July 1988, 13 months after release, still showed the game rising.[^ref-20] He also composed the Larry theme, which is credited across the series.[^ref-17][^ref-19]

### The Black Cauldron (1986)

Lowe's own credit list gives him design, writing, music and programming on The Black Cauldron.[^ref-25] The 1986 date here follows MobyGames,[^ref-19] but contemporary sources place the IBM PC release in late 1985: a Standard & Poor's stock report on Sierra lists "The Black Cauldron (10/85)" among its products,[^ref-31] and Sierra's December 1985 advertisement in Creative Computing announces it as "NEW!" for the IBM PC/PCjr.[^ref-32] Roberta Williams was co-designer: Jimmy Maher writes that it was "largely designed by Roberta and programmed, as usual, by Al Lowe."[^ref-29] He built it without a mouse: "I did Black Cauldron entirely with the keyboard."[^ref-12] In 2026 he told Spillhistorie.no that he feels he "invented the point-and-click adventure," because the whole game could be played with a mouse or joystick, with function keys used instead of clicking icons.[^ref-33] MobyGames credits him with game design and programming across the PC Booter, DOS, Apple II, Atari ST, Apple IIGS and Amiga versions.[^ref-19]

### The Laffer Utilities (1991)

The Laffer Utilities was a "nonproductivity" spin-off of the Larry series that parodied the Norton Utilities. Wikipedia dates its release to 1991, credits Lowe as designer, and reports that it was a commercial failure.[^ref-27] Lowe's own credit list dates it to 1990 and credits him with design and writing.[^ref-25]

### Freddy Pharkas: Frontier Pharmacist (1993)

Lowe designed and wrote Freddy Pharkas with Josh Mandel.[^ref-25][^ref-4] It is a comic Western about a meek pharmacist who must take up his old gunslinging ways to save his adopted town.[^ref-24]

### Torin's Passage (1995)

[[1995 - Torin's Passage|Torin's Passage]] was Lowe's attempt at a game for parents and children to play together. He wrote it in 1994 after seeing Mrs. Doubtfire with his daughter and noticing that the audience laughed "in two different pitches": children at the slapstick, adults at the lines meant for them.[^ref-22][^ref-5] Ken Williams, Lowe said, "felt that there was an audience for a game like King's Quest that wasn't King's Quest."[^ref-5] The game follows Torin and his sidekick Boogle through five worlds nested one inside the other.[^ref-22][^ref-24] Its original score is by Michel Legrand.[^ref-22] Lowe's own Torin credits are as designer and writer, among the credited musicians, and as voice director and voice caster.[^ref-23][^ref-19]

## Design Philosophy

Lowe has said he censored Larry only to keep it a comedy rather than pornography, aiming at the broad audience that bought Sierra's "quest" games.[^ref-12] He has also stressed how primitive Sierra's early tools were. Art was built in a vector drawing program one clicked line at a time, years before Photoshop or scanners reached the studio, and what set King's Quest apart was its system of screen depth bands, which Sierra called "priorities".[^ref-12]

On his website he has published his design documents for Leisure Suit Larry 5, Freddy Pharkas, Leisure Suit Larry 6, Torin's Passage, Leisure Suit Larry 7 and Leisure Suit Larry's Casino as a lesson for newcomers to game design. He notes that he wrote no dialogue in those documents. All of it was written directly in Sierra's in-house "Message Editor" while the game was being programmed.[^ref-9]

## Legacy

Lowe's longest-lived creation is Larry Laffer. The series he started in 1987 continued with Sierra through Love for Sail! (1996) and Leisure Suit Larry's Casino (1998), and the series later went on with Reloaded (2013) and Wet Dreams Don't Dry (2018).[^ref-4][^ref-19] He has also left a record of his own working methods: published design documents,[^ref-9] interviews describing Sierra's early tools and the registration-mark and mouse-macro conventions,[^ref-12] and the 2018 video tour of his Sierra source-code archive, which he then sold on eBay.[^ref-8][^ref-14]
