# Daryl Gates

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 15, 2026</small>

## Overview

Daryl F. Gates (1926–2010) was Chief of the Los Angeles Police Department from 1978 to 1992 who, after leaving the force, lent his name and advice to Sierra On-Line's Police Quest and SWAT games[^ref-2][^ref-6]. Born in Glendale, California on August 30, 1926, Gates joined the LAPD in 1949 and served until his resignation on June 28, 1992 — 43 years of police service by the count in Sierra's own SWAT 3 strategy guide[^ref-2][^ref-18]. His tenure was associated both with SWAT and the DARE program and with intense controversy over police brutality, the Rodney King beating and the 1992 Los Angeles riots[^ref-3][^ref-4].

Months after his resignation, Sierra founder [[Ken Williams]] approached Gates about the next Police Quest game[^ref-19]. According to Sierra's then director of product design [[Josh Mandel]], Williams expected Gates' notoriety to draw attention: "He thought that would get a lot of press and publicity for the game"[^ref-5]. The result was Police Quest: Open Season (1993), which The Digital Antiquarian describes as the first Sierra game "that could be called a full-blown full-motion-video adventure"[^ref-6]. Gates was credited as the game's "Author", but it was designed and written by [[Tammy Dargan]][^ref-5][^ref-21]. VICE's 2018 retrospective on the game ran under the headline "How Sierra and a Disgraced Cop Made the Most Reactionary Game of the 90s"[^ref-5].

## Career

### Early Law Enforcement Career (1949–1992)

After Navy service, Gates was taking pre-law courses at the University of Southern California when he joined the LAPD in 1949; he later finished his degree at USC[^ref-4][^ref-20]. He rose through the ranks "at the arm of" the department's long-time chief, William H. Parker[^ref-9]. The 1965 Watts Riots, which Gates watched from Parker's side, were a formative experience; in his memoir he wrote: "We had no idea how to deal with this. We were constantly ducking bottles, rocks, knives, and Molotov cocktails. It was random chaos. We did not know how to handle guerrilla warfare"[^ref-6].

In the wake of Watts, Gates organized the tactical unit that became the LAPD's SWAT team, and he is often credited with the creation of police SWAT teams[^ref-4][^ref-18][^ref-20]. By July 1975, according to The Digital Antiquarian, there were 500 other SWAT teams in the United States[^ref-6]. In 1983 Gates started the DARE (Drug Abuse Resistance Education) program with the Los Angeles Unified School District[^ref-18][^ref-3].

### Transition to Game Development (1992)

Following his departure from the LAPD in 1992, Gates was approached by Sierra founder Ken Williams[^ref-10][^ref-19]. The earlier Police Quest games had been designed by [[Jim Walls]], a California Highway Patrol officer Williams had met in 1985[^ref-5][^ref-6]. Mandel suggested crime novelist Joseph Wambaugh as an alternative, arguing that a New York Times bestselling author would also draw attention; he recalled Williams replying: "Our players don't read the New York Times"[^ref-5].

Gates visited Sierra's Oakhurst offices in late 1992[^ref-6]. His stated goal was to "maybe say something important about law enforcement… [and to] try and give people a better appreciation for what officers face on the job and encourage a willingness to support them"[^ref-5].

### Sierra Years (1992–1999)

Gates' first Sierra project was Police Quest: Open Season, released in November 1993[^ref-5][^ref-11]. VICE reports that although Gates was "nominally hired as the 'author'", the game "was chiefly written and designed by Tammy Dargan", a former producer of America's Most Wanted, and that "Gates gave her script notes"[^ref-5]. The Digital Antiquarian likewise notes there "had never been any real expectation that Gates would roll up his sleeves and design a computer game"[^ref-6].

The project was unpopular with some Sierra staff. According to The Digital Antiquarian, "a stubborn core of dissenters took to calling the game Rodney King's Quest"[^ref-6]; Mandel told VICE, "We started calling it Rodney King's Quest"[^ref-5]. Mandel also recalled his concerns at the time: "I thought it's one thing to seek controversy, but it's another thing to really divide people. It seemed obvious… that there were going to be existing Police Quest players who would drop out rather than buy a game with Daryl Gates' name on it"[^ref-5].

Gates' name also appeared on the title of Police Quest: SWAT (1995), which was designed, written and directed by Dargan[^ref-10][^ref-22]. He was credited as "Content Consultant" on Police Quest: SWAT 2 (1998)[^ref-23] and served as a consultant on SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle (1999)[^ref-17]; Sierra's SWAT 3 strategy guide lists him among the game's SWAT advisors and says he "signed on as lead advisor for Sierra Online's Police Quest and SWAT games"[^ref-18].

## Notable Works

### Police Quest: Open Season (1993)

[[1993 - Police Quest - Open Season|Police Quest: Open Season]] moved the series from the fictional town of Lytton to Los Angeles and made extensive use of digitized scenes and actors[^ref-6][^ref-10]; IMDb lists it as a 1993 title[^ref-14]. Kotaku's 2010 obituary notes that under Gates the series "dealt less with law enforcement procedure and more with storyline", and that both Open Season and SWAT "were considered disappointments"[^ref-10].

The game's dialogue for black characters drew criticism; VICE quotes lines such as "This be my 'hood". Asked about it by Vibe, Gates said: "I told [Sierra] that these people use the same language that you and I use"[^ref-5].

### Police Quest: SWAT (1995)

[[1995 - Police Quest - SWAT|Police Quest: SWAT]] carried Gates' name in its title[^ref-10]. MobyGames credits Tammy Dargan as its director, designer and writer[^ref-22]. Engadget's 2010 obituary headline called him the game's designer[^ref-13], but the credits list Dargan in that role[^ref-22].

### Later SWAT Games

Gates was credited as "Content Consultant" on [[1998 - Police Quest - SWAT 2|Police Quest: SWAT 2]][^ref-23] and was among the advisors on [[1999 - SWAT 3 - Close Quarters Battle|SWAT 3: Close Quarters Battle]][^ref-17][^ref-18]. In the SWAT 3 guide he described the games' purpose: "I think in the SWAT games we're teaching people that we don't live on violence, that the police don't respond to violence with violence unless it's absolutely essential and absolutely within the law"[^ref-18].

## Legacy

Gates' involvement in games remains tied to his reputation in American policing. The Guardian's obituary notes that after leaving the force he "helped create two video games, Police Quest: Open Season and Swat"[^ref-4]. Assessments of the games are divided: VICE's 2018 retrospective called Open Season "the most reactionary game of the 90s" in its headline[^ref-5], while Gates himself presented the SWAT games as a way to show the challenges of police work[^ref-18]. Gates died of cancer on April 16, 2010, at his home in Dana Point, California, aged 83[^ref-2][^ref-3][^ref-4].
