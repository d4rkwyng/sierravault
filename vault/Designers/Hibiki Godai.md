# Hibiki Godai

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 26, 2026</small>

## Overview

Hibiki Godai (五代響) is the pen name of the Japanese programmer, designer and composer Kohei Ikeda (池田公平).[^ref-11][^ref-5] He is best known as the co-designer, with Satoshi Uesaka, of Game Arts' [[1985 - Thexder|Thexder]] (1985), and he also wrote the opening demo and part of the music for Game Arts' [[1988 - Silpheed|Silpheed]].[^ref-1][^ref-3][^ref-4][^ref-15][^ref-16] He began at ASCII Corporation, where his credits from 1983 and 1984 include Rise Out, Pairs and Star Command. He has headed the developer Technical Arts since its incorporation in 1988; it began in December 1987 as a brand of Game Arts.[^ref-5][^ref-14][^ref-11]

## Career

### ASCII Years (1983–1984)

Godai's route into games began when he bought an NEC PC-6001 to make and sell games. A game he finished and sold in Akihabara caught the eye of someone at ASCII, and he went on to work there; he recalled this at a 2017 retro-PC talk in Akihabara.[^ref-15] His MSX credits at ASCII include programming, direction and music on Pairs (1983), programming on Star Command (1983), a copyright credit on Rise Out (1983), direction on Tiny Rise Out (1983), a programming credit on Trial Ski (1984), and music for Rotors (on which he is also credited with program) and Lord Over (1984).[^ref-5][^ref-19] He also worked on the sound for ASCII's MSX game *Iligks Episode One: Theseus* (1984); at the 2017 talk he said this came about only because they were working together in a cramped shared room and his hands happened to be free (たまたま手が空いていたから). Generation MSX lists him in the game's special thanks.[^ref-15][^ref-5] Rise Out is his own work.[^ref-8] His music for Rise Out and Star Command later appeared, alongside Thexder, on Game Arts' *MICOM GAME MUSIC VOL.1* cassette, which credits him as composer and arranger.[^ref-18]

### Game Arts (1985–1987)

At Game Arts, Godai and Satoshi Uesaka designed Thexder, released for the NEC PC-8801 in 1985; MobyGames credits Godai with its direction and game design.[^ref-4][^ref-19] In a 1985 interview with BEEP magazine, the two explained the idea. "First, with regard to the transforming robot game, we were thinking about commercial appeal as you might have guessed. That's the kind of thing that would appeal to elementary and middle school age kids, we thought," Godai said. "As for the high-speed scrolling, so far there have been no high-speed scrolling games on the PC-88 machines, so we thought it would seem fresh and new."[^ref-3] He added: "the faster the screen scrolls, the larger the maps need to be. If you don't make them large, it'll feel cramped and small to players. For Thexder, we've prepared 480 screens worth of maps."[^ref-3]

He also composed the game's "THEXDER" theme.[^ref-18] Music blogger Unobtainium13 (necromoonyeti) calls Thexder "the first noteworthy soundtrack for the platform I've found making use of the Yamaha YM2203 sound chip."[^ref-7]

Silpheed's PC-88 version (1986) credits Godai as "Sub programer (Music and opening)" and, with Hiromi Ohba, as composer of music tracks 5 to 12; a 2017 AKIBA PC Hotline report likewise describes his Silpheed role as the PC version's demo and music.[^ref-16][^ref-15] Sierra's 1989 DOS release credits him for the original score.[^ref-19] MobyGames also credits him with the demonstration and with music tracks 5 to 12 on the 1988 FM-7 version.[^ref-19] His Silpheed music was released on cassette in 1988.[^ref-10] He was also a sub programmer on the PC-88 version of [[1990 - Zeliard|Zeliard]] (1987).[^ref-19][^ref-20]

Because Game Arts had developed Thexder on a prototype PC-8801mkII SR borrowed from NEC, NEC later asked for help with its PC-88VA, and Godai spent about six months working at NEC on the machine's development.[^ref-15]

### Technical Arts (1987–Present)

Technical Arts began in December 1987 as a separate brand of Game Arts and was incorporated in 1988 as an independent limited company, with Kohei Ikeda as its representative director. Its first job, commissioned by NEC, was a Z80 cross-assembler that was released as a PC Engine SDK.[^ref-14] The Game Developer Research Institute explains the split: "Game Arts began to focus on console games, but Ikeda wanted to continue working on computer software."[^ref-11] Ikeda still heads the company, which is based in Chiyoda, Tokyo.[^ref-14]

The company's own history lists its first game work as the PC-88VA version of Ultima IV (1988, for Pony Canyon), its first in-house game as *SL Simulator: Jōki Kikanshi* (2000), released by Smarts Japan, and its first smartphone game as *RISEOUT Over The Centuries* for iOS (2011), followed by a military quiz game (2017) and the VR tennis game *CYBER TENNIS* (2018 onward).[^ref-14] MobyGames also credits him with music for *Game Basic for SegaSaturn* (1998), under the Technical Arts name.[^ref-19]

Godai had no role on the development team of the 2009 remake [[2009 - Thexder Neo|Thexder Neo]], which was developed by Zereo, published by Square Enix and directed by Masayuki Shimada. He and Uesaka are credited there as "Original Thexder Staff" and in the remake team's Special Thanks, and his "Thexder" theme is used as the opening music.[^ref-17][^ref-2] He is also listed in the "Special Thanks" of Sierra's [[1995 - Thexder 95|Thexder]] for Windows (1995).[^ref-19]

## Notable Works

### Thexder (1985)

Thexder, co-designed with Satoshi Uesaka, is a side-scrolling action game starring a robot that transforms into a jet.[^ref-3][^ref-4] Godai said development took about two months, and that it took "480 screens worth of maps" to make the fast scrolling feel roomy.[^ref-3] The back of the game's box carried a picture of its two designers.[^ref-4]

### Silpheed (1986)

On Silpheed, Godai programmed the opening demo and composed part of the music, tracks 5 to 12, with Hiromi Ohba.[^ref-16][^ref-15]

## Design Philosophy

In the 1985 BEEP interview, Godai described a one-person approach: "I think it differs from person to person, but in my case, I like to do everything myself, from start to finish."[^ref-3] He also described his working hours: "when I'm into it, I'm fine working three days straight without sleep. Working for three days, then sleeping 20 hours, that kind of schedule."[^ref-3]
