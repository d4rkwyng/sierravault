# Kevin Ryan

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 9, 2026</small>

## Overview

Kevin Ryan is a programmer and game designer who was one of the owner-partners of Dynamix, and the designer and programmer of The Incredible Machine.[^ref-1][^ref-18] The Dynamix Wiki gives his birth year as 1961; it also notes that he grew up in Fresno, California, and now lives in Shaver Lake, California.[^ref-2] He graduated from the University of Oregon with a BS in Computer and Information Science in 1983.[^ref-11] Ryan has been modest about his best-known work: "I've created somewhere between 20-30 games — apparently my most well known design is The Incredible Machine. I'm always surprised when I hear that people have heard of it or played it."[^ref-1]

Ryan describes himself as having made games "for over 4 decades", starting with BASIC games on an 8 KB computer before moving to the Apple II, Commodore 64 and Amiga.[^ref-9] Fellow Dynamix co-founder Damon Slye said of him: "He is a brilliant programmer and game designer. He's another one of those rare people who is really nice, great to work with, and super talented."[^ref-4]

## Career

### Early Career (1976–1984)

Ryan first encountered computers on a 1976 junior-high field trip to Berkeley, where a university lab let visitors play "Guess The Animal" on a mainframe. In high school he wrote games in BASIC on a Wang computer with 8K of memory.[^ref-13] One of the earlier developers for the Apple II, he saw his first games, Zoo Master and Black Belt, published by Earthware Computer Services.[^ref-2][^ref-11] MobyGames dates Zoo Master to 1983 and Black Belt to 1984.[^ref-12]

### Dynamix (1984–1991)

In 1984 Ryan received an unexpected call from Damon Slye, who had heard of him through Jeff Tunnell's Eugene computer store, and he joined Dynamix.[^ref-13] His first job there was porting Sword of Kadash to the Commodore 64. Two or three months later he was asked to become a partner, alongside Jeff Tunnell, Damon Slye and Richard Hicks.[^ref-13][^ref-2] A photograph of this early incarnation of Dynamix shows Ryan, Tunnell, Slye and Hicks together.[^ref-15]

Electronic Arts wanted Dynamix to develop for the Amiga, which led to Arcticfox (1986).[^ref-13] Slye said Ryan "handled all the graphics, sounds, and Amiga specific interfaces".[^ref-4] Ryan's own biography calls Arcticfox the first original game for the Amiga.[^ref-11] Slye was more cautious: "I believe that Arcticfox was the first original game released on the Amiga... It may have been that it was the first original that EA released."[^ref-4] *Computer Gaming World*'s Roy Wagner called it "the first original new [EA game] that uses the distinctive features of the Amiga".[^ref-17]

Ryan went on to create Skyfox II: The Cygnus Conflict for the Commodore 64 (1987), and to design and program F-14 Tomcat for the Commodore 64 (1989).[^ref-12] The F-14 Tomcat player's guide credits Ryan and Nick Skrepetos as programmers and says that Ryan "designed TOMCAT while a close friend attended A.O.C.S."[^ref-20] He was also credited with design, programming and the screenplay for David Wolf: Secret Agent (1989), and he worked on the Dynamix Game Development System behind Rise of the Dragon and Heart of China.[^ref-12] For Heart of China he also programmed the tank sequence.[^ref-12] Ryan quit Dynamix in November 1991, after design work on a game called Vivarium and a "3D toys" project that did not go ahead.[^ref-13][^ref-2]

### Jeff Tunnell Productions and Sierra (1992–2001)

About three weeks after Ryan left Dynamix, Jeff Tunnell called and offered him a job at Jeff Tunnell Productions (JTP), a small "satellite" Tunnell was forming, with the suggestion of "maybe a Rube Goldberg game".[^ref-13][^ref-2] The Incredible Machine was designed and coded by Ryan, produced by Tunnell at JTP, and published by Dynamix.[^ref-16] Jimmy Maher writes that the years Ryan spent building vehicle simulators at Dynamix gave him much of the expertise and code he needed for the game's physics, and that he had the basic engine working within a handful of months.[^ref-14] Ryan recalls spending January to March writing the design document, then coding from March 17 until mid-September, working mostly from his home basement. He puts the budget at $37,500.[^ref-13] Wikipedia gives US$36,000 and a nine-month development.[^ref-16]

Ryan was designer or co-designer on The Even More! Incredible Machine (1993) and The Incredible Machine 2 (1994). On the two Toons titles his credit was as one of the "Puzzlomaniacs".[^ref-12] He was co-designer and game-logic programmer of 3-D Ultra Minigolf (1997), and designer of 3-D Ultra MiniGolf Deluxe (1998).[^ref-12][^ref-2] He began work on Return of the Incredible Machine: Contraptions, but his son was born with liver failure in 1999 and he spent most of a year at a San Francisco hospital. In his words, "ultimately I wasn't all that involved."[^ref-13] MobyGames credits him with contraption design and parts programming on Contraptions (2000), and with additional design and parts programming on Even More Contraptions (2001).[^ref-12]

### Top Meadow and later work (1998–present)

Ryan and Brian Supple founded Top Meadow in 1998, and Ryan became its lead coder; his son's illness kept the studio's work on and off for years.[^ref-1] After attending the 2001 Computer Game Developers Conference he connected with GarageGames, a company of former Dynamix staff, and he contributed to Marble Blast and Chain Reaction (2002) and to Blockland.[^ref-2][^ref-12] Top Meadow's Puzzle Poker began as a one-day prototype in November 2005, built in GarageGames' Torque Game Builder, and shipped in early May 2006.[^ref-1] Asked whether it had been hard to design without examples to follow, Ryan said: "It wasn't really that hard. I've been doing this for over 20 years now and I've gotten used to doing this stuff."[^ref-1]

Top Meadow's Minigolf Mania, built on the Torque Game Engine and sold through GarageGames, was reviewed by *Macworld* in April 2007.[^ref-22][^ref-8] It placed second in GameTunnel's Game of the Year sports award.[^ref-1]

Ryan then worked on Contraption Maker with Spotkin, the studio of Jeff Tunnell, his son Jonathon and Keith Johnston, where he "was responsible for getting the physics right".[^ref-18] The game was released on Steam on July 7, 2014, and is now listed with Ryan as developer and Top Meadow as publisher.[^ref-19] Wikipedia describes it as the spiritual successor to The Incredible Machine.[^ref-16] In 2017 Ryan said that Tunnell was "kinda done with games" and that he had taken the game over.[^ref-13] He has also written about Contraption Maker's design for Game Developer (formerly Gamasutra), including a 2016 post on its ropes.[^ref-21]

## Design Approach

Ryan has said The Incredible Machine's sandbox mode was not planned from the start. He built a puzzle editor for the team and kept thinking "this is really fun – just to let people do stuff", so it was improved for players.[^ref-13] He credits that editor for the game's lasting appeal: "It was so open-ended and with its interactions, you could build anything with it."[^ref-13] Speaking of Tunnell's original idea, he wrote that "Jeff presented the concept to me, but he didn't do the design except for feedback as dev went along."[^ref-14]

On the technical side, Ryan wrote The Incredible Machine's physics in integer math, because floating point was too slow at the time.[^ref-13] For Contraption Maker he converted the Chipmunk physics engine from floating point to integer, so that contraptions would run identically on every platform.[^ref-13][^ref-18] He explained the problem in a 2016 blog post: "Chaos theory is where very small differences in the starting conditions of a dynamic system can result in completely different final results."[^ref-18] The Incredible Machine's engine also uses no random number generator, so any machine always behaves the same way.[^ref-16]

His advice to new developers, given in 2007: "For those that haven't finished a game yet. Finish it. Ship it. You may make some money. And for sure you will learn a lot."[^ref-1]
