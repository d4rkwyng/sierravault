# Ken Allen

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 16, 2026</small>

## Overview

Kenneth Allen is an American video game composer, sound designer and producer. He worked at Sierra On-Line from April 1989 to October 1991.[^ref-5][^ref-7] According to the Adventure Game Fan Fair, he became a musician at age 10, learning the French horn because his school was "all out of trumpets".[^ref-1] By his own account he went to college to become a music teacher. After leaving Sierra he took film-scoring classes at UCLA, including an all-day session taught by Jerry Goldsmith.[^ref-5] He entered the games industry by answering a classified ad in the Fresno Bee for a "Musician needed for video games" at Sierra.[^ref-5]

Allen composed for games in series including King's Quest, Space Quest, Star Trek and Descent.[^ref-1][^ref-18] On his about.me page he says he wrote "more than 300 pieces for over two dozen games", and that by the mid-1990s he had composed "over 900 minutes of music for games, or roughly 15 hours of scoring."[^ref-2] After leaving Sierra in October 1991 he worked at Tsunami Media. There he composed for games such as Ringworld: Revenge of the Patriarch and Blue Force, did level design on Protostar, and was lead designer and producer on Return to Ringworld.[^ref-5][^ref-7][^ref-15]

He later worked mainly as a producer, at companies including Interplay Productions, Westwood Studios, Midway Games, Atari, THQ and Trion Worlds. His titles include Nox, RollerCoaster Tycoon 3 and the MMO Rift.[^ref-5][^ref-7][^ref-18] He still composed for Parallax Software's Descent (1994).[^ref-18][^ref-10] In January 2013, the month he launched an album Kickstarter, he agreed to be the main composer for SpaceVenture, the spiritual successor to Space Quest by his former collaborators Mark Crowe and Scott Murphy. The game was released in 2022.[^ref-3][^ref-10][^ref-20]

## Career

### Early Career

Allen's path to video game composition was circuitous. He went to college to become a music teacher.[^ref-5] After graduating, he landed a teaching job at a private school where he was required to teach both music and mathematics.[^ref-5] However, Allen quickly discovered he "hated teaching math and there was not enough call for a full time music teacher," leading him to leave after just one semester.[^ref-5] He then took a job at the post office sorting mail while figuring out his next career move, during which time he wrote music for local theater and churches and taught himself to write computer games in BASIC and some assembly language.[^ref-5]

At the dawn of the MIDI era he built a home MIDI studio, which let him produce music as well as compose it.[^ref-5] When he saw Sierra's classified ad in 1989, he initially thought "there was no way this was a real full time job because it seemed too good to be true."[^ref-5] Despite his skepticism, Allen prepared his first professional resume and recorded a tape of his music, even composing a brand new piece specifically to demonstrate his ability to work quickly with high quality.[^ref-5]

### Sierra Years (1989–1991)

Allen's interview process at Sierra On-Line involved meeting with key figures including Stuart Goldstein (a systems programmer devoted to music technology), Mark Seibert (who would become head of the music department), Rick Cavin (head of operations), and founder Ken Williams.[^ref-5] In salary negotiations he made what he later described as a blundered pitch. He offered to take a significant pay cut to prove himself, on the understanding that his wage would be raised after a 90-day probationary period. The agreement was never recorded, and pay became an ongoing issue.[^ref-5]

Beginning his career at Sierra on April 7, 1989, Allen was able to tell friends and family that he "made a living sitting around making funny noises."[^ref-5] His work encompassed not only composition but also sound effects creation and the technical challenge of adapting music for various sound cards of varying quality.[^ref-7] As Allen explained, "There were really only two types of sound cards when I started at Sierra, those that used FM synthesis (the kind used in the Yamaha DX7) and those that used Addative synthesis from Roland." Each song had to be adapted for five different playback devices.[^ref-5]

One of Allen's significant technical contributions was creating sound banks for various sound cards, leveraging his background with synthesizer technology.[^ref-5] He had built a synthesizer from a kit in 1978, a smaller version of the Moog depicted on the cover of *Switched-On Bach*. That gave him the expertise to create "high quality instruments and sound effects using the synth technology of the day".[^ref-5] He believes Sierra was the first to use "redbook audio" in a CD-ROM game, with the CD-ROM version of Mixed-Up Mother Goose, for which he produced studio recordings of his arrangements.[^ref-5][^ref-2]

Allen also composed the Sierra logo theme that played at the start of each Sierra game.[^ref-5][^ref-15] He thinks he worked on "about a dozen games in all for Sierra".[^ref-5] They include The Colonel's Bequest, Fire Hawk: Thexder - The Second Contact, King's Quest V, Oil's Well, Space Quest I VGA, Space Quest IV, Jones in the Fast Lane, Castle of Dr. Brain and Conquests of the Longbow.[^ref-18][^ref-15]

Allen left Sierra in October 1991 over the unresolved salary agreement.[^ref-5][^ref-7] After he was told again that there was no money in the budget, he found he could raise his income by more than 50% as an independent contractor. He resigned, working out his notice "so I could finish The Island of Doctor Brain".[^ref-5]

### Tsunami Media & Freelance Period (1991–1994)

After leaving Sierra, Allen worked as a freelance composer for companies including Accolade, Disney Interactive, Futurescape, Tachyon and Interplay before joining Tsunami Media.[^ref-5] Tsunami was started by several ex-Sierra employees under former Sierra CFO Ed Heinbockle, who had helped take Sierra public. Allen accepted Tsunami's offer on the condition that he could also become a game designer, and the company agreed.[^ref-5]

At Tsunami, Allen earned the nickname "Tsunami Renaissance Man" for his diverse contributions including music, sound design, directing voice talent, designing, writing, writing user guides, and even IT work.[^ref-5] His initial work included composing for Ringworld: Revenge of the Patriarch (1992), based on Larry Niven's Known Space novels, as well as Blue Force (1993), the police procedural adventure designed by former Police Quest creator Jim Walls.[^ref-7][^ref-15]

Allen also did level design on Protostar: War on the Frontier (1993), a game originally intended as a sequel to Star Control, and composed its music.[^ref-5][^ref-18] His biggest role at Tsunami was as lead designer and producer of Return to Ringworld (1994), the sequel to the original Ringworld. MobyGames credits him as its designer, director, story writer and composer.[^ref-5][^ref-18]

During this period, Allen also worked on various freelance projects outside Tsunami. They include Les Manley in: Lost in L.A. (1992), Road & Track Presents Grand Prix Unlimited (1992), Wacky Funsters! The Geekwad's Guide to Gaming (1992), WarpSpeed for SNES and Genesis (1992–1993), and Man Enough (1994).[^ref-7][^ref-18]

A lesser-known fact from this period: Allen actually composed the complete score and sound effects for Space Quest V for Mark Crowe at Dynamix. As Allen heard it, Ken Williams learned of his involvement on returning from a vacation at the Barcelona Olympics, and ordered the music replaced with compositions by Sierra employee Chris Stevens. Allen was paid in full for his work. His SQ5 score was never used, though he believes most of his sound effects survived.[^ref-5]

### Interplay & Parallax Era (1994–1998)

Allen composed music for Parallax Software's Descent, collaborating on its audio with Brian Luzietti, Larry Peacock, Leslie Spitzer, Jim Torres, and Tim Wiles.[^ref-7][^ref-18] He composed for Interplay's Star Trek: Starfleet Academy Starship Bridge Simulator (SNES and 32X, 1995) and for Clay Fighter 2: Judgement Clay (SNES, 1994).[^ref-18]

When it became clear that Tsunami's days were numbered, he joined Interplay as an external producer.[^ref-5] MobyGames credits him as producer of the Star Trek: Judgment Rites CD-ROM edition, in which he also voiced Capt. Luke Rayner. He was supervising producer of Frankenstein: Through the Eyes of the Monster (1995) and producer of Mummy: Tomb of the Pharaoh (1996).[^ref-18][^ref-15] He later became an internal producer heading development of Star Trek: Secret of Vulcan Fury, which was never released.[^ref-5]

### Later Career (1998–Present)

According to VGMPF, Allen worked as a producer for Realtime Associates from the start of 1998. The same timeline also says he joined Electronic Arts in February 1998, so the exact dates are unclear.[^ref-7] He was a producer on Westwood Studios' Nox (2000); by his account, this was around the time EA bought the studio from Virgin Interactive.[^ref-5][^ref-18]

According to VGMPF, Allen joined Midway Games as a senior producer in May 2000. He moved to Atari as senior game producer in February 2002 and later became an executive producer there.[^ref-7] MobyGames credits him as senior producer on RollerCoaster Tycoon 3 (2004).[^ref-18] He joined THQ in October 2006; MobyGames credits him as senior producer on Destroy All Humans! Big Willy Unleashed (2008).[^ref-7][^ref-18][^ref-14] In June 2008, he left THQ for Trion Worlds, where he was one of the producers on the MMO Rift (2011).[^ref-5][^ref-7][^ref-18]

Over the following decade he worked for various studios, primarily as a producer, including IGT, Shuffle Master, Petroglyph Games and Konami.[^ref-7]

In 2013, Allen launched a Kickstarter campaign for "Under the Half Dome", an album of new arrangements of his game music. It passed its $10,000 goal, raising $21,090 from 575 backers.[^ref-9][^ref-15] The album was never completed. In January 2016, about three years on, with roughly a third of it scored, Allen shelved the project. Citing family elder-care, his SpaceVenture work and other personal matters, he offered refunds to all backers starting that February.[^ref-17]

In January 2013 Allen agreed to be the main composer for SpaceVenture.[^ref-10] He told The Grig Post at the time that he "hadn't composed music in 15 years".[^ref-10] SpaceVenture was released in September 2022, with Allen credited for composition and audio production.[^ref-20]

## Notable Works

### Space Quest IV: Roger Wilco and the Time Rippers (1991)

Allen calls the Space Quest IV opening his favorite piece from his Sierra years.[^ref-5] It came together under pressure. While music director Mark Seibert was on vacation, Mark Crowe and Scott Murphy came to Allen in a panic: they needed music for the opening sequence for a board meeting only days away.[^ref-5]

Over a single weekend, Allen composed six and a half minutes of orchestral music plus sound design and custom patches. In his words, doing so "in just a couple days is just unheard of, probably even today."[^ref-10] Mark Crowe wanted the music to be "John Williams BIG" with very specific sound effects, while Scott Murphy was more of a "just knock our socks off, Ken," kinda guy.[^ref-10] When the opening was played back, Crowe's "normal smiling grin turned into a fully teethed happy face", and the SQ4 team in the adjacent cubicles gathered to watch.[^ref-10] After the product review meeting Allen was officially assigned to compose the game's music, and he remembers thinking, "looks like I passed the audition."[^ref-10] On his about.me page Allen recalls that PC Magazine's review of the game was "the very first to comment on a game's music", calling the opening theme "reminiscent of John Williams, err Gustov Holtz."[^ref-2] MobyGames credits him with "Music / Sound Programming" on the game.[^ref-18][^ref-22]

### King's Quest V: Absence Makes the Heart Go Yonder (1990)

Allen worked on the music and sound of King's Quest V alongside Mark Seibert.[^ref-7] MobyGames credits him as a composer and for sound effects on the DOS version, and lists him among the uncredited composers of the NES port.[^ref-18]

### Space Quest I VGA Remake (1991)

Allen is credited as composer of the 1991 VGA remake of Space Quest I.[^ref-18][^ref-7]

### Descent (1994)

Working with Parallax Software, Allen composed music for Descent, collaborating on the audio with Brian Luzietti, Larry Peacock, Leslie Spitzer, Jim Torres, and Tim Wiles.[^ref-7][^ref-18] In 2013 he said he planned to include one tune from Descent on his "Under the Half Dome" album, which was later shelved.[^ref-10][^ref-17]

### Ringworld Series (1992–1994)

At Tsunami Media, Allen composed the score for Ringworld: Revenge of the Patriarch (1992). He was designer, director, story writer and composer on Return to Ringworld (1994).[^ref-18][^ref-21][^ref-5] Both games are based on Larry Niven's science fiction novels.[^ref-7]

## Design Philosophy

Allen's approach to game audio was rooted in the hardware limits of early PC gaming. He developed expertise in creating "high quality instruments and sound effects using the synth technology of the day", drawing on his experience building synthesizers from kits in the 1970s.[^ref-5] That included building custom sound banks for the sound cards Sierra supported, and later a General MIDI sound bank for the non-General MIDI cards.[^ref-5]

His favorite composer was Jerry Goldsmith, who scored Planet of the Apes, four Star Trek films, Hoosiers, Rudy, and Patton—the latter being the film score that inspired Allen to become a composer.[^ref-5] After leaving Sierra, Allen attended an all-day session at UCLA taught by Goldsmith, who signed his copy of the Rudy soundtrack album.[^ref-5]

He taught himself to write in any style required for games, including "big band, rockabilly, technorock, classic rock, bluegrass, cartoon music, practically everything."[^ref-5] His favorite style is composing for orchestra.[^ref-5]

## Legacy

Allen composed the Sierra logo theme and scored games in the King's Quest and Space Quest series.[^ref-5][^ref-18] In 2012 he estimated his MobyGames list at "about 50 games", with "about 10 more" uncredited games he had worked on.[^ref-5] MobyGames now credits him on 61 games.[^ref-19][^ref-4] Fan interest in his Sierra music funded his 2013 "Under the Half Dome" Kickstarter, though the album was shelved in 2016.[^ref-9][^ref-17]

Of hearing that Mark Crowe and Scott Murphy were reuniting to make SpaceVenture, he said: "All of the creative juices began to flow again. It's like they were global warming and I was Glacier National Park."[^ref-10]
