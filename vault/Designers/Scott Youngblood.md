# Scott Youngblood

<small style="color: gray">Last updated: October 15, 2026</small>

## Overview

Scott Youngblood is a video game designer best known as the lead designer of Dynamix's Starsiege: Tribes (1998).[^ref-17][^ref-21] He came to the Tribes team from Dynamix's Front Page Sports line, having just finished Front Page Sports: Ski Racing, and he oversaw the team's adoption of the "skiing" movement bug that became the game's signature.[^ref-21][^ref-5] He later worked at Electronic Arts, Pipeworks Software, Sony Computer Entertainment America and Red 5 Studios, where he was lead designer of Firefall.[^ref-9][^ref-5]

## Career

### Dynamix (1990–1999)

By his own account, Youngblood worked at Dynamix from 1990 to 1999.[^ref-9] His earliest credits, from 1992, are in testing: Q.A. programming on Front Page Sports: Football and quality assurance on Aces of the Pacific and the Amiga version of The Adventures of Willy Beamish.[^ref-17] He then moved into design and production on the Front Page Sports line, with additional design and direction on Football Pro (1993), associate producer and additional design on Baseball '94 and Football Pro '96, and direction and design on Football Pro '97.[^ref-17] He was the credited designer of Front Page Sports: Ski Racing (1997).[^ref-17]

He joined the Tribes team after completing Ski Racing, while the project was still codenamed "Fear," and led the game's first public demo.[^ref-21] He and lead software engineer Mark Frohnmayer had been playing competitive Quake together, and Youngblood has said: "I was trying to do something more than the other shooters that were out there."[^ref-21] Watching Dennis "Thresh" Fong win the 1997 Red Annihilation Quake tournament with rocket jumps shaped the game's jetpacks: "To be honest, that's where the jetpacks in Tribes came from. Here's a player who can do something so far outside the norms that changes the way he plays, and everyone has to change how they play too. That's cool."[^ref-5][^ref-21]

The "skiing" mechanic was found in-house. About halfway through development, software engineer Dave Moore, who was working on collisions and character movement, showed Youngblood a bug in which pressing jump cancelled the collision with the landscape.[^ref-21] "I looked at him and was like, 'Nope, don't fix it. This is now a feature,'" Youngblood recalls; he then designed the remaining maps with skiing in mind.[^ref-21] He has also said: "We were the guys that invented the concept of 'capture and hold,' which basically turned into all the point-defense games."[^ref-21] He has said the game could support up to 128 players under ideal conditions.[^ref-21]

The Escapist reported that his plans for Tribes included a meta-game of resource gathering and territory grabbing, most of which was cut to ship the game.[^ref-15] He left Dynamix in 1999.[^ref-9] Tribes 2 (2001) credits him with Special Thanks.[^ref-17]

### Electronic Arts and Pipeworks (1999–2001)

At Electronic Arts he worked on content for the alternate-reality game Majestic, and he is credited on Majestic: Special Edition (2001) as part of the Majestic Content Team.[^ref-13][^ref-17] His self-reported history then lists a year at Pipeworks Software (2000–2001) on Raven-X, a project that was cancelled.[^ref-9]

### Sony Computer Entertainment America (2001–2007)

Youngblood joined Sony Computer Entertainment America's Bend studio in 2001, near the end of Syphon Filter 3's development.[^ref-9] He was lead designer on Syphon Filter: The Omega Strain (2004) and lead multiplayer designer on Syphon Filter: Dark Mirror (2006).[^ref-17][^ref-6] Syphon Filter: Logan's Shadow (2007) credits him with Special Thanks.[^ref-17]

### Red 5 Studios (2007–2014)

In April 2007, Red 5 Studios announced that Youngblood had accepted a position at the studio.[^ref-6][^ref-4][^ref-1] He had been recruited through Red 5's "Golden Ticket" campaign, and he described receiving it in a 2007 interview: "I'll just describe what it was like getting one of these 'golden tickets,' as they're referred to around here. This overnight package shows up to my office at Sony--I had no idea what it is."[^ref-13][^ref-19]

Mark Kern, Red 5's president and CEO, said at the time: "Scott is exactly the caliber of team member we're looking for at Red 5. We founded this company on passion and vision, two qualities that Scott has embodied throughout his career as a top-tier game designer."[^ref-6] Youngblood said: "When Mark asked me to join Red 5, I was blown away by the vast and vibrant world they're creating and by the company they're building. It's a combination I couldn't refuse."[^ref-6]

At Red 5, Youngblood was lead designer of Firefall, an online shooter released in 2014.[^ref-5] He recalled that when he started, Kern asked him whether he wanted to buy the Tribes licence; he wanted Firefall "to be its own game, not a Tribes clone."[^ref-5] After Red 5 removed the game's arena mode, he said: "We were spread too thin. We were trying to do too much."[^ref-5] His self-reported history has him at Red 5 until November 2014.[^ref-9]

## Notable Works

### Starsiege: Tribes (1998)

Youngblood is credited with lead design on Starsiege: Tribes.[^ref-17] A team of 15 to 20 people took about two and a half years to make the game, which shipped as a multiplayer-only shooter.[^ref-21] Youngblood said Dynamix saw it as a proof of concept: "We didn't even put copy protection on it, on purpose, so people would go out and pirate the crap out of it, which they did."[^ref-21] The Academy of Interactive Arts & Sciences named it Online Action/Strategy Game of the Year, and PC Gamer US gave it a "Special Achievement in Innovation" award.[^ref-21]

### Front Page Sports: Ski Racing (1997)

Ski Racing was Youngblood's first credit as designer. He is also credited with its video script and game documentation.[^ref-17] The Ringer notes that he came to Tribes "just after completing an actual skiing game."[^ref-21]

### Firefall (2014)

The Escapist presented Firefall as the game in which Youngblood brought back the resource-gathering ideas cut from Tribes.[^ref-15] It had jetpacks, but not Tribes-style skiing. Youngblood explained that "the speed of momentum for Firefall was not the same as the speed of momentum for Tribes," and that in an MMO with widely varying pings "the movement of the players becomes something you have to be in more control of."[^ref-5]

## Design Philosophy

Youngblood has described a feedback-driven approach: "Try and ask, try and ask. In my opinion this is the best way."[^ref-5] Asked about his design philosophy at Red 5 in 2007, he answered: "Would we like to play it? We're making a game that we want to play. We're building it for ourselves, but it's going to be pretty cool that a bunch of other people will be able to come in and share this world with us."[^ref-7]

On Tribes, he said: "Even on Tribes we had all sorts of things I never intended players to do but turned out to be awesome."[^ref-5] He has criticized later jetpack shooters for making jumps behave differently by class or mid-air input, because players then lose "predictable flight and combat that derives around being able to predict where the players are going to land."[^ref-21]

## Legacy

Starsiege: Tribes sold modestly (fewer than 99,000 copies, by The Ringer's account), but it gave rise to a dedicated competitive community of clans and tournament ladders.[^ref-21] Youngblood himself said of the game: "For people who played the game and understood what it was and how much influence it had on the rest of games that followed it, it was absolutely a success."[^ref-21] In December 2024 he joined a fan's stream celebrating Tribes' 26th birthday for an interview about the game's origins.[^ref-8]
