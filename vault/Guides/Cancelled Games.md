# Sierra's Cancelled Games: The Ones That Got Away

Sierra Entertainment built long-running series such as King's Quest, Space Quest and Leisure Suit Larry, but for every game that shipped, others died on the vine. This guide documents Sierra's most significant cancelled projects—ambitious games that never saw release, leaving fans to wonder what might have been.

## Overview

Sierra's cancelled games represent a fascinating cross-section of gaming history. These weren't just abandoned prototypes; many were substantial projects with famous designers, completed voice recordings, and playable builds. Their cancellations reveal the corporate dysfunction, industry shifts, and creative conflicts that marked Sierra's decline in the late 1990s and early 2000s.[^ref-1]

The pattern is striking: multiple beloved franchises—King's Quest, Space Quest, Leisure Suit Larry—all had planned sequels cancelled during the tumultuous period between 1997 and 2013. The corporate upheaval following CUC International's acquisition of Sierra, culminating in "Chainsaw Monday" (February 22, 1999), decimated Sierra's development teams.[^ref-2][^ref-1]

> [!note] A Timeline of Loss
> - **1989:** Manhunter 3: London (Evryware's partnership with Sierra ends)[^ref-28]
> - **1997-1999:** Space Quest VII, Leisure Suit Larry 8
> - **1998-2001:** Middle-earth Online (Sierra version)[^ref-18]
> - **1999:** Babylon 5: Into the Fire[^ref-27]
> - **c. 1999-2002:** King's Quest IX (Twins of Change)[^ref-6]
> - **2004:** SWAT: Urban Justice (discontinued April 2004)[^ref-30]
> - **2013:** King's Quest IX (Telltale), Precinct, Red Baron Remake

## Major Cancelled Games

### Space Quest VII: Return to Roman Numerals

Space Quest VII went through multiple development iterations between 1997 and 2002, including both an internal Sierra version and a later Escape Factory console reboot.[^ref-3]

**What Happened:** Development began in early 1997 under series co-creator [[Scott Murphy]] and co-designer [[Leslie Balfour]].[^ref-3] The project was announced for late 1998 but placed on indefinite hold by December 1997. By mid-February 1998, rumours were spreading that Murphy had been fired. A restart was proposed in early 1999 but quickly stalled.[^ref-4] The project died when Sierra closed its Oakhurst, California facility on "Chainsaw Monday," firing two-thirds of the employees there.[^ref-3] SpaceQuest.net says Murphy's contract "had expired and was not renewed,"[^ref-3] but Murphy himself said in 2006: "I didn't actually lose my job on Chainsaw Monday. I was dumped a month and a half previous to that."[^ref-37]

**Why It Was Cancelled:** Corporate pressure to add multiplayer functionality fundamentally compromised the design. Murphy later admitted: "We were being pressured to make Space Quest 7 a multiplayer game, which, by its nature, it just never should have been. Therefore, the design had to be something that I didn't want it to be and I thought it sucked for the most part."[^ref-26]

**What Would Have Been:** Concept art of a slime monster on the streets of Polysorbate LX, the polluted planet from Space Quest 6, led fans to speculate that Roger would return there, but this was never confirmed.[^ref-3] Scott Murphy later revealed that the 1997 Sierra design's "big twist" was that Sludge Vohaul was Roger Wilco's brother—a failed clone. A later Escape Factory console reboot was cancelled by Sierra on December 19, 2002.[^ref-4]

**What Survives:** A promotional trailer included with The Space Quest Collection shows Roger "dying" while self-referentially joking about the game's likely cancellation. The trailer "had nothing to do with what Space Quest 7 was supposed to be" according to developers—it was "merely eye candy for management."[^ref-4]

### King's Quest IX: The Long Wait

"King's Quest IX" is an umbrella term for **several failed attempts** to continue Sierra's flagship adventure game series between 1998 and 2013—three official attempts (Twins of Change, Silicon Knights and Telltale), after Roberta Williams was "never able get Sierra greenlight development on a new game," plus a fan game that was eventually released under another name.[^ref-2] The King's Quest Omnipedia stresses that "King's Quest IX is not The Silver Lining."[^ref-2]

| Years | Project | Developer | Status |
|-------|---------|-----------|--------|
| 1998–1999 | Roberta's Concept | Sierra On-Line | Never greenlit |
| c. 1999–2002 | Twins of Change | Sierra Seattle | Studio closed |
| 2000–2011 | The Silver Lining (fan game) | Phoenix Online (fan) | Episodes 1–4 released under new name |
| c. 2007 | Silicon Knights | Silicon Knights | Prototype; code probably destroyed under court order |
| 2011–2013 | Telltale Games | Telltale Games | Cancelled |

**Twins of Change:** King's Quest: Twins of Change was directed by [[Mark Seibert]] and written by Cindy Vanous at Sierra Studios Seattle. It featured Alexander and Rosella transformed by wild magic, able to change into animal forms, in a Zelda-inspired action-adventure. Seibert recalled that the concept "got 2 green lights" but "never got fully funded." The studio "finally died under Vivendi Games" before completion.[^ref-6]

**The Strangest Fate:** Silicon Knights' c. 2007 King's Quest prototype only came to light during its lawsuit with Epic Games over the Unreal Engine. The judge ordered Silicon Knights to destroy code from games that had used Unreal Engine 3, "so if there was anything related to the prototype its probably been destroyed."[^ref-2]

**What Survives:** [[2010 - The Silver Lining|The Silver Lining]] began as "King's Quest IX: Every Cloak Has A Silver Lining" (production began in October 2000) before cease-and-desist letters from Vivendi and later Activision forced it to continue under a new name. Four of five planned episodes were released between July 2010 and November 2011.[^ref-7][^ref-31]

The series eventually continued with [[2015 - King's Quest|The Odd Gentlemen's 2015 reboot]], which developers explicitly stated was **not** King's Quest IX.[^ref-2]

### Leisure Suit Larry 8: Lust in Space

Al Lowe's planned sequel would have taken Larry Laffer into space as the series' first fully 3D entry.[^ref-8]

**What Happened:** Development began in 1998 under series creator [[Al Lowe]], who had designed every mainline Larry game. Lowe says that "at the end of 1998, while Sierra and I were negotiating a contract for Leisure Suit Larry 8: Lust in Space, Sierra cancelled production."[^ref-29][^ref-8]

**Why It Was Cancelled:** Corporate turmoil following Sierra's acquisition by CUC International. Lowe described the situation: "Sierra management was in turmoil after Ken and Roberta got bought out by CUC. The new management tried to get me to design my next game without a contract, saying 'we'll work it out later.'"[^ref-9]

Lowe has written that he "refused to flesh out the design until we reached an agreement on terms. They didn't want to negotiate, so I left." He gives no exact date for his departure.[^ref-32]

**What Survives:** A few test renders and a 21-second 3D test animation by Jason Zayas, which Lowe later published, survive from the development period.[^ref-8][^ref-29] An easter egg in [[1996 - Leisure Suit Larry 7 - Love for Sail|Love for Sail!]] referenced the planned sequel.[^ref-8]

**The Aftermath:** The Larry series would not continue until 2004's [[2004 - Leisure Suit Larry - Magna Cum Laude|Magna Cum Laude]]—the first Larry game without Al Lowe's involvement, featuring a new protagonist (Larry Lovage) and a radically different tone.[^ref-10]

### Babylon 5: Into the Fire

A space combat game that was **65% complete** when it was cancelled, with live-action sequences filmed with four of the TV series' leads.[^ref-11][^ref-33]

**What Happened:** Developed by Yosemite Entertainment, this ambitious space combat simulator featured several of the TV series' main actors—Bruce Boxleitner, Jerry Doyle, Mira Furlan and Tracy Scoggins—reprising their roles in filmed sequences.[^ref-11][^ref-33] Sierra announced its partnership with Warner Bros. in October 1997; the release slipped to spring 2000.[^ref-11]

**Why It Was Cancelled:** Sierra cancelled the project in a September 1999 reorganization that also cancelled Desert Fighters, Pro Pilot Paradise and Orcs: Revenge of the Ancients; more than 100 people working on those titles were laid off.[^ref-27]

**Technical Achievements:** The game used an inertial flight model and featured over 60 combat ships; Sierra cited a figure of 1,000 simultaneous ships for some battles, while IGN's preview reported "engagements between hundreds of ships at once."[^ref-11][^ref-12] IGN wrote that "Sierra may be the first company to really get space flight accurate while keeping it fun."[^ref-12]

**What's Lost Forever:** The filmed sequences were shot over a week on the standing Babylon 5 sets, plus a three-day blue-screen shoot.[^ref-33] A 2014 fan forum post, relaying a second-hand account attributed to series creator J. Michael Straczynski, claims further cast recordings were made; that claim is unverified.[^ref-13]

**What Survives:** A beta build dated September 1999 surfaced in 2014, fifteen years after cancellation. Abandonware France identifies it as the demo shown at E3 1999 and says it is playable but rather temperamental to run.[^ref-11]

### Precinct: Jim Walls Returns

In 2013, [[Jim Walls]]—creator of the Police Quest series—attempted to revive police procedural adventures with Precinct, a spiritual successor to his beloved franchise.[^ref-14]

**What Happened:** The Kickstarter campaign launched July 16, 2013, seeking $500,000 to develop a first-person police adventure set in the fictional corrupt town of Fraser Canyon, California. With $85,756 pledged (17% of goal), it was cancelled by its creators on August 6, 2013.[^ref-14][^ref-34]

**Why It Was Cancelled:** Robert Lindsley, president of Jim Walls Reloaded, said the campaign was cancelled so the team could build a playable pilot version, letting potential backers "experience" the vision rather than judge it from a tech demo video.[^ref-14]

**What Was Planned:** Players would control Officer Maxwell Jones, combining real police procedures with action sequences—shootouts, high-speed car chases, foot pursuits, and hand-to-hand combat.[^ref-34]

### SWAT: Urban Justice

Sierra's planned fourth SWAT game, Urban Justice was meant to succeed [[1999 - SWAT 3 - Close Quarters Battle|SWAT 3]] but was cancelled in favor of what became [[2005 - SWAT 4|SWAT 4]] from Irrational Games.[^ref-16]

**What Happened:** Set in 2006 Los Angeles during the city's 225th anniversary, the game featured three rival gangs—the Loco Riders, Krazy Boyz, and Compton 187—in open warfare. The game slipped out of 2002 and then 2003,[^ref-38] and in April 2004 Vivendi Universal said work on it had been "discontinued" in favor of SWAT 4.[^ref-16][^ref-30]

**Technical Details:** The Takedown 3D engine supported 5,000-polygon character models with 29 differentiated impact points for realistic damage modeling.[^ref-17]

**What Survives:** A 2002 trailer was included on Disc 2 of No One Lives Forever 2. An easter egg in SWAT 4's Victory Imports Auto Center mission shows Urban Justice as an arcade cabinet—a company in-joke acknowledging the cancelled project.[^ref-16]

### Middle-earth Online

Sierra's Middle-earth Online was one of the earliest attempts to bring Tolkien's world into MMO gaming, with a sandbox design closer to Ultima Online than to later themepark MMOs.[^ref-18]

**Distinctive Design Ideas:**
- **Permanent Character Death:** The team wanted characters to die permanently in certain situations[^ref-18]
- **Few Elves:** Higher-ups pushed for "loads more Elves" and more magic, which the team resisted[^ref-18]
- **Restricted Magic:** One fan reviewer at TheOneRing.net noted that even Gandalf "didn't show a full array of fire balls and lightning bolts"[^ref-19]

**What Happened:** Development began at Sierra in 1998, targeting a 2000 release, but faced layoffs and a 1999 move from California to Washington amid what Engadget calls "some serious lack of management." Vivendi Universal took over the license in 2001, and it later passed to Turbine, "which took the IP and did its own thing" with The Lord of the Rings Online.[^ref-18]

### Manhunter 3: London

The Manhunter series—Barry, Dave, and Dee Dee Murry's dark post-apocalyptic adventure games—was planned as a trilogy, but the third game was never made.[^ref-20]

**What Happened:** A poster in [[1989 - Manhunter - San Francisco|Manhunter 2]] advertised "Manhunter 3: London," teasing the planned sequel.[^ref-20] The association between Evryware and Sierra ended in 1989, and "there was never a deal struck between Evryware and Sierra to actually move the game into production."[^ref-28] A 2003 post on the Sierra Gamers forum, written from the point of view of Sierra's management (the author now shows only as "Unknown") and opening "I hope I am remembering this right," says Brøderbund won the Murrys' Ancient Art of War and that "one of my 'rules' was to never be the 'high bidder'"; a reply in the same thread points out that Ancient Art of War (1984) came out before Manhunter, so the account does not fit the published chronology.[^ref-21]

**Revival Attempt:** In 2008, an anonymous forum poster claimed to have emailed one of the Murrys ("Mike") about a Manhunter London game with a dual protagonist structure. The thread's last reply notes that "Nothing has been confirmed," and nothing further appeared.[^ref-22]

### Red Baron 2013 Remake

Damon Slye, creator of the Red Baron flight simulator, attempted to revive the franchise through Kickstarter in 2013.[^ref-15]

**What Happened:** Mad Otter Games launched a Kickstarter on October 22, 2013, seeking $250,000. Mad Otter posted that it would cancel the campaign "for now," but it ran to its November 22 deadline and failed, with $40,493 pledged by 1,118 backers (about 16% of goal).[^ref-24]

**Why It Failed:** Slye candidly assessed the problems: "We didn't build a community before we launched the Kickstarter. We didn't present it very well. The game didn't look right. It looked a little arcade-ey. It didn't look like living history, interactive history."[^ref-15]

## Why They Were Cancelled

### Corporate Instability

Sierra's acquisition by CUC International in 1996, followed by the merger into Cendant and sale to Havas/Vivendi, created constant leadership changes that disrupted ongoing projects.[^ref-1]

### Adventure Game Decline

The late 1990s saw traditional adventure games fall out of commercial favor. Sierra President Mike Ryder declared in 2002 that "the adventure genre as we knew it may no longer be viable."[^ref-4]

### Original Creators Departing

[[Roberta Williams]] left Sierra shortly before the release of King's Quest: Mask of Eternity, bound by a non-competition agreement;[^ref-2] [[Al Lowe]] left after Larry 8 was cancelled during contract talks at the end of 1998;[^ref-29] and [[Scott Murphy]], by his own account, was let go about a month and a half before Chainsaw Monday.[^ref-37] The designers who had built Sierra's franchises were gone.

### Chainsaw Monday

February 22, 1999 devastated Sierra's studios. Ken Williams described it: "This is a sad ending to Sierra's twenty-year operating history in Oakhurst, which at one time, represented over 550 Oakhurst-based employees."[^ref-3]

## What Survived

### Spiritual Successors

Several Sierra alumni turned to Kickstarter in 2012–13:

| Designer | Original Series | Spiritual Successor |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|
| [[Scott Murphy]] & [[Mark Crowe]] | Space Quest | [[2022 - SpaceVenture\|SpaceVenture]] (backers 2022;[^ref-39] full release December 23, 2025)[^ref-35] |
| [[Jim Walls]] | Police Quest | Precinct (cancelled) |
| [[Damon Slye]] | Red Baron | Red Baron Remake (Kickstarter failed) |
| [[Al Lowe]] | Leisure Suit Larry | [[2013 - Leisure Suit Larry - Reloaded\|Leisure Suit Larry: Reloaded]] (2013; a crowdfunded remake of the first Larry, not a sequel)[^ref-36] |

### Fan Projects

The King's Quest community maintained hope through multiple cancelled official projects. [[2010 - The Silver Lining|The Silver Lining]] came closest to being a "King's Quest IX," releasing four of five planned episodes before development stalled—though it was forced to drop the KQ name entirely.[^ref-7][^ref-31]

### Official Revivals

Some cancelled projects eventually led to official revivals in different forms:

- **Middle-earth Online** → The Lord of the Rings Online (2007) (license passed to Turbine, which built its own game)[^ref-18]
- **King's Quest IX** → [[2015 - King's Quest|King's Quest (2015)]] (not considered KQ9)
- **SWAT: Urban Justice** → [[2005 - SWAT 4]] (different developer)

## The Human Cost

Behind every cancelled game were developers who poured years into projects that would never see release.

**Evan Buehler**, sound designer on SWAT: Urban Justice, flew from Sierra's office in Bellevue, Washington, to Idaho to record over 40 firearms with professional studio equipment—audio work that would never be heard in a finished game.[^ref-25]

The **Babylon 5: Into the Fire** team filmed its live-action sequences with series leads Bruce Boxleitner, Jerry Doyle, Mira Furlan and Tracy Scoggins; the game was 65% complete when it was cancelled.[^ref-33][^ref-11]

**Scott Murphy** learned his project was cancelled on Christmas Eve 1997: "That was the word used. And wasn't that a nice Christmas present?"[^ref-26]

## Lessons Learned

Sierra's cancelled games offer cautionary tales about:

1. **Corporate Acquisition:** The CUC/Cendant/Vivendi ownership changes repeatedly disrupted projects in progress[^ref-1]
2. **Chasing Trends:** Scott Murphy said the pressure to make Space Quest VII multiplayer meant "the design had to be something that I didn't want it to be"[^ref-26]
3. **Timing:** Babylon 5: Into the Fire was 65% complete when it was cancelled[^ref-11]
4. **Crowdfunding:** Damon Slye said of the failed Red Baron campaign, "We didn't build a community before we launched the Kickstarter. We didn't present it very well."[^ref-15]
